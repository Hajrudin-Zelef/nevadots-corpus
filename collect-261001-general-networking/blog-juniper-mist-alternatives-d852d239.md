1. Cisco Meraki: Best for Simple Cloud Management
Cisco Meraki is the platform most often weighed head to head against Juniper Mist, and it wins on dashboard simplicity rather than on AI depth. Meraki began at MIT in 2006 and Cisco acquired it in 2012, and it has since become the go-to for teams that want a single clean cloud screen across every site. Where Mist leads with Marvis and Service Level Expectations, Meraki offers lighter cloud AI and basic automation, so it suits buyers who value ease of use over self-driving operations.
That simplicity comes tied to a license you cannot opt out of. A Meraki access point only works while its cloud license is active, and once the license lapses the unit stops forwarding traffic to the internet rather than just losing extra features. Cisco spells this out in its Meraki licensing FAQ, which states every hardware component must carry a cloud license to be managed. The same gripe shows up on Capterra, where reviewers call unlicensed gear dead weight and point to renewal costs that rise year over year.
On PeerSpot, Meraki wireless averages 4.1 out of 5 from 123 reviewers as of September 2026. There is no public price list; hardware moves through quotes and resellers, where street prices land around $1,055 for the MR36 and $1,717 for the MR44 at time of writing, so plan for the license on top of every unit. The MR wireless license splits into Enterprise and Advanced, both sold per access point on multi-year terms, with Advanced layering on AI configuration and deeper security tooling (Cisco’s MR license guide breaks down the two). Meraki is the fit for teams that want one simple multi-site dashboard and treat the ongoing license as the price of that simplicity. HPE 2. Aruba: Best for Large Enterprise Campus Networks
HPE Aruba is the enterprise-campus pick, but in 2026 it comes with a caveat that changes the whole reason to consider it as a Mist alternative: it is now owned by the same parent as Mist. Aruba Networks was founded in 2002 and became part of HPE in 2015, and it anchors HPE’s campus networking line. If you are eyeing Aruba as your way out of the Mist stack, you would be moving to a sibling brand under the same owner.

For a buyer, the practical takeaway is that Mist and Aruba are no longer two independent choices. Choosing one to escape the other means that at renewal you are negotiating with one company, not two.

HPE Aruba wireless holds a 4.2 out of 5 rating across 151 reviews on PeerSpot as of September 2026, one of the deepest review bases on this list. Aruba Central uses a tiered per-device subscription, and hardware is quote-based rather than list-priced. Aruba fits large campuses and multi-building enterprises that want a deep, integrated stack and can weigh the consolidation question with clear eyes. 3. Extreme Networks: Best for Schools and Large Venues
Extreme Networks is a strong fit for schools, campuses, and large venues that want one cloud console across wired and wireless. Extreme was founded in 1996 and grew by absorbing well-known lines, including the Aerohive cloud-management platform, which is how it ended up with ExtremeCloud IQ spanning switches and access points. Its AI-operations layer, ExtremeCloud IQ CoPilot, provides explainable machine-learning insights, making it one of the genuine Marvis-style options on this list.
The limitation is where the features live. The free “Connect” tier of ExtremeCloud IQ is capped at 10 devices, and accounts that exceed the cap are moved to the paid Pilot tier, according to Extreme’s own support documentation. Full management, analytics, and support require that Pilot subscription. ExtremeCloud IQ holds a 4.4 out of 5 rating across 78 reviews on PeerSpot as of September 2026, with reviewers naming pricing as a concern.
The AP4000 Wi-Fi 6E access point runs around $776 at resellers at time of writing. The ExtremeCloud IQ Pilot subscription is priced per device per year and is generally quote-based, though buyers have reported figures near $150 per device for Pilot. Extreme fits education and large-venue teams that want deep monitoring across a mixed wired and wireless estate from one console. 4. RUCKUS: Best for High-Density and Difficult RF Environments
RUCKUS earns its place when the radio environment is the hard part of the job, in stadiums, warehouses, and dense multi-tenant buildings where ordinary access points struggle, because its BeamFlex antennas steer signal around interference. Founded in 2004 on the strength of that radio engineering, RUCKUS also carries its own machine-learning assurance layer, RUCKUS AI (previously RUCKUS Analytics), so a Mist buyer who relies on AIOps is not giving it up by switching here. The complication in 2026 is who owns the company. RUCKUS passed from CommScope to a spun-off firm called Vistance Networks in January 2026, and Belden has since agreed to buy RUCKUS from Vistance for about $1.85 billion (Network World reported the deal), a chain of ownership laid out on Wikipedia. Ask your vendor directly about roadmap and support continuity before you sign.
Those changes come alongside two complaints that recur on PeerSpot: price, with one reviewer putting RUCKUS at three to four times the cost of rivals, and support that some feel has slipped since the earlier acquisitions. Even so, RUCKUS wireless carries a 4.3 out of 5 score from 105 PeerSpot reviewers as of September 2026, a sign the radio engineering still delivers despite the corporate churn.
The R350 indoor Wi-Fi 6 access point sells for about $540 at major resellers at time of writing, with no published vendor list price. You manage it one of two ways: the per-device RUCKUS One cloud subscription, which resellers price from roughly $170 for one year to $510 for five on Essentials and $315 to $945 on Professional at time of writing, or an on-premises SmartZone controller for teams that want management kept in-house. That on-prem path is why RUCKUS appeals to buyers whose buildings defeat ordinary access points but who would rather not lean on a cloud subscription. 5. Ubiquiti UniFi: Best for Lean IT Teams That Self-Manage
Ubiquiti UniFi is the clean escape from Mist’s subscription model, pairing public list pricing with no recurring license. Founded in 2005, Ubiquiti runs its UniFi gear on a controller you host yourself at no charge, on your own box or a Ubiquiti Cloud Gateway, and it stretches from a one-office setup to a multi-building campus. There is no Marvis-class assistant here, which is the point: UniFi is for teams that would rather run the network themselves than pay for one that runs itself.
What you give up is a support desk, not money. Ubiquiti ships no enterprise phone line or SLA, and its free support runs through chat and email only; a named-contact phone plan exists but is a paid add-on tied to Cloud Gateway deployments (per Ubiquiti’s support page). Reviewers on PeerSpot make the same point, noting that when a problem is a true show-stopper there is nobody to call for a fast fix. Ubiquiti UniFi rates 4.2 out of 5 from 13 PeerSpot reviews as of September 2026.
Pricing is public and flat: the U7 Pro ceiling access point lists at $189, and the enterprise E7, a 10-stream Wi-Fi 7 unit with a 10 GbE uplink, at $499 on the Ubiquiti store as of September 2026 (see the U7 Pro and E7 pages). Nothing recurs after purchase, so the sticker price is the whole cost, the sharpest break from Mist’s mandatory per-access-point Assurance subscription you will find on this list. UniFi suits lean IT teams that can self-manage and can live with a rare outage and no support line. Our Ubiquiti installation team deploys and configures UniFi at commercial scale.
6. TP-Link Omada: Best for Mid-Market Sites Stepping Up From Consumer Gear
TP-Link Omada is the second license-free option, aimed at mid-market sites moving up from consumer routers without paying for a full enterprise platform. Omada is the business networking line of TP-Link, a networking hardware maker founded in 1996, and it runs from a controller that lives on a hardware appliance or on free software you host yourself. Like UniFi, it has no AI-operations assistant, so it competes on price and simplicity rather than self-driving management.
Its honest limitation is ceiling, not cost. Omada lacks the enterprise feature depth, AI-operations, and support contracts of true enterprise vendors, and its controllers manage smaller device counts, capping at 100 devices on the OC200, 500 on the OC300, and 1,500 on the self-hosted software, as documented in an independent Omada overview that describes it as living in the middle ground. Omada access points come in at 3.9 out of 5 from 15 PeerSpot reviews as of September 2026, the lowest score on this list. One point needs care rather than hype: US government scrutiny of TP-Link has targeted consumer and small-office home routers as a proposal, not an enacted ban, and no source names the Omada business line as a target, so treat it as a consumer-gear question, not an enterprise one.
Pricing stays low and license-free. The EAP670 Wi-Fi 6 ceiling access point sits around $166 at time of writing, the optional OC200 and OC300 hardware controllers run roughly $100 and $160, and no per-access-point subscription applies (confirmed on Omada’s site). Omada is the fit for a growing mid-market site that wants managed WiFi without a subscription and has no need for Marvis-class analytics.
7. Fortinet FortiAP: Best for Security-First Fortinet Networks
Fortinet FortiAP makes sense mainly for shops already running Fortinet, since the access points are driven from the FortiGate firewall instead of a dedicated wireless controller. The company was founded in 2000, and its Security Fabric was built to run wireless, switching, and firewalling from one security-first platform, close to the opposite of Mist’s WiFi-led, AI-first cloud.
The same integration is the constraint. Because the full controller lives inside FortiGate, you get the most out of FortiAP only if you own a FortiGate, per Fortinet’s WLAN architecture guide. Running the access points standalone means FortiLAN Cloud, whose free tier stops at 30 unlicensed access points and three switches with basic management, above which each device needs a license (per Fortinet’s FortiLAN Cloud licensing docs). Fortinet FortiAP scores 4.3 out of 5 from 17 PeerSpot reviews as of September 2026.
Reseller pricing at time of writing puts the FAP-431F-A Wi-Fi 6 access point near $527 and the Wi-Fi 7 FAP-241K-A near $647, with an entry FortiGate to manage them starting around $362. FortiAP is for security-first networks already committed to Fortinet that want wireless folded into the same fabric, not for teams shopping a best-of-breed standalone WLAN with a Marvis-style assistant.
8. Cambium Networks: Best for Distributed Sites With Outdoor Coverage
Cambium Networks suits operators whose sites sprawl outdoors, across campuses, yards, and buildings that indoor-first vendors cover poorly, because outdoor and fixed-wireless radio is where the company began. It spun out of Motorola’s broadband business in 2011 and today runs its enterprise WiFi through the cnMaestro platform. For a Mist buyer the trade is plain: Cambium gives you analytics rather than a Marvis-style assistant, so it is a coverage-and-management choice, not an AI-operations one.
Its limitation follows the familiar cloud-managed shape: the real management tier is paid. cnMaestro Essentials is free but caps out at 10 management users, and the enterprise capabilities live in the paid cnMaestro X subscription (per Cambium’s cnMaestro Essentials page). One more thing a buyer notices fast: Cambium lists no hardware prices on its own site, so every quote runs through a reseller. On PeerSpot, Cambium wireless sits at 4.1 out of 5 from 20 reviews as of September 2026.
Expect to pay around $1,350 for the XV3-8 tri-radio Wi-Fi 6 access point at resellers at time of writing, with cnMaestro X billed per device on one, three, or five-year terms. Cambium is the pick for multi-site operators, particularly those with outdoor or campus-wide footprints, that want one platform covering both indoor and outdoor wireless.

Juniper Mist is now a Hewlett Packard Enterprise product, two acquisitions removed from where it started. Why Enterprise Teams Look Beyond Juniper Mist
Enterprise teams leave or reconsider Mist for four repeated reasons: stacked subscriptions, cloud-only dependency, cost, and post-acquisition uncertainty. None of these mean the technology is weak. They are about the commercial model and the roadmap around it.
The subscription structure is the concrete, current driver. Mist’s base Wireless Assurance service is a mandatory per-access-point subscription, and Marvis AI is an additional add-on on top of it. Juniper’s subscription documentation states the number of subscriptions must match the number of access points in a site, and its Marvis documentation confirms you also need Marvis subscriptions for each device. That stacking is why buyers describe the licensing as heavy, and it is vendor-confirmed rather than just sentiment.
Cloud-only dependency is the second reason. Mist has no on-premises management option, so everything runs through the Mist cloud, and teams that want a local or controller fallback flag that as a constraint. Cost is the third, and it shows up as a long-running complaint pattern. Some IT teams on community forums have called Mist expensive relative to peers, though the loudest of those threads date back several years, and reviewers on analyst platforms echo that the licensing could be simpler. The fourth reason is the newest: after HPE closed the Juniper deal, some customers are concerned that overlapping product lines could be consolidated over time, which is a reasonable question to raise during a renewal.

How to Migrate Off Juniper Mist
Leaving Mist is a physical project, not a dashboard swap, so budget for survey, cabling, and cutover work rather than a quick login change. Picking the replacement is the easy part. Mounting, powering, and tuning the new access points without downtime is what actually decides whether the project lands.
A clean migration follows five steps:
- Start with a real site survey. Walk the actual RF environment and measure it, rather than trusting a vendor demo or the design you inherited. An over-provisioned rollout, where you buy more access points than the space needs, almost always traces back to a survey nobody ran.
- Check the cabling and PoE headroom before you order. Newer access points can pull more power and expect newer cabling, so confirm the switches and structured cabling will carry the replacement hardware first.
- Redesign access-point placement for the new radios. Every vendor’s radios propagate differently, so a layout tuned for Mist rarely transfers cleanly to the platform that replaces it.
- Move over zone by zone, not all at once. Where the building allows, keep the old and new networks live in parallel and migrate one area at a time, so nobody loses connectivity in a single hard cutover.
- Decommission cleanly and cancel the old subscriptions. Pull the retired gear and confirm your Mist Wireless Assurance and Marvis subscriptions are switched off, so you are not paying for a platform you have already left.

Frequently Asked Questions
Did Juniper acquire Mist?
Yes. Juniper Networks acquired Mist Systems in 2019, bringing the Mist AI platform and the Marvis virtual assistant into Juniper’s portfolio. In 2025, Juniper itself was acquired by Hewlett Packard Enterprise, so Mist is now an HPE product two acquisitions removed from the original startup.
Is Juniper Mist owned by HPE now?
Yes. HPE closed its acquisition of Juniper Networks on July 2, 2025, in a deal worth roughly $14 billion. Juniper Mist now sits inside HPE Networking alongside HPE Aruba Networking, so the two platforms share a parent company.
What is Juniper Mist used for?
Juniper Mist is used for AI-driven enterprise wireless and wired networking, managed from the Mist cloud. It combines WiFi access points, the Marvis virtual assistant for troubleshooting, Service Level Expectations for measuring network health, and location services, aimed at large organizations that want AI to help operate the network.
Is Juniper Mist free?
No. Juniper Mist requires paid subscriptions for production use. The base Wireless Assurance service is a mandatory per-access-point subscription, and Marvis AI is a further per-device add-on on top of it. Evaluation units and trials exist, but running Mist in production means paying for the subscriptions.
How much does Juniper Mist cost?
Juniper Mist pricing is quote-based and has three parts: the access-point hardware, a mandatory Wireless Assurance subscription for each access point, and an optional Marvis AI subscription for each device. Because the subscriptions stack per device and pricing is negotiated through resellers, the real cost depends on your access-point count and the term length you choose.
Which is better, Juniper Mist or Cisco Meraki?
It depends on whether you want AI operations or dashboard simplicity. Juniper Mist leads on AI-driven operations with Marvis and Service Level Expectations, which suits large enterprises that want the network to self-diagnose. Cisco Meraki leads on a simpler cloud dashboard that is easier to run across many sites. Both use mandatory per-device licensing, so the decision usually comes down to whether the AI capability justifies its premium.
Who are Juniper Mist’s main competitors?
Juniper Mist’s main competitors in enterprise WiFi are Cisco Meraki, HPE Aruba, Extreme Networks, and RUCKUS, with Ubiquiti UniFi, TP-Link Omada, Fortinet FortiAP, and Cambium Networks serving narrower cases. HPE Aruba is now owned by the same parent as Mist, so it is a competitor in name more than in corporate independence.
