*Nova Scotia’s $10 billion Point Tupper data centre is expected to support 215 permanent jobs, or about $46.5 million of investment for each one. A Nova Scotia business owner gets more from three smaller numbers: $19 million per megawatt, 215 permanent jobs and a power tariff that switches the campus off first.*

The Nova Scotia data centre planned for Point Tupper arrived with one headline number, $10 billion, the largest private investment in the province’s history. Budget holders should look at a different figure. Divide that money by the 215 permanent jobs it is expected to support and you get about $46.5 million per position, which tells you this deal pays Nova Scotia through power contracts, rent and tax, and very little through payroll.

We still think the project is worth backing, judged on the clause that touches every commercial power bill in the province: the campus buys grid electricity on an interruptible basis, so homes and businesses keep priority when the system is tight. If that holds up before the regulator, the Cape Breton build is a good deal for Nova Scotia ratepayers. If it softens, the jobs math alone does not carry it.

## What did EverWind and Anthropic announce for Point Tupper?

On October 16, 2026, EverWind announced a data centre campus at its Point Tupper industrial site near Port Hawkesbury, with a 95 MW first phase targeted for late 2027 and a 325 MW expansion targeted for 2028. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, signed a long-term lease for the first phase. EverWind put the campus at more than $8 billion.

Another $2 billion goes to four onshore wind projects: Bear Lake, Windy Ridge, Kmtnuk and Upper Afton. EverWind’s announcement also says it is negotiating a share of campus ownership with a group of First Nations led by Membertou Development Corporation. Those talks are not finished, so nobody outside the room knows the size of that stake yet.

## How much does each megawatt and each job cost?

Using EverWind’s own figures, the Nova Scotia data centre costs about $19 million per megawatt at its planned 420 MW, and the whole $10 billion program supports about $46.5 million of investment per permanent job. Those two ratios explain where the value lands. The money buys machines and electricity, so the local return arrives as tax, rent and supplier work.

In plain terms: $8 billion divided by 420 MW is $19.0 million per megawatt. $10 billion divided by 215 jobs is $46.5 million each. $1.5 billion of projected provincial corporate tax over 20 years averages $75 million a year. (Davis Pier projections published by the developer, so treat them as the top of the range.)

Now move the main assumption. The expansion is conditional, and EverWind did not split its spending by phase. Purely as an illustration, if spending scaled evenly with megawatts, the 95 MW phase would be about $1.8 billion of the campus, and if the benefits scaled the same way, every per-year figure above would shrink to roughly 23% of the full-build number. A Strait Region supplier planning around full-build volumes should plan for the smaller case first.

## What protects ratepayers if the Nova Scotia data centre needs more power?

EverWind says it will pay all connection, network upgrade and other electricity-system costs tied to the project, and that grid power will be bought under an interruptible tariff that keeps homes and businesses first. The Nova Scotia Energy Board oversees those arrangements, which makes its review the place where that promise becomes enforceable or does not.

The project site adds the operating detail: battery storage plus diesel backup generation keep the campus running when it is asked to cut its grid draw. That is the right design for a province where a 95 MW customer is large. It also means the campus pays twice for reliability, once in batteries and once in generators, and the developer is betting a 20-year lease covers both.

Ottawa’s Responsible Data Centre Development Principles say data centres must not shift electricity costs to Canadians, and EverWind signed on the day of the announcement. As we noted when the federal data centre principles came out, they carry no penalty. In Nova Scotia, the tariff and the Energy Board do the enforcing. Calgary is learning the reverse lesson, with a council pause that hangs on a definition rather than a tariff.

## Does a Cape Breton data centre keep Canadian AI data in Canada?

Only if the customer’s contract says so. More capacity in Nova Scotia makes Canadian hosting possible, but where a given customer’s prompts and files are processed depends on that customer’s contract with the AI provider. The announcements describe a lease for space and power, not the residency terms a Halifax law firm or a Sydney clinic would sign.

EverWind estimates Nova Scotia uses about 110 MW of data centre capacity while hosting about 25 MW, and says the first phase lifts hosted capacity to about 120 MW. That is a useful supply figure. It does not tell a buyer which region their account runs in. We made the same point about Ottawa’s Cohere stake: residency is a contract line, so get it in writing.

## Is there a stronger case for the deal than the jobs figure?

Yes, and it rests on tax and grid economics. EverWind projects about $137 million of provincial GDP a year at full operation, about $1.5 billion of provincial corporate tax over 20 years and more than $400 million in municipal tax over the projects’ lives. For a host municipality like Richmond County, that last figure matters more than any job count. Alberta hosts start from a thinner position, because the Alberta data centre levy can fall to 0% for self-powered campuses and servers are not assessable for property tax.

There is also an energy argument. A large customer that agrees to step back when the grid is tight, and helps pay for new wind, can make better use of capacity that already exists. The developer says so on its project site. That is plausible, and the fair test is the Energy Board record, not the press release. Our position holds unless that review finds costs leaking to other customer classes.

## What should Atlantic Canadian businesses do about Point Tupper?

Treat the Nova Scotia data centre as a construction-window opportunity with a long, thin tail. About 2,000 construction positions are spread across a staged build, while permanent operating jobs number about 215. Suppliers, landlords and trades in the Strait Region should price for the build years and treat permanent demand as a bonus.

1. Strait Region contractors: EverWind says dozens of Nova Scotia contractors and suppliers are already engaged, and the first phase targets late 2027. Ask how it will publish its local procurement figures, which it says it supports.
2. Commercial power customers anywhere in Nova Scotia: follow the Energy Board filings on cost allocation for the project. That is where the “no higher rates” promise gets tested.
3. Firms using Claude or any AI service for client data: ask your provider in writing which country processes your data today, and whether that changes when Canadian capacity opens.
4. Municipal finance staff in Richmond County: model the tax line on the 95 MW phase alone until the expansion clears its approvals.

Our wider guide to AI data centres in Canada tracks the other provincial projects, including the Moose Jaw build that stalled on power.

The inputs for the Nova Scotia data centre are all public: $8 billion, 420 MW, 215 jobs, $1.5 billion over 20 years. Swap in the 95 MW case or a slower expansion date and rerun them. One number is missing from that list, and it decides the deal: the cost allocation the Nova Scotia Energy Board approves for the interruptible tariff.

## FAQ

### How big is the Nova Scotia data centre at Point Tupper?

EverWind plans a 95 MW first phase, targeted for late 2027, and a 325 MW expansion targeted for 2028, for 420 MW at full build. The expansion depends on system studies, transmission work and approvals.

### Who is leasing the Point Tupper data centre?

Anthropic, the developer of Claude, signed a long-term lease for the first phase. EverWind will build and own the campus.

### Will the Nova Scotia data centre raise power rates?

EverWind says it will fund all electricity-system costs tied to the project and buy grid power under an interruptible tariff. The Nova Scotia Energy Board oversees those arrangements.

### How many permanent jobs will the project create?

EverWind expects about 215 permanent operating positions across the campus and its wind projects, plus about 2,000 construction positions during the staged build.
