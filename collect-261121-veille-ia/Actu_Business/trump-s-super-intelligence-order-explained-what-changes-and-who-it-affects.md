*Originally published on agent5.news.*

On September 29, 2026, the White House did something that has rarely happened in technology policy: it ordered the government to stop using a widely understood technical term and swap it for a new one. President Trump signed Executive Order 14434, titled "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence," directing every federal department and agency to replace "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" with "Super Intelligence" and "SI" across official communications. The same afternoon, six of the largest AI companies in the world signed a voluntary pledge at the White House. A week later, a new federal task force was announced to oversee the whole effort.

Whether you think the move is a savvy rebranding of a winning political issue or a distraction from harder regulatory questions, the order and its companion pieces are now part of the official policy landscape. Here is what each piece actually says, what it covers, and what it does not.

The push for a terminology change did not emerge from a regulatory review or a technical advisory committee. Trump had publicly argued that the word "artificial" undersells what modern AI systems can do.

The White House framed the rename in promotional terms, describing the order as recognizing the advancing technological frontier and the promise it offers the American people. Trump also tied it to a geopolitical argument, suggesting that winning the superintelligence race would determine the broader competition for global technological leadership.

The executive order states that the term "Super Intelligence" more appropriately captures the promise, potential, and rapidly advancing capabilities of these technologies.

The order, formally numbered EO 14434 and titled "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence," includes several directives.

Among them, all executive departments and agencies are instructed to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in place of "Artificial Intelligence" and "AI" in official executive branch correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents, and other non-statutory materials, to the maximum extent permitted by law.

The order also explicitly states that agencies "will not acknowledge" the terms "Artificial Intelligence" or "AI" in applicable settings.

The order further directs the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology (APST) to propose a formal federal definition of "Super Intelligence" and to identify any additional action needed to implement that definition. That proposal must assess whether the new definition should modify, expand, or supersede the existing statutory definition of AI.

Critically, the order is not retroactive. Existing regulations, previously issued presidential actions, contracts, grants, and historical documents are explicitly exempt. The order changes the label used going forward, not the substance of anything already on the books.

Here is where the order creates its most significant conceptual tension. In AI research, "superintelligence" already has a specific meaning: it refers to a hypothetical category of AI that would exceed human intellectual capabilities across virtually every domain, a threshold that no existing system has crossed. Using the same word for today's chatbots, image generators, and recommendation engines blurs that distinction considerably.

The order sidesteps this problem in a pragmatic way. For its purposes, "Super Intelligence" and "SI" carry the same meaning as "artificial intelligence" as already defined under existing federal law (15 U.S.C.). In other words, the administration is changing the words but borrowing the existing legal definition, at least for now. The 60-day clock to propose new legislative language is intended to eventually close that gap.

Critics have pointed out that calling existing systems "superintelligence" blurs the distinction between a research prospect and a demonstrated capability, and that a presidential directive can change the government's vocabulary without establishing that any technology has actually crossed that threshold.

Signed on the same day as the executive order, the White House Accord on Super Intelligence is a separate voluntary document. It was signed by the CEOs or presidents of six companies: Google, Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, xAI, and Nvidia. The accord commits those companies to four layers of internal controls for the safe development of their most advanced systems, including monitoring, safety and security teams, third-party audits, and board-level oversight of those practices.

Trump described the commitments as "morally binding," but the accord is voluntary and is not the same as federal regulation or a statute. It sets no legal penalties, contains no enforcement mechanism, names no specific auditor, imposes no publication requirement on audit results, and offers no government role in verification. The America First Policy Institute has described the accord as binding developers to essential internal practices, while critics in the Senate argued the accord has "no teeth" and will not hold companies legally accountable.

The distinction between a voluntary accord and binding regulation matters because many of the tech industry's previous voluntary safety pledges at the White House have faced questions about follow-through.

On October 4, 2026, Trump announced the creation of the "Super Intelligence Force," a White House task force that formalizes the government-side coordination piece. The force is chaired by Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton, effectively giving Clayton a dual role as the administration's AI czar. Vice chairs include Federal Trade Commission Chair Andrew Ferguson, the Defense Department's Undersecretary of War for Research and Engineering Emil Michael, and Office of Personnel Management Director Scott Kupor.

The task force reports directly to Trump. Its stated mission is to coordinate the federal government's engagement with consumers, public interest groups, religious organizations, critical infrastructure providers, and what the order calls "Super Intelligence Companies."

Clayton has framed the work as a national security matter, telling reporters that the technology represents a national security issue and that "the risk of not being first is high." He has also pushed back against proposals from some AI developers to slow down model releases for additional safety evaluation, pointing out that other countries are not pausing their own development programs. The task force has 120 days from its formation to deliver a report on AI's risks and opportunities and to recommend what role the federal government should play in overseeing the technology.

For all the noise around the terminology shift, there are clear limits on what the order can do by itself.

Private companies, universities, and researchers face no requirement to change their language. The order covers only the federal executive branch, and even within the executive branch it applies only to new, non-statutory materials. Existing statutes that use the term "artificial intelligence" remain unchanged. Federal contracts, grants, and regulations already in effect keep their original wording unless explicitly amended.

The order also does not preempt state AI laws. At the moment there are already dozens of state-level AI regulations in various stages of passage and implementation, and EO 14434 says nothing about how those interact with the new federal vocabulary. That question remains open and may become more complicated if Congress eventually adopts new legislation using the "Super Intelligence" framing.

For businesses with federal contracts or grant agreements that reference "artificial intelligence," legal analysts have noted that the near-term practical effects are likely to be procedural and administrative rather than substantive. The bigger risk they identify is a potential future divergence between federal terminology and the international standards, state laws, and corporate compliance frameworks that still use "AI."

This order does not exist in isolation. When Trump took office in January 2025, one of his first acts was to revoke the Biden administration's 2023 executive order on AI safety, which had directed developers of large models to share safety test results with the government. Trump replaced it with EO 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," which shifted the emphasis from guardrails to speed and competitiveness.

In December 2025, Trump signed a separate executive order aimed at establishing a uniform national framework for AI regulation and limiting the patchwork of conflicting state laws. The Super Intelligence rebranding order is the latest chapter in that same deregulatory, competitiveness-first posture, adding a rhetorical layer on top of the policy architecture already in place.

The White House also pointed to America's AI Action Plan, released in July 2025, which identified federal actions designed to accelerate AI innovation, build infrastructure, and extend American technological leadership. The "Super Intelligence" framing is intended to reinforce that agenda.

The answer depends on who is asking.

For federal agencies, the change is immediate and procedural. New memos, websites, press releases, and policy documents are expected to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI." Agencies have until late November 2026 to submit proposed legislative language defining the term for statutory purposes, which is a concrete internal deadline with real downstream consequences.

For the six companies that signed the accord, the immediate obligation is to implement the four layers of internal controls they pledged. Because the accord is voluntary, there is no government body currently mandated to check whether they do so. Third-party evaluators are mentioned in the accord's text, but no specific auditing body or standard is named.

For the broader technology industry, researchers, and media, the order carries no legal requirement. But the administration's language has already escalated, with Trump posting on Truth Social that the White House considers anyone using the term "Artificial Intelligence" instead of "Super Intelligence" to be an enemy, a statement that, while not legally enforceable, signals the administration's appetite to use the naming convention as a political marker.

For critics of the administration's AI policy, the order has become a focal point for a deeper argument: that rebranding the technology does nothing to address the underlying safety, labor, or civil rights questions that advocates have been raising, and that voluntary accords without enforcement mechanisms are insufficient responses to the risks that AI systems already pose.

Several concrete milestones will determine whether this rebranding effort has lasting policy substance or remains largely rhetorical.

The most important near-term date is the approximately November 28, 2026 deadline for the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology to submit proposed legislative language formally defining "Super Intelligence" under federal law. That proposal will indicate whether the administration intends to expand, modify, or simply relabel the existing statutory definition of artificial intelligence, and it will determine whether the federal government eventually operates with a genuinely new legal framework or simply a new vocabulary for the same one.

The Super Intelligence Force's 120-day report on risks and opportunities is also a significant deliverable. If it recommends new legislation or regulatory frameworks, that would represent a meaningful escalation of the executive branch's engagement with AI governance. If it does not, critics will argue that the entire effort was primarily symbolic.

Congress has its own role to play. Any statutory change to replace "artificial intelligence" with "super intelligence" in federal law requires legislation, which means the Senate and House will eventually have to weigh in. At the same time, some lawmakers have been preparing separate legislation using the term "superintelligence" in its original technical sense, referring to systems that exceed human-level capabilities, which could create a collision between the political and scientific uses of the same word.

Finally, watch whether the six companies that signed the accord report publicly on their internal audits and safety reviews. The accord commits them to third-party evaluations but does not require public disclosure of results. Whether they publish those findings voluntarily will be an early test of how "morally binding" the pledge turns out to be in practice.
