Citi has updated its Online User Agreement to make customers responsible for activity conducted through third-party AI agents they authorize, according to TechCrunch’s November 18, 2026 report. The reported change goes beyond an assistant paying the wrong bill. It also addresses losses when credentials entrusted to an AI tool are subsequently leaked or compromised.

JPMorgan, Bank of America, PNC and Citizens have issued similar terms or warnings, TechCrunch reports. The practical issue is immediate for anyone considering connecting a financial assistant to a bank account: granting access may carry consequences that outlast the original instruction.

Citi also distinguishes outside agents from tools it provides itself. That makes the provider, access method and permissions important parts of the decision, alongside whatever the AI promises to do.

## What Citi’s Updated Terms Cover

Citi’s spokesperson told TechCrunch that the agreement reinforces that:

“customers who choose to grant account access to third-party AI agents are responsible for activities conducted through those agents.”


Citi’s public Online User Agreement governs electronic services accessed through Citi Online and Citi Mobile. It also says the underlying accounts and services are governed by separate agreements. That layering matters: an AI-related provision in the online agreement may sit alongside account-specific rules and transfer terms.

A published breakdown by Unite.AI describes the updated agreement’s Section H as applying broadly to third-party software taking autonomous, semi-autonomous or non-autonomous actions for a customer. On that account, the provision is not limited to a fully independent agent that manages someone’s finances without supervision.

The detailed Section H and funds-transfer provisions discussed here come from that secondary breakdown; TechCrunch separately reports the central liability shift and carries Citi’s response.

According to Unite.AI, supplying credentials or otherwise permitting an agent to access an account causes Citi to treat the agent’s access and actions as customer-authorized. The summary says customers must monitor those actions for accuracy, completeness and compliance with the authority they intended to grant.

The important distinction is between **what you asked the agent to do and what access you gave it**. Citi’s reported terms give account access consequences that a narrow prompt may not undo.

## A Prompt Is Not a Bank-Enforced Permission

An instruction such as “review my bills, but don’t pay anything” describes the customer’s intent. It does not necessarily create a technical restriction preventing payment.

Consider a hypothetical assistant given banking credentials to review upcoming expenses. If it mistakenly initiates a payment, the customer may see an obvious violation of the instruction. Under the terms described by Unite.AI, Citi can still treat the agent’s action as authorized through the access the customer granted.

The same analysis describes a November 2026 update to Citi’s separate Online Funds Transfers Agreement. Its Section 4 reportedly makes customers responsible for instructions supplied by agents acting on their behalf and treats agent-initiated transfers as directly authorized, even when they exceed or conflict with the customer’s prompts. Unite.AI’s breakdown also says Citi has no obligation to investigate an agent action’s authority or validity under these provisions.

My view is that this is the most consequential detail for consumers: a chat instruction is a weak substitute for a permission enforced by the bank or account connection. An assistant promising to ask before paying is different from a connection that technically cannot submit payments.

These provisions describe a bank’s contractual position, not the inevitable outcome of every dispute. Responsibility can depend on the applicable agreement, the facts and the law; the reported language should not be read as proof that every reimbursement claim will fail.

## Credential Leaks Extend the Risk Beyond Agent Errors

TechCrunch reports that Citi’s update also places responsibility on customers when credentials supplied to an AI tool leak or are compromised and someone else uses them.

Unite.AI’s more detailed account says the authorization language extends to credentials subsequently shared, leaked or otherwise distributed to other people or agents. That creates a second risk path:

- **Execution risk:** The assistant misunderstands instructions, takes an unintended action or exceeds the authority the customer meant to give it.
- **Credential-compromise risk:** Someone else obtains the credentials entrusted to the assistant and uses them to access the account.

The second category matters even if the assistant’s financial reasoning is excellent. Better answers, more reliable planning and clearer prompts cannot by themselves prevent credential theft.

I’d evaluate an agent’s access design before its financial advice. A tool may offer useful analysis while still requiring an access arrangement whose potential downside outweighs that convenience.
