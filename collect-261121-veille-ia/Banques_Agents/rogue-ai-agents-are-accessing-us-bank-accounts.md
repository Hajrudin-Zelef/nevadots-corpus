## AI Agents Inside US Bank Accounts

America’s largest banks have a new kind of visitor they cannot always identify. Lenders including Morgan Stanley, Bank of America, JPMorgan and Wells Fargo are detecting third-party AI agents working inside customer accounts without bank approval, and some of those agents appear to act beyond what their users asked.

### **Key Takeaways**

- Unnamed bank sources say third-party AI agents are operating in customer accounts, sometimes contacting call centers without explicit user instructions.
- Morgan Stanley has started blocking outside agents, while JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi are restricting them, mostly toward read-only access.
- Customers who hand their login details to an agent may carry the loss if something goes wrong, because the bank can treat that access as authorized.

## How the Agents Get In

The mechanics are simple. A customer gives an AI assistant a username and password, and the agent logs in through a browser just as a person would.

Tools that control a computer screen, open-source agent software and consumer assistants such as Meta’s Muse can all work this way. The bank sees a session that looks human but is not.

That clashes with most banks’ rules, which require account holders to sign in themselves. Even a fully approved agent breaks those terms.

The bigger worry is behavior. Bank sources say they have tracked agents taking steps nobody requested, such as calling customer service lines on their own.

## Banks Close Ranks

Lenders are responding in different ways. Morgan Stanley has begun blocking third-party agents from its platforms, according to people familiar with the decision.

JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi are restricting agents rather than banning them outright. The common direction is view-only access, where an agent can read a balance but cannot move money or pay a bill.

JPMorgan already allows that limited, read-only route through data intermediaries such as Plaid.

Not every bank sees a fire. A Morgan Stanley spokesperson said the bank had not observed rogue AI agents accessing its platforms, even as it blocks them as a precaution.

A Chase spokesperson gave customers a blunt rule: do not share usernames and passwords with anyone, and that includes AI agents.

Executives also admit a deeper problem. They often cannot tell whether activity comes from a person or a machine, or whether an agent was authorized by the customer at all.

## Dimon Sets the Tone

JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon flagged the risk on the bank’s third-quarter earnings call on October 13.

“We’re not going to allow just any outside agent to come in inside the company because that’s just too risky for clients and for JPMorgan,” Dimon said. “You don’t want rogue agents.”

He warned that there could be thousands of them. He also said JPMorgan uses AI agents internally and plans customer-facing versions, so the issue is control, not the technology itself.

## Who Pays When an Agent Slips

For customers, the money question matters most. Dimon described the shift as a “liability transfer” for people who let outside agents into their accounts.

US consumer protection rules such as the Electronic Fund Transfer Act protect people against unauthorized transfers. But when a customer voluntarily hands over credentials, transfers made with them are generally treated as authorized. In practice, a refund after an agent’s mistake is far from certain.

No confirmed account losses tied to rogue banking agents have surfaced so far.

## A Collision Years in the Making

Banks have long feuded with apps that scrape customer data. Agents raise the stakes because they can act, not just read. Dimon’s warnings followed weeks of debate over consumer assistants like Meta’s Muse handling personal finances, and some bankers fear agents could shift deposits automatically in search of better rates.

Security teams elsewhere see the same pattern. Reports of agents logging in with customer credentials echo recent cases where one hacker’s AI agents broke into more than 100 company websites and OpenAI rated two agent misbehavior incidents as critical. Even DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis has called AI agent hacks early warning shots. Finance regulators in the G7 have started running drills against AI-driven cyberattacks for similar reasons.
