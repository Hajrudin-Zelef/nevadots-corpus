The Bluetooth Special Interest Group has adopted a new High Data Throughput feature that raises the maximum supported Bluetooth Low Energy bit rate to 7.5 megabits per second, nearly four times the rate of the existing LE 2M physical layer. Announced on November 4, Bluetooth HDT aims to move larger amounts of data more efficiently while improving capacity and reliability. The first flagship application is expected to be standardized premium wireless audio, with compatible products anticipated in 2027.

Bluetooth has become a standard connection method for headphones, keyboards, wearables, sensors and smart-home equipment, but its capabilities have expanded gradually through different versions and optional features. The new High Data Throughput feature addresses a limitation that becomes more visible as devices exchange richer media and larger data sets. Bluetooth SIG says HDT introduces a new physical layer, or PHY, with modulation schemes designed to carry more information efficiently over a Bluetooth Low Energy link.

The headline figure is a maximum supported bit rate of 7.5 Mbps. Bluetooth SIG compares this with the 2 Mbps maximum of the Bluetooth LE 2M PHY, describing the new rate as nearly four times higher. The number is a specification ceiling, not a promise that every device will sustain 7.5 Mbps in normal use. Actual performance depends on the radio environment, implementation, distance, interference and the capabilities of the devices communicating with one another.

## A new physical layer raises the Bluetooth LE ceiling

The SIG says the change aims to improve more than raw speed. By increasing spectral efficiency, HDT can let devices move more information within available wireless resources. Sending a larger data payload in less time can also allow a radio to return to a lower-power state sooner, potentially improving energy efficiency. The organisation also identifies reliability as a design goal, particularly in environments where multiple devices compete for wireless capacity.

The first major application identified by the standards group is premium wireless audio. Bluetooth SIG expects standardized lossless and high-resolution audio products to emerge in 2027. Higher throughput could provide more room for audio data and support multi-channel surround and spatial audio experiences. That does not mean every Bluetooth headphone will suddenly become lossless. Both the transmitting device and the receiving product will need compatible hardware and software, and audio codecs and product implementations will continue to determine the actual listening experience.

## Audio is the first major application planned

The distinction between a new radio capability and a new codec is important. Bluetooth HDT provides the transport capacity that an audio system can use; it does not, by itself, define every part of the audio encoding and decoding process. The standardisation effort aims to give manufacturers a common foundation rather than forcing each company to rely entirely on a proprietary wireless method. Consumers will still need to check product specifications for explicit support rather than assume that a device labelled Bluetooth 7.0 includes every optional feature.

Bluetooth SIG says HDT is an optional feature in the recently adopted Bluetooth Core 7.0 specification. Optional status means support can vary between products even when they share the same core-version number. A phone may support some Bluetooth 7.0 features while a headset supports a different subset. Manufacturers will have to implement the new PHY and validate interoperability before products can use the higher throughput. Older radios cannot be upgraded to the new physical layer through a routine firmware update.

## Existing devices will not gain the new capability through software alone

The potential applications extend beyond music. Bluetooth SIG points to faster synchronisation of sensor-rich smartwatch data, richer smart-glasses experiences that move data to a phone for processing, and shorter firmware-update times for connected home products. These examples reflect a broader shift in connected devices: products increasingly exchange more than small control messages. Wearables collect more detailed measurements, glasses may send data for AI processing, and household devices need to receive larger updates without occupying the radio for extended periods.
