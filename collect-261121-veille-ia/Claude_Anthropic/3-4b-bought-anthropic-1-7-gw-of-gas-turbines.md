Anthropic just spent $3.4 billion on natural gas turbines — $2,000 per kilowatt for 1.7 gigawatts of generation it will own outright. For a company that has always rented its compute, that is not a power purchase. It is a confession that renting stopped working.

The Financial Times reported on November 8 that Anthropic agreed in late September to pay about $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty gas-fired generators from OnePWR Solutions, a San Antonio equipment supplier. The FT called it the largest known single purchase of power-generation equipment by an AI lab.

The mechanics matter more than the headline. OnePWR ordered the Caterpillar generators about two years ago but could not find financing to complete the purchase. Anthropic originally planned to be OnePWR's customer and buy the electricity. Instead it took over the equipment orders directly — plus the engineering and construction capability to install them. It did not buy OnePWR itself, and none of OnePWR's employees moved over.

Two honesty notes before the math. First, neither Anthropic nor OnePWR commented, and Reuters said it could not independently verify the FT report. Second, there is one independent anchor: investment bank Piper Sandler, which advised OnePWR, lists the transaction on its own site as a $3.4 billion sell-side deal — the sale of OnePWR's "power generation platform to a frontier AI lab." That corroborates the price and the shape of the deal, if not every detail of the FT's account.

I ran the numbers against Anthropic's own filings, and the purchase looks small until you compare it to the right denominator:

- 
**$2,000 per kilowatt.** $3.4 billion divided by 1.7 gigawatts. That is the price of a delivery slot in a supply book that is already sold out — the backlog for gas turbines stretches into the early 2030s, and grid-connection queues run just as long.
- 
**2.72 months of rent.** Anthropic disclosed in its S-1 that it pays xAI $1.25 billion per month through May 2029 for compute. This purchase costs less than three months of that rent — and buys hardware it owns instead of hours it borrows.
- 
**20% of a quarter's revenue, 34.5% of a quarter's compute.** Q3 revenue was $17.0 billion; Q3 compute and infrastructure spend was about $9.86 billion, 58% of revenue. The turbines are one-third of a single quarter's compute bill.
- 
**0.38% of the commitments.** Anthropic's amended S-1, filed November 2, disclosed about $895.4 billion in total purchase and lease commitments, with roughly $69.8 billion due in the next twelve months. The $3.4 billion is 4.9% of that twelve-month slice.
- 
**1.7x a nuclear reactor.** A typical large nuclear reactor produces about 1 gigawatt. Anthropic just bought the generating capacity of nearly two of them, in gas form.

The point is not that $3.4 billion is large. Against $895.4 billion of commitments, it is a rounding error. The point is what it buys: queue position. Anthropic did not buy electricity. It bought factory-order slots placed two years ago, because ordering today means waiting until the early 2030s.

Here is the detail that makes the story stick. Anthropic's amended S-1, filed November 2, added a specific warning for prospective shareholders: its third-party infrastructure partners depend on outside financing to build the facilities Anthropic has contracted to rent, and if those partners fail to raise the money, the capacity could be delayed, repriced, reduced, or never delivered.

Within a week, that risk factor showed up on a flatbed truck. OnePWR — a third-party infrastructure partner — could not raise the financing. Anthropic's answer was to stop being a customer and become the owner, putting up $3.4 billion in cash commitments to rescue the delivery schedule.

That is a flip of the company's entire posture. The same S-1 says Anthropic expects "to enter into additional similar services contracts" — more renting. Now it is buying generation hardware outright. When your risk factor materializes in seven days, the strategy changes in seven days.

Anthropic is not alone in this. Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta have all announced plans to build massive on-site gas plants rather than draw from the public grid. The industry has decided that waiting for the grid is the bigger risk.

But gas has its own price. TechCrunch notes one analyst expects gas prices could more than triple by the end of the decade in some markets. And the permitting fight is already here: on October 30, a federal judge ruled xAI's 35 gas turbines at its Memphis-area site are not "mobile sources" under Mississippi's clean-air rules — the trailer loophole is dead, and I wrote about that ruling last week. Anthropic's 1.7 gigawatts will need permits too, and nobody has said where the generators will go or when they will run.

So the honest read is this: Anthropic paid $3.4 billion to convert a financing risk into a fuel-price risk and a permitting risk. That is still a good trade when the alternative is no power at all. But it is a trade, not a solution — and the company that just told IPO investors its suppliers might not be able to finance their promises is now the one writing the checks.
