# Anthropic Cuts Claude Prices 40% for U.S. Government Buyers, Extends Deal to States and Cities

Anthropic has widened its procurement agreement with the U.S. General Services Administration, offering federal agencies a 40% discount on Claude Enterprise, Claude for Government and the Claude API, and for the first time extending that pricing to state, local, tribal and territorial governments.

The company announced the deal on Oct. 27, 2026. The reduction applies to direct purchases immediately and will reach purchases made through cloud providers over the next several months. Anthropic estimates the arrangement will save the government about $1 billion.

Agencies buy at the OneGov rate through the GSA's Multiple Award Schedule. There is no minimum spending commitment; customers pay only for the usage they consume. Anthropic said it is also starting a dedicated program to help agencies deploy Claude, bringing practices common among its corporate customers to public-sector users in the coming weeks.

"This agreement is built to support that work, so they can apply Claude to hard problems without a large upfront commitment," the company said in its announcement.

Anthropic said roughly 500 U.S. federal, state and local government organizations now use Claude. Weekly active users of Claude for Government have risen about eightfold since February, and usage across federal civilian agencies has grown about 20-fold in a year, according to the company. Those figures were not independently verified.

### Security clearances and platform access

Claude for Government became generally available on Sept. 30 at the FedRAMP High authorization level, with near-feature parity to the commercial product. Agencies building their own tools can reach Claude through Amazon Bedrock or Google Cloud's Vertex AI; both carry FedRAMP High authorization, and select models on Bedrock are approved for Defense Department IL4 and IL5 workloads in AWS GovCloud. Claude Enterprise is pursuing FedRAMP Moderate authorization through the FedRAMP 20x program.

The GSA acts as procurement intermediary and negotiator under its OneGov strategy, which coordinates government-wide technology buying. The agency disclosed on Sept. 10, 2026, that it had added discounted, consumption-based access to OpenAI's ChatGPT to OneGov, and said in an April 29, 2026, update that the program had saved taxpayers $1.1 billion in its first year.

Anthropic cited public-sector deployments in its announcement, including a Utah pilot in which 34 developers across 10 state agencies received Claude Code. The company said 80% had a working environment within 30 minutes and 71% reported saving more than 20 hours by the end of the four-week trial. It also said one state IT developer moved nearly 900 API methods to a new framework in two days, a project scoped for a month, and that a state CIO's team responded to a ransomware attack in eight minutes rather than the usual two to three days. Those accounts were provided by the company and were not independently verified.

### What it means for the market

The move deepens a relationship that has swung sharply over the past year. Anthropic's post referenced a February order halting use of its tools across federal agencies, subsequent court rulings and rival pricing decisions as the backdrop to the discount, without detailing them.

The pricing puts Anthropic in closer competition with OpenAI for federal wallets, and the extension to states and municipalities opens a customer base that often lacks the budget staff to negotiate bespoke contracts. Consumption-based terms remove the upfront commitment that has slowed adoption among agencies with long procurement cycles.

For Anthropic, the discount trades margin for volume and for a foothold in systems that, once built around a model provider, tend to stay there. For cloud partners, the expanded channel means government Claude spending will increasingly flow through Bedrock and Vertex AI. For investors tracking enterprise AI revenue, the key question is whether the government segment can grow fast enough to offset the lower per-unit price, and whether the $1 billion savings figure holds up once agencies report actual usage.
