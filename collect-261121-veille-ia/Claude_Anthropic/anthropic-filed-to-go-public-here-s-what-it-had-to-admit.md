Anthropic filed to go public. Here's what it had to admit.

"Disclose," if you ask the lawyers. It means the same thing, written down early so nobody can say it was hidden.

On October 26, the company that makes Claude filed the paperwork to sell its stock to the public. The law makes a company write down what could go wrong before it takes your money, so these 335 pages say more than Anthropic has ever said in public. Here's who controls Anthropic, where the money really goes, why it signed up for so much computing power and who else is on the hook, what it says about jobs and safety, and where its story doesn't hold together.

Why this document

A keynote carries little legal risk. A prospectus carries a lot.

Every AI company tells you a story: blog posts, product launches, interviews. Very little of it can get the company sued.

An S-1 is different. It's the registration statement a company files with the Securities and Exchange Commission before it first sells stock to the public. If it leaves out something important, or says something misleading, investors can sue the company and the people who signed it. So lawyers go through every sentence, and the known risks get written down.

That's why Anthropic's S-1 is worth reading closely: in this document, spin costs more.[1]

Anthropic filed on October 26, 2026, after five months of confidential drafts. Its stock would trade on Nasdaq as "ANT." The price isn't set, and a lot of numbers are still blank. What's there is plenty.

Anthropic wants to be two things at once: the company racing to build the most powerful AI, and the brake on that race. Its own filing shows the race is written into contracts, and those contracts run through Amazon, Google and Broadcom. The brake is real, but it's a judgment call, and the contracts make every long use of it expensive.

Their claims, stamped by their own filing

Seven things Anthropic says, each checked against what the rest of the filing shows.

“Claude has experienced the fastest enterprise adoption of any technology in history”

Can't check

The filing gives no comparison with earlier technologies, so no number in it could prove this. The growth itself is real: $11.6 billion of revenue in the second quarter of 2026, and about $17 billion implied for the third.

“a net loss of … $80.7 billion for the six months ended June 30, 2026”

Mostly paper

$72.4 billion of it is re-pricing notes that turn into stock, most of them Amazon's. The operating loss was $0.7 billion, and cash from operations was positive.

It has: one training run was delayed more than a month. But the pause is written as a judgment call, while most of the compute contracts are written as "must."

“our technology should not be used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons”

It cost them

The federal government ordered agencies to stop using Anthropic and labeled it a national-security supply-chain risk. An appeals court upheld one designation, 2 to 1.

A

Part A: What they're selling

Three numbers tell you what kind of company this is: the growth, a loss that isn't what it looks like, and a bill that is.

f1strongQuarterly revenue, from the filing

Revenue went from $386 million in all of 2024 to $11.6 billion in a single quarter.

On the recordThat's three months of actual revenue, not the "annualized" figure companies like to quote. One quarter is about 30 times all of 2024, and the filing hints the next one was bigger.

Revenue was $41 million in 2023, $386 million in 2024 and $4.6 billion in 2025.[2] The filing calls it "the fastest enterprise adoption of any technology in history." That's hard to check: it would take a definition of adoption and a comparison with earlier technologies, and the filing gives neither. The quarterly line isn't in doubt.

The third quarter is blank too, at least in the tables. But the business section says revenue for the quarter ending September 30, 2026 "was 86 times our revenue for the three months ended December 31, 2024," and that quarter was $197 million. That works out to about $17 billion.[3]

What to watch: the real third-quarter number in the amended filing. If it lands near $17 billion, the growth hasn't stalled.

f2strongThe income statement, plus Amazon's own report

Most of the $80.7 billion loss is accounting, not cash. The operating loss was $0.7 billion.

On the recordWhen Anthropic's value rises, the notes that turn into its stock get more expensive on paper. Amazon's own report shows the other side: a $62.8 billion gain.

Anthropic raised much of its money through notes that turn into stock, mostly from Amazon. When its value goes up, the stock those notes will become is worth more, and Anthropic books the difference as a loss. The filing's line for this, which also covers a few similar instruments, was $72.4 billion in the first half of 2026. Another $8.8 billion of other expense came mostly from agreements that let Google and Amazon buy preferred shares at $259 each, weeks before other investors paid $589 (both prices from before a 10-for-1 split).[4] The operating loss was $693 million, and the second quarter showed an operating profit.

Paper losses still mean something. Those notes become shares, which shrinks everyone else's slice. And most of the cash on hand was raised, not earned: selling preferred stock brought in $110.4 billion in the first half alone. Cash from operations was helped by timing, such as bills not yet paid, and the filing expects "significant net cash used in operating and investing activities for the foreseeable future" as it builds data centers.[4, 2, 6]

You can check the paper side from outside the filing. Amazon's quarterly report shows a $62.8 billion gain on its Anthropic stake over the same six months, and $15.9 billion of tax expense on it.[5] The amounts and instruments aren't identical, but it's the other side of the same rise in value.

What to watch: whether a headline means the paper loss or the operating loss. They differ about a hundredfold.

f3strongContract commitments, from the filing's notes

The bill is real: roughly $850 billion of computing commitments, paid out through as late as 2045, against about $46 billion of revenue a year.

On the recordMuch of it must be paid "regardless of actual usage." Much of the revenue can leave on short notice.

Anthropic has committed to roughly $850 billion of computing power, data centers and chips, most of it due between now and 2045. It has also committed to buy more TPU chips, Google's AI processors, for an estimated $125 to $150 billion, depending on what gets delivered.[4, 7]

Not all of it is the same kind of promise. The Microsoft and Amazon deals say "regardless of actual usage." The Broadcom equipment leases, $161.2 billion in all, are non-cancelable; the biggest single one, signed in March 2026, is $125.2 billion over five years. SpaceXAI is the exception: up to $120.5 billion, about $3.1 billion a month once its capacity arrives, which Anthropic expects in December. After early 2027, either side can cancel on 90 days' notice. Nscale, Lambda and AMD depend on delivery and other conditions.[2]

The revenue side is looser. The filing says much of it isn't under long-term contracts and that many customers can "terminate with limited or no notice." The top five customers were about a quarter of revenue over nine months. And models released in the previous six months brought in over 80% of third-quarter revenue, so the revenue depends on new releases continuing.[8, 2]

What to watch: the payment schedule. The filing leaves near-term payments blank. The next version should show how much comes due each year, and how much can be canceled.

f4strongFinancing terms, from the filing and Amazon's report

The money moves in circles.

On the recordAmazon's newest financing unlocks as Amazon delivers the computers Anthropic rents from Amazon. Broadcom offers financing "solely to finance" rent owed to Broadcom.

Nearly half of Anthropic's revenue, 48% in the first half of 2026, comes through the cloud marketplaces of Amazon, Google and Microsoft.[8] Those same companies are also its investors, suppliers and competitors. The filing says so directly: "certain of the CSPs on which we rely for compute are also strategic investors in us, are our customers, and offer AI models and services that compete with ours."[2]

None of this is hidden; it's all in the filing. But it means a company built around safety has staked roughly a trillion dollars on demand growing fast.

B

Part B: Who's steering

This is the part that confuses everyone on first read, so here it is drawn out. There are two different groups of seven.

The thread

The race is in the contracts. The brake is a "may." This part is about who decides which one wins.

f5strongDescription of capital stock

Seven founders share one share. It casts 50.1% of the general shareholder vote.

On the recordThat vote doesn't shrink when they sell or give shares away. It lasts until fewer than three qualifying founders are left.

Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei, Tom Brown, Jack Clark, Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish and Chris Olah sit in a company of their own, the Founder LLC. It holds exactly one share of a special class, Class F. On matters put to shareholders generally, that share casts 50.1% of the vote, "including the election of our directors other than the Class T directors."[9] Some decisions, like changes to the trust below, need separate votes by other classes too.

One condition for staying in the group is keeping at least 2% of the shares a founder held on IPO day. Once fewer than three qualifying founders remain for 90 days in a row, the Class F vote winds down over 450 days. The filing also describes ways it can end sooner.[9]

f6moderateOwnership and voting power, partly derived

About 10% of the money. A fixed majority of the vote.

On the recordMuch of what Amazon and Google bought is non-voting stock. Everything sold to the public shares a 49.9% slice with the founders' own stock.

The founders' share of the company is my arithmetic, not the filing's. It adds up their listed holdings and divides by the fully diluted share count the filing implies.[10] The voting side isn't arithmetic. The filing states it.

Its most direct sentence is this one: holders of Class A stock "generally will not be able to affect the outcome of any matter submitted to a vote of our stockholders or the election of our directors, regardless of how they vote." The exception is in the same sentence: if a majority of the founders can't agree, their share votes in proportion to everyone else's.[9]

f7strongManagement, governance

The board has seven seats. The trust elects four, and the general vote, which the founders control, elects three.

On the recordOn IPO day the trust's majority is softer than it sounds. One of its seats is empty, and changing another needs a board committee's OK, or else an employee vote.

The board runs the company and can hire or fire the CEO. The Long-Term Benefit Trust elects four of its seats. The trust itself is a separate group: trustees paid $400,000 a year each ($425,000 for the chair), who can't buy or hold the stock. The other three directors are elected by the general vote, which means the founders: Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei and Yasmin Razavi, an investor.[12]

Look closer and the trust's four becomes three. One trust seat is empty at listing. Chris Liddell holds another. To swap in someone else for that seat, or remove him without cause, the trust needs the OK of the board's nominating committee, which Daniela Amodei chairs. If the committee says no, employees who hold Class E stock vote on it. Removal for cause needs neither.[9, 12]

f8strongGovernance, related-party transactions

Dario Amodei is on every side of the table.

On the recordHe's the CEO the board can fire, a director, one of the seven founder votes, and the proxy voter for some donated shares. The trust meant to balance him elects most of the board, but its only other check is advance notice, which can be waived.

Dario Amodei votes shares that other people donate to two charity funds "in his sole and absolute discretion," unless the board names someone else. He's also one of the four first managers of a new fund that holds 5% of the company; until its governance is set, the board decides what it gives away.[12]

To be fair to Anthropic, it argues this is the point. A normal public company gets pushed to grow profits every quarter. Anthropic's answer is a trust with no money at stake that elects most of the board, plus founders it calls "distinctly equipped to be stewards of our mission." It's also a public benefit corporation, so its directors are "not merely permitted, but obligated" to weigh the mission against profit. And two independent directors sit on a board committee that oversees model safety.[13, 8, 12]

So how strong is the trust? Its main power is real: it elects and can remove most of the board. Beyond that it has "no decision-making authority in the day-to-day management or strategy setting of the company." Its other direct check is advance notice before certain big moves, including handing control of a model past capability limits the board sets, and that "notice period may be waived." The company pays the trust's bills, $8.4 million in the first nine months of 2026. Executives "have collaborated closely on sourcing, interviewing, and selecting Trustees." Anthropic gave $450,000 to a think tank run by one trustee. And the board sets its own yardstick for the public benefit; no outside standard is required.[9, 12]

The trust's rules can be rewritten, or the trust ended, with no public vote, if 80% of the board and 80% of the trustees agree. Without the trustees' support, a change needs the founders, the employees and a majority of Class A shareholders. Anthropic says it kept that door open "to preserve long-term flexibility if circumstances change."[8, 9]

You can buy Anthropic. You can't steer it.

C

Part C: Where they say AI is going

Strip out the legal language and the filing makes a plain prediction, describes how it plans to get there, and says what that has already done to its own staff.

The prediction: "Powerful AI," systems that match top experts across most fields and work on their own for hours, days or weeks, "will become widely available within a few years."[13]

The method is what they call "closing the loop": each version of Claude helps build the next. And the risk section adds this: "we may not identify a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred."[8]

f9strongBusiness section, Anthropic's own workforce

Anthropic's staff grew more than fivefold. Its quarterly revenue grew 86-fold.

On the recordThe filing says less about jobs than you'd expect, so the clearest evidence is the company itself. In April 2026, its own internal use of Claude, valued at list prices, passed its monthly personnel costs, not counting stock pay.

In September 2026, "over 90% of the contributions to our codebase … were written by Claude." In the third quarter, the daily amount of code per engineer was over 45 times the 2024 average. Outside the company selling the tool, the measured effect of AI on building software is far smaller.[14]

That's one unusual company, and it hired more people, not fewer. What changed is how the work gets done. But it's the company selling the tool, describing what the tool did to its own workforce. In the risk section, it warns of new "taxes, fees, or other measures specifically applicable to AI companies or AI-related labor displacement," and says it may pay displacement costs "for which we may not receive a corresponding commercial benefit."[8]

What to watch: your own week, if your work is mostly reading, writing or code. The useful question is which parts of it a tool like this already does.

f10strongRisk factors, verbatim

The safety section says things companies selling stock rarely say.

On the recordThese are Anthropic's own words about its own models and the industry's. The legal duty to disclose produced them.

The company also writes that concerns that advanced AI could "pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity" have been raised "within our organization," and that its models could give "meaningful assistance to malicious actors seeking to develop chemical, biological, or other dangerous weapons." It warns that as models get more capable, they've become better at getting around the tests meant to catch them.[8]

Some of it has already happened. In September 2025, a Chinese state-sponsored group used Claude Code to try to break into about 30 organizations, with "a limited number of successful intrusions." Anthropic calls it "the first documented case of a large-scale cyberattack executed without substantial human intervention." In June 2026, the Commerce Department imposed worldwide export restrictions on two of its models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, and Anthropic switched them off for every customer until the restrictions were lifted. In October, the Federal Trade Commission sent it a civil investigative demand about its privacy and safety practices.[8]

And it estimates that 5% to 7% of its revenue over nine months came from customers it later found breaking its usage policy or terms, and that enforcement will cost it some growth. In 2025 it agreed to pay $1.5 billion to settle a class action by book authors over copyright, and more suits are pending.[2, 8]

A company selling stock rarely writes sentences like these about its own product, and that counts in Anthropic's favor.

D

Part D: Where it doesn't hold together

Read the whole thing and four tensions keep coming back. Then the facts that cut the other way.

1. The engine is also the danger. The business section calls AI that helps build AI "one of the most significant structural advantages in frontier AI." A few pages later it "could also pose a considerable safety risk." Anthropic asks governments for industry-wide pacing on exactly this, then adds that the call "does not describe our own model development or release timelines."[14]

2. "Not in tension." The business section says "commercial success and a serious commitment to safety are not in tension." The risk section says "we may also face potential conflicts when safety and business interests diverge." Safety can help sell the product and still make a given release slower or less profitable. The first sentence leaves that out.[14, 8]

3. The pledge that keeps the power. Each founder has pledged 80% of their Anthropic stock to charity. The filing calls the pledges "voluntary, revocable expressions of each co-founder’s philanthropic intent" that "do not constitute legally binding obligations." As long as they keep the minimum stake, giving shares away changes nothing about control: the founders' "personal economic interests in us will decrease while their voting control through the Founder LLC will remain unchanged."[8]

f11moderateMy reading of the filing's own wording

4. The brake is a 'may.' Most of the bill is a 'must.'

My readingThe pause is written as a judgment call. Most of the compute contracts are written as obligations.

The quotes are the filing's. The reading is mine: a company whose contracts "generally must be satisfied regardless of our revenue or utilization," with cross-default clauses that could make "substantially all" of its debt come due at once, and whose revenue depends on releasing new models, has an expensive reason to keep any pause short.[2, 8]

Anthropic says it may have to pause. Its trillion-dollar bill makes every long pause expensive.

The counterweight. Stopping there would be unfair, because the filing also shows Anthropic using the brake. It delayed pretraining a new frontier model "for more than a month" to tighten security against theft of its model weights. In April 2026 it held back its most capable model, Mythos, giving it only to selected organizations for cyber defense instead of releasing it broadly. Its safety policy requires it to publish risk reports on its models.[8, 14]

It has also paid for a principle. In February 2026, Anthropic said its technology shouldn't be used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons. The President directed every federal agency to stop using it. The Department of War, as the filing names it, labeled Anthropic a "supply-chain risk to national security" and said no military contractor "may conduct any commercial activity" with it. A district court called that retaliation for protected speech. In September, an appeals court upheld one of the designations, 2 to 1, and Anthropic has asked the full court to rehear it.[8]

Those choices cost it something. None of them cost billions on the compute bill, and that's the test this filing can't answer yet.

A class action filed in September accuses Anthropic and other AI labs of agreeing to slow down AI development, in violation of antitrust law. Anthropic says it disagrees and will fight it.[8] The brake is now something it's being sued over.

E

Part E: Why so much, and who else is on the hook

That's what the filing says. Three plain questions are left: why sign up for that much, can it actually get built, and if it goes wrong, who else gets hurt?

f12strongThe filing's notes and risk factors

It ran short before. Leases it had signed but not started grew more than sixfold in six months.

On the recordBuy too little and you turn customers away again. Buy too much and you pay for idle machines. Anthropic chose too much.

The filing is plain about why. "In the past, we have significantly underestimated our compute requirements, which left us capacity constrained and impacted both product development and deployment." It also names the cost of the fix: computing power that comes online before the demand does could mean "periods of excess or underutilized compute and committed cash outflows that do not produce immediate revenue."[8]

So it signed. Leases that hadn't started yet were about $50.2 billion at the end of 2025 and about $321.4 billion six months later.[4, 26]

f13strongAmazon's, Alphabet's and Anthropic's filings

Big tech spends like this too. But Amazon and Google each have about half a trillion dollars of signed future sales behind them. Anthropic has $10.8 billion.

On the recordMost of Anthropic's customers pay as they go. Its bills are fixed. Its revenue isn't.

The obvious objection is that Amazon, Google and Microsoft also spend hundreds of billions on data centers. They do. But Amazon reports about $496 billion of signed future sales, "primarily related to AWS," and Google Cloud reports $513.9 billion.[5, 23]

Anthropic's own figure is $10.8 billion.[4] Its revenue is "primarily consumption-based, and much of it is not subject to long-term contractual commitments, while our compute and infrastructure commitments generally extend over multiple years." About 80% of its first-half revenue was pay as you go.[8, 2]

Anthropic is also about a fifth of each partner's total. Its Amazon commitment is about 22% of Amazon's signed future sales, and its Google commitment about 22% of Google Cloud's.[26]

f14strongRisk factors, plus a word count

Every commitment in the filing is in dollars. Not one says how much electricity.

On the recordA data center needs chips, memory, heavy power equipment and power. Each one comes from a short line of suppliers.

The chips come mostly from Taiwan, "primarily at Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company." The fast memory on them is "largely manufactured by" three companies: SK hynix, Samsung and Micron. The electrical gear, including high-voltage transformers, switchgear and turbines, is made by "only a few manufacturers," and some of it "must be ordered years in advance."[8]

Then there's power. The filing says the grid "was not designed to accommodate the rapid increase in demand associated with AI development." Yet the words "gigawatt" and "megawatt" don't appear in it once.[8, 27]

f15moderatePress and county reports, plus the filing's power terms

The building is the quick part. Getting the power company to hook it up has been quoted at seven years.

On the recordSo builders skip the line and make their own power on site. Anthropic pays that fuel bill with no cap, on some deals for 20 years.

Take Loudoun County, Virginia, the heart of what's called Data Center Alley. A big data center building typically goes up in one to four years.[31] In 2022 the local power company, Dominion, said full power for new arrivals would take four years. In 2024 it stretched that to seven, for data centers needing more than 100 megawatts.[28, 29] Now the county is weighing a pause of up to 12 months on some new data center applications, with the decision set for November 17.[30] The filing doesn't say where Anthropic's own sites are, so treat Loudoun as the clearest example of the wait, not as Anthropic's address.

The way around the line is to make power next door. The filing says "certain of our planned data center facilities rely on dedicated on-site natural gas-fired generation," with fuel costs "borne by us on a pass-through basis without cost caps." Some of these deals have "initial terms of 20 years with annual escalators," with payments owed "regardless of actual utilization."[8]

And the chips keep changing. Nvidia moved to a "one-year rhythm" for new data center chips in 2024.[32] A typical company server rack draws about 11 kilowatts; Nvidia's current AI rack draws up to 142, cooled with liquid.[33] A building can be behind the chips by the time it's plugged in.

Anthropic's answer is to split the two. It locks in data center space early and buys the chips late: "this approach enables us to secure data center capacity early while deferring larger accelerator investments until our specific compute needs are more certain."[14] Reserve the garage. Buy the cars later.

So can it get built? Yes. Slowly, and at a price.

f16moderatePartner filings, matched to the S-1

It isn't Anthropic's bill alone. Amazon, Google, Broadcom and their lenders are wired into it.

On the recordMarking up its Anthropic stake was about half of Amazon's profit in the first half of 2026, after tax. Anthropic's leases are nearly the size of Broadcom's whole order book.

Amazon is the plainest. Its quarterly report shows a $62.8 billion first-half gain, "primarily from our nonvoting preferred stock in Anthropic," with $15.9 billion of tax on it. That's about half of its $92.9 billion profit for the half.[5, 26]

Google is the same pattern, unnamed. Alphabet's quarterly report never mentions Anthropic. But it lists "$20.0 billion of future capital funding commitments with a private company contingent upon the achievement of specified operational and financial milestones through 2030," and that matches the terms of Anthropic's Google note agreement.[23, 25] Its untraded stakes, "primarily" one private company, produced a $113.6 billion paper gain in the first half, before tax. Alphabet also guarantees up to $43.8 billion of other companies' data center payments, without naming them.[23]

Broadcom doesn't name Anthropic either, and its terms match too: up to $42 billion of a customer's notes, to be used "solely" for lease payments, on five-year rack leases.[24, 25] Its firm backlog is about $179.2 billion; Anthropic's Broadcom leases come to $161.2 billion. Broadcom handed the racks and leases to a "financial partner," and it covers up to about $29 billion if the customer stops paying.[24, 2] Press reports put Apollo and Blackstone behind that partner, with a $35 billion deal.[34] Broadcom's top five customers were about 55% of its revenue last quarter, up from about 40% a year earlier.[24]

Then the smaller builders. Anthropic's $44.6 billion Nscale deal depends on Nscale getting financing for the chips and buildings. Its biggest Lambda deal, $34.9 billion, can end if Lambda doesn't secure the building lease in time.[2]

Amazon and Google are big enough to take a hit. It's still a web: when Anthropic's value moves, their profits move with it.

Where it's thin

Three places carry the strain, all in the filing.

Some money only flows if revenue hits targets

Some of Anthropic's note agreements can be drawn only if it hits "certain revenue performance targets and/or" if others deliver more computing power. Google's is the one spelled out: draws are tied to trailing revenue targets, and "Google may elect to defer funding of revenue-linked draws." Broadcom's isn't shown to work that way. Anthropic says it has "historically surpassed all such revenue performance targets."[8, 2] A bad quarter could shut that tap right when it's needed.

One default can pull the rest

A default under certain agreements could trigger cross-defaults, "potentially resulting in the acceleration of substantially all of our outstanding, and any future, indebtedness."[8] Miss one payment, and nearly all the debt can come due at once.

A big supplier is a rival

SpaceXAI, the company behind a competing model, is set to supply about $3.1 billion of computing a month from December. After early 2027, "each agreement may be cancelled by either party upon 90 days' advance written notice."[2]

f17moderateMy reading, built from the filings' own mechanisms

How it could fall: three paths. This part is interpretation, not the filing.

My readingEach step is a mechanism a filing describes. Putting them in a row is my reading, not a forecast.

Path one: demand slows. Revenue misses a target, so the revenue-linked money pauses. A payment gets missed, and the cross-defaults and guarantees kick in. Broadcom's backstop allows "reselling the AI racks" after a default.[24] Repossessed chips coming onto the market would press on every loan backed by chips.

Path two: nothing breaks, but the stock drops. Amazon and Google booked gains as Anthropic's value rose. They'd book losses if it fell, and their profits would fall with it. Big index funds hold both, so that's how it would reach a retirement account.

Path three: supply runs short. Chips, memory or power arrive late. Some rent can start anyway: the Broadcom leases say payments "may commence earlier in certain circumstances, such as when the readiness of a data center facility is delayed."[2] Customers who pay as they go move to rivals, and you're back on path one.

Remember the brake from Part D? A long safety pause would slow new releases, and models released in the previous six months brought in over 80% of third-quarter revenue.[2] Slower revenue is what pauses the money that's tied to revenue targets.

The brake is wired to the bill.

F

Part F: The other way to do it

OpenAI hasn't published its filing yet, so this is a lighter comparison, with every OpenAI number labeled by where it came from.

OpenAI confidentially submitted a draft filing and announced it on June 8.[15] Its numbers here come from its partners' audited filings, the state attorney general's statement, and news reports of documents shared with investors. Treat them as softer than Anthropic's.

The control setups are close to mirror images. At OpenAI, a nonprofit, the OpenAI Foundation, has "sole power and authority to appoint members of the PBC Board of Directors" and can remove them. It keeps a safety committee that can require changes "up to and including halting the release of models," and on safety questions the company's directors may weigh only the mission, not shareholders' money.[16] Two state attorneys general hold those commitments. Anthropic's filing names no comparable arrangement with an attorney general; its outside checks are the courts, its public-benefit duties and regulators like the ones that have already acted.

On paper, OpenAI's brake is more direct. The closest thing to a test came under its older structure: in November 2023 the nonprofit's board fired Sam Altman, and he was back within days.

The revenue lead has flipped. OpenAI's revenue was about three times Anthropic's in 2025.[21] In the second quarter of 2026, Anthropic's was about 1.7 times OpenAI's.[18] In the first quarter, both lost money on operations: Anthropic $1.7 billion, and OpenAI a reported $9.3 billion.[2, 17] In the second, Anthropic showed a $1.0 billion operating profit; OpenAI's second-quarter result was reported only in summaries, so it's left out.

One caution on that profit: Anthropic hasn't yet counted most of its employee stock awards as an expense. Had it gone public on June 30, it would have booked $2.9 billion at once, and $23.7 billion more over about two years.[4] Its operating results will look worse once it lists.

Both stand on the same giants. Microsoft's audited report shows $24.1 billion of revenue from OpenAI in its last fiscal year.[22] Amazon is Anthropic's largest backer, and it has also committed $50 billion to OpenAI.[5]

What to watch

Five things will show whether the brake holds.

The payment schedule

The filing leaves the near-term bill blank. The amended version should show how much comes due each year, and how much of it can be canceled.

The third quarter, and the targets

Third-quarter revenue is blank in the tables, though one sentence implies about $17 billion. The real number shows whether the growth is a peak or a pace, and whether revenue keeps clearing the targets that unlock some of the money.

The empty seats

Watch who fills the board vacancy and the fifth trustee seat, and how they're chosen.

The founders' gifts

The 80% pledges will show up as gift filings, or they won't. Watch where the shares go, and who votes them afterward.

The next pause
