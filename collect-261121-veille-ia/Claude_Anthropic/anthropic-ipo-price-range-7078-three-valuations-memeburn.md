## **The Anthropic IPO Price Range and Terms**

If you’re new to this, an **IPO**, or initial public offering, is when a private company sells shares on a stock exchange for the first time. Anthropic plans to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker **ANT**. The price range is the company’s opening offer to big investors. The final price can still land inside it, above it, or below it. 

The numbers below come from Anthropic’s amended registration statement, filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) on November 10, and its own roadshow announcement:

| **Term** | **Figure** | 
| Price range | $70.00 to $78.00 per Class A share | 
| Shares offered | 825,000,000 | 
| Sold by Anthropic | 801,351,354 | 
| Sold by existing shareholders | 23,648,646 (Anthropic gets none of this money) | 
| Extra shares banks can buy (30-day option) | Up to 123,750,000 | 
| Public offering size (our math) | $57.75B at $70 to $64.35B at $78 | 
| Private sale of non-voting Class N shares | $26.0B, at the IPO price | 
| Exchange and ticker | Nasdaq Global Select Market, ANT | 
| Lead banks named first | Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley | 

The **private sale** is unusual. Seven companies, **AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC Partners**, have agreed to buy **$26 billion** of non-voting shares at whatever price the IPO sets. Most of them sell Anthropic the chips, memory or cloud computing it runs on. That gives the deal a built-in base of demand, but it also ties Anthropic even more closely to its suppliers.

Add the two together, and the total raise reaches about **$90.4 billion** at the top of the range. That would edge past the roughly **$86 billion** SpaceX raised in June, which Anthropic had reportedly hoped to match or beat. On the public offering alone, though, Anthropic would raise less than SpaceX did.

Reports citing people familiar with the deal say pricing is expected on **November 18**, with trading on **November 19**. Anthropic hasn’t confirmed those dates, so treat them as likely, not fixed.

## **Why There Are Three Different Valuations**

A company’s **valuation**, or market value, is its share price multiplied by its number of shares. The disagreement is about which shares to count.

| **Valuation** | **How it’s calculated** | **At $70** | **At $78** | 
| Shares outstanding after the IPO | 13.09 billion shares, from the filing | ~$916B | **~$1.02T** | 
| “About $1.3 trillion” | Reported by several outlets; method not disclosed | — | ~$1.3T | 
| Fully diluted | Includes shares that could be issued later, such as employee stock awards | — | **Up to ~$1.4T** | 

*First row: our math from the filing’s post-offering share count. The $1.4 trillion figure implies about 18 billion fully diluted shares at $78, by our math.*

The **$1.02 trillion** figure counts only shares that will exist right after the IPO. The **$1.4 trillion** figure also counts shares Anthropic has promised but not yet issued, mainly stock awards for employees. That’s roughly **5 billion** extra shares, by our math, which is why the gap is so big.

Our view: the fully diluted number is the fairer one for judging what you’d pay for a slice of the company, because those extra shares will eventually exist and dilute everyone else. The smaller number is the more accurate headline for what the market will show on day one.

## **Down From $2 Trillion: What the Range Tells You**

A few weeks ago, reports said Anthropic could seek a valuation of up to **$2 trillion**. The range lands well below that. One way to compare is the **price-to-sales ratio**: how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of yearly revenue.

Anthropic reported about **$17.0 billion** of revenue for the three months to **September 30**. Multiplied by four, that’s roughly **$67.8 billion** a year at its current pace. Here’s how the valuations compare, by our math:

| **Valuation** | **Price-to-sales (Q3 revenue x 4)** | 
| $916B (bottom of range, shares outstanding) | ~13.5x | 
| $1.02T (top of range, shares outstanding) | ~15.0x | 
| $1.4T (top of range, fully diluted) | ~20.6x | 
| $2T (figure reported in October) | ~29.5x | 

Even the lowest of these is a high multiple for most companies. But it’s a big step down from the $2 trillion talk, and at the top of the range on shares outstanding, the price is only about **6% above** the **$965 billion** valuation from Anthropic’s private funding round in May, by our math. The debate over a $2 trillion price tag was always about whether growth could justify it. This range suggests the banks found a lower number easier to sell.

The timing doesn’t help. Investors have been questioning how long the AI spending boom can last, and AI stocks have cooled this autumn. Anthropic’s own filings show why. Its Q3 revenue grew about 12x from a year earlier, but the quarter also swung to an operating loss as short-term computing costs jumped.

## **What a Class A Share Actually Gets You**

This is the part most coverage skips. Anthropic has **five classes of stock**, and the shares sold to the public carry the least control of any voting class.

- **Class A (public buyers):** one vote per share.
- **Class F:** a single share held by a company owned by Anthropic’s co-founders, including Dario and Daniela Amodei. It carries**50.1% of general voting power** , and no claim on profits.
- **Class T:** held by Anthropic’s**Long-Term Benefit Trust** , an independent body set up to keep the company focused on its safety mission. It elects the directors who make up a**majority of the board** .
- **Class N:** non-voting shares, including those sold to the seven tech companies.
- **Class E:** shares for employees other than the co-founders, with limited voting rights.

In plain terms, public shareholders get a share of the profits, if any, but very little say. With 50.1% of the general vote in the founders’ hands and the board majority picked by the trust, Class A holders can’t outvote either group. This structure makes Anthropic a **“controlled company”** under Nasdaq rules.

Anthropic is also a **public benefit corporation**, a for-profit company legally required to weigh a stated mission alongside profits. Its filing warns that AI systems could pose “catastrophic or existential risk to humanity.” That’s an unusual line for an IPO document, and it explains why the founders built so many layers of control.

## **Where the Money Goes**

Anthropic says it will use the money for general purposes, including computing infrastructure, hiring and possible acquisitions. The bigger context is its spending commitments. The filing shows about **$900.9 billion** in committed computing and infrastructure spending, with about **$70.7 billion** due within 12 months of September 30.

At the midpoint price of **$74**, Anthropic estimates net proceeds of about **$84.6 billion**, including the private sale. By our math, that’s about **1.2 times** the bill due over the next year. The IPO doesn’t fund Anthropic’s long-term plans on its own, but it gives it a cushion for the next year of commitments.

## **FAQs**

### **What is a greenshoe option in an IPO?**

A greenshoe, or overallotment option, lets the banks running an IPO buy extra shares from the company, usually up to 15% more, within 30 days. If demand is strong, the banks use it to sell more stock. If the price falls, they can buy shares in the market instead, which helps support the price in the first weeks.

### **How do IPO roadshows work?**

During a roadshow, a company’s executives and bankers pitch the stock to large investors in meetings and online presentations, usually over one to two weeks. The banks collect orders at different prices, called the book. That demand helps set the final IPO price, which can land inside, above or below the original range.

### **Can I buy IPO shares through a regular brokerage app?**

Sometimes. Several US brokers offer limited IPO access to eligible customers, often with minimum account sizes or trading history rules. You usually request shares before pricing and may get fewer than you asked for, or none. Most individual investors end up buying once the stock starts trading on the exchange.

### **What happens to a stock when a lock-up period ends?**

When a lock-up ends, employees and early investors can sell shares for the first time. If many do, the extra supply can push the price down, sometimes sharply. If insiders hold on, the effect can be small. Investors often watch lock-up dates as a known source of selling pressure.

### **Is OpenAI planning an IPO too?**

OpenAI hasn’t filed publicly, but reports have suggested it could follow Anthropic to the public markets.
