The Claude maker's S-1 filing shows on-site natural gas generation at the center of its compute plans, along with a list of uncapped energy risks

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, has a power problem. Its solution, laid out in an S-1 filing dated October 26, 2026, is to generate electricity on-site by burning natural gas.

A post on X attached a price tag to the move: a **$3.4 billion** agreement to produce gas-fired electricity for data centers. Research into the filing points to a more layered arrangement. It found no single deal of $3 billion or $3.4 billion devoted solely to gas generation. Anthropic’s gas exposure instead sits inside much larger compute and infrastructure contracts.

## What the S-1 actually says

The filing states that Anthropic’s coming data center facilities will rely on dedicated natural gas generation located on-site. That is a meaningful shift for a company that has historically rented capacity from cloud giants.

The reasoning is practical. Competition for conventional grid power is heating up, and connecting a new facility to the grid can take years. Gas plants can be stood up faster than renewable projects or new grid capacity, which makes them the quickest route to the megawatts Anthropic needs.

The filing does not dress this up as a risk-free choice. It flags rising operating costs, including fuel and transportation expenses that are **uncapped**. It also cites potential supply disruptions and volatile pricing as demand for natural gas climbs across the sector.

The company also acknowledges that clean energy alternatives may not be workable within the timelines it needs.

## The Nscale contract and West Virginia

The biggest piece of the gas puzzle is a $45 billion, six-year agreement with Nscale for compute capacity at its Monarch campus in West Virginia. The campus will run on natural gas generators, with the site aiming for roughly 2 GW of on-site generating capacity.

Anthropic’s own slice of that facility targets around 460 MW. For scale, that single commitment dwarfs the $3.4 billion figure circulating on social media by more than tenfold.

It is not the only power arrangement on the books. A 470 MW power deal with ERock was reportedly disclosed in August 2026.

These gas-related commitments fit within a broader compute strategy spanning multiple providers and hyperscalers, valued somewhere in the $500B to $800B range.

## From tenant to landlord

The deeper story here is a change in how Anthropic gets its computing power. Historically, AI labs have rented server capacity from hyperscalers, the large cloud providers that handle the messy business of buildings, cooling and electricity.

Under what the filing calls the “Anthropic Direct” model, the company is moving toward owning its facilities. When Anthropic rented, fuel price spikes were mostly someone else’s headache, at least in the short run. Now those costs flow straight through to its own books.

The filing also commits Anthropic to covering grid upgrade costs tied to its projects. It says the company will absorb any electricity price impacts its facilities create for consumers.

## What this means

The filing positions power supply as one of the hardest limits on how fast Anthropic can grow ahead of its expected IPO. For prospective investors, the large capital commitments signal ambition, but uncapped energy costs could produce unpleasant surprises, and the filing’s own risk factors suggest profitability may take a hit in the short term if gas prices move against the company.

There is also a reputational dimension. AI companies have spent years promoting sustainability goals, and leaning on gas generation sits awkwardly next to that messaging. Anthropic’s argument, as reflected in the filing, is essentially one of timing: clean power cannot arrive fast enough, so gas fills the gap.

Key things to watch include how the Monarch campus ramps toward its targeted capacity, whether further deals like the reported ERock arrangement surface, and how Anthropic’s cost disclosures evolve as the IPO process moves forward.
