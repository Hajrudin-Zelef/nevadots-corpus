# Anthropic's Claude discovers an unknown enzyme system similar to CRISPR from bacteriophages, and CEO Dario Amodi predicts that AI could 'cure many diseases within 5-10 years.'


 AI company Anthropic has announced that its AI, Claude, has analyzed bacteriophage DNA and discovered an unknown enzyme system with repetitive sequences similar to the genome editing technology **CRISPR** . The specific function of this system, named 'array-associated reverse transcriptases,' or 'ART' for short, is not yet known, but it is an example of Claude autonomously examining a large amount of DNA sequences and discovering a new biological system to study.

In this research, Claude is used to analyze large amounts of DNA sequences and related literature to find proteins and biological systems that have not yet been thoroughly investigated, and to narrow down candidates that are worth experimenting with.

Claude was given the task of searching for interesting **reverse transcriptases** (RTs) in a massive DNA sequence database. Reverse transcriptases are enzymes that create DNA using RNA as a template, and it is known that various types exist not only in retroviruses but also in bacteria and other organisms. According to Anthropic, Claude collected more than 200,000 reverse transcriptases, selected about 3,500 new candidate systems, and then analyzed 20 of the most promising ones in detail.

 Approximately 950 Claude agents were used in parallel for this task, processing a total of 210 million tokens over 21 hours. During this process, one agent noticed a region next to a certain reverse transcriptase gene where the DNA sequence was repeated regularly many times. Claude examined the number and intervals of repetitions, compared them to known reverse transcriptase systems, and checked whether the same structure had been reported in previous studies before reporting it to human researchers as a candidate for a new biological system. The research team has named the system discovered by Claude 'ART'.

 Claude has discovered a previously unknown enzyme system hidden in the DNA of bacteriophages. Beside the enzyme's gene sits a long array of repeating DNA—a structure that looks somewhat similar to CRISPR.

ART was discovered primarily in bacteriophages, which are viruses that infect bacteria. Its basic structure consists of three components: a gene that produces reverse transcriptase, another gene of unknown function located next to it, and a long DNA repeat sequence arranged at regular intervals. While reverse transcriptase itself had already been found in previous studies, according to Anthropic, this is the first time that this repeat sequence and adjacent proteins have been recognized as a single system.

 The research team points out that the arrangement of these repetitive sequences is similar to that of CRISPR. In CRISPR-Cas, RNA is created from the repetitive sequences and the sequences between them in the DNA of bacteria and other organisms. The Cas protein then uses this RNA as a marker to recognize specific DNA, and this mechanism has been applied to develop genome editing technology that manipulates targeted DNA sequences.

Anthropic's initial experiments confirmed that multiple short RNAs are produced from the repeating sequence of ART. This is one of the similarities with CRISPR, but it is still unknown what these RNAs and reverse transcriptases actually do. Anthropic states that the combination of features found in ART has only been observed in a few systems so far, and some of these known systems have functions such as cutting, replicating, and inserting DNA. However, just because the repeating sequence of ART is similar to that of CRISPR does not mean that ART is a system that edits DNA in the same way.

 Anthropic CEO Dario Amodi commented on the findings, stating that 'Claude led the discovery of a molecular system that could be a new gene-editing mechanism.' However, Amodi himself acknowledged that its precise function, potential applications in biotechnology, and the significance of the discovery are 'still unclear.' Furthermore, Amodi predicted that the acceleration of scientific research by AI could potentially lead to treatments for many currently incurable diseases within the next 5 to 10 years.

Lucas Harrington, a CRISPR researcher, explained the announcement by saying, 'Simply put, this research noticed the presence of strange DNA repeat sequences next to known and new genes.' In other words, he emphasized that the important achievement at this stage is the discovery of a candidate for an unknown system, not the elucidation of its biological role.
