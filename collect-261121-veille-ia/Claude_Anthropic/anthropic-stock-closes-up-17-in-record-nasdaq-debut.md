Anthropic finished its first session as a listed company on Thursday, November 19, closing at $97.17 per share, 17% above its $83 offer price. Shares of the Claude developer now trade on Nasdaq as ANT, and the sale ranks as the largest IPO on record.

Trading opened at $111.50, about 34% above the offer, then climbed to an intraday peak of $114.99 that briefly lifted the company’s valuation past $2 trillion. Gains faded through the afternoon. Measured at the closing price, Anthropic’s fully diluted valuation came to $1.76 trillion, compared with roughly $1.5 trillion at the $83 offer.

Altogether, Anthropic sold 980 million Class A shares and pulled in $81.3 billion, surpassing the $75 billion SpaceX collected at its June listing. Early marketing pegged the stock at $70 to $78, and the company lifted the price once investor demand proved strong.

Just 7.4% of Anthropic’s shares are free to trade publicly, by Renaissance Capital’s estimate. Counting Class A stock held by employees outside the founding group pushes that figure to as much as 9.8%.

Separately, AMD, Broadcom, Google, Microsoft and Nvidia are set to put another $26 billion into the company, according to Anthropic’s earlier statements. That private money would bring total fundraising to roughly $107 billion.

Each of the seven co-founders now holds a stake worth around $20 billion on paper, and the group has pledged 80% of its Anthropic equity to philanthropy. Roughly 5,100 of the company’s 5,300 employees hold equity valued above $1 million, based on Hill.com data.

Dario Amodei, the chief executive, and Daniela Amodei, his sister and the company’s president, celebrated the debut at Nasdaq’s MarketSite in New York. Speaking there, the CEO said the aim is to “make the whole industry better,” whether or not Anthropic happens to be ahead at a given moment. He described the arrival of machine intelligence as a turning point in human history.

Dan Ives, a partner at Yorkville Ives & Co., called the offering an “A-plus success” and read it as investors backing the technology sector.

Jay Ritter, who studies IPOs at the University of Florida’s Warrington College of Business, wrote that the price swings came as no surprise given sharply divided views on how profitable Anthropic will become. Matthew Kennedy, a strategist with Renaissance Capital, had forecast a $100 share price by day’s end, a level the stock fell short of by $2.83.

One analyst joked that Anthropic might build fully autonomous AI that wipes out humanity, an outcome that could seriously dent earnings. The quip pointed to the pull between the company’s safety-first mission and the expectations of its new shareholders.

Wall Street’s main indexes sank on the same day. By late afternoon trading, the Nasdaq had shed 550 points (2.3%), the S&P 500 had dropped 2% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down over 1,100 points (2.2%). Investors sold on company-specific headlines and persistent worries about spending on AI data centers.

Sam Grelck, who analyzes equity strategy for Truist Advisory Services, cautioned against reading the IPO as a judgment on the AI sector. In his view, the reception offers “another indication of investors’ appetite for the AI theme” following a rough stretch for tech stocks. Jensen Huang’s abrupt departure from Nvidia the previous week had already unsettled investors.

Exposure to the listing extends past direct buyers, since AI stocks carry heavy weight in the market and can reach retirement savings such as 401(k) plans.

No other frontier AI lab had gone public before. Past mega-listings carry a warning, too: a Truist review of the 32 biggest IPOs in history found that over half saw shares drop by 50% or more at one point within a year of listing.
