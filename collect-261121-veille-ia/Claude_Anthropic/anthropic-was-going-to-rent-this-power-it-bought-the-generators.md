*Anthropic paid a reported $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of gas generators it first planned to buy electricity from. The reason sits in its credit file, where an 8.5% yield meets $131.7 billion of cash.*

Anthropic bought 1.7 gigawatts of Caterpillar gas generators from OnePWR Solutions for a reported $3.4 billion instead of renting their output, because its cash is cheaper than its credit. The deal alone equals 5.4 times all the capital spending Anthropic reported from January 2024 through June 2026.

Picture a power developer in San Antonio holding something half of corporate America would fight for: a two-year-old place in Caterpillar’s order book. The engines are coming. The engineers who know how to install them are on payroll. The customer is lined up. The only thing missing is the money to close the purchase.

Then the customer walks back in. Not to sign the 20-year power contract. To buy the whole order.

That is roughly what happened in September, and every headline since has filed it under “AI labs are becoming energy companies.” That is true and also the least interesting thing about it. The deal is a lesson in what a credit rating is worth when you have a mountain of cash and nobody wants to lend against your signature at a good price.

## What Happened

Anthropic agreed to pay about $3.4 billion for commitments covering 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty gas-fired generators, the Financial Times reported on Sunday, in a story carried by CNBC and Reuters. The seller was OnePWR Solutions, an equipment and power developer based in San Antonio, and the agreement was struck in late September.

The details that matter:

- **What Anthropic bought:** the generator commitments plus the engineering and construction capability needed to install them, according to the FT’s source.
- **What it did not buy:** OnePWR itself. No employees moved over.
- **The equipment:** Caterpillar generators that OnePWR had ordered about two years ago but needed financing to complete.
- **The pivot:** Anthropic originally planned to be OnePWR’s customer and buy the electricity. It took over the equipment orders instead.

The FT called it the largest known single purchase of power-generation equipment by an AI lab. Neither company commented publicly.

There is one document the coverage skipped. Piper Sandler, which advised OnePWR, lists the transaction on its website as a $3.4 billion sell-side deal: the sale of OnePWR’s “power generation platform to a frontier AI lab.” The bank booked it as M&A. The buyer took the platform and left the company behind.

## The Backstory

OnePWR’s business model was built for exactly the customer that ended up buying it out.

In February, the company announced it had assembled more than 1.2 gigawatts of generation equipment: 400 megawatts in inventory and ready to deploy, and 800 megawatts secured for delivery over the following 24 months. The pitch was simple. OnePWR develops, owns and operates on-site power plants, sells the electricity under long-term power purchase agreements, and does the engineering, procurement and construction itself. Customers get firm start dates in 2026 and 2027 without spending capital up front. CEO David Coker summed up the sales line: “Access to equipment alone doesn’t solve today’s power constraints.”

A month later, Caterpillar, OnePWR and carbon-storage company Vero3 announced a collaboration for data center power. Caterpillar would supply gas and diesel generators, turbines and controls. OnePWR would build, own and operate the plants and sell continuous power under long-term contracts, starting with a 500-megawatt prime power project planned for 2026.

So OnePWR’s product was not electricity. It was the ability to skip a queue without writing a check. The queue is real: Caterpillar management said in September that lead times for gas engines used for prime power stretch into the back half of 2028 and into 2029. BMA has covered why the equipment makers would rather ration that queue than build their way out of it, and why reciprocating engines became the fallback once turbines sold out.

Anthropic, meanwhile, had made a promise that pointed straight at a deal like this. In February it pledged to cover the grid upgrade costs of its data centers and to bring new generation online rather than contract for existing capacity, a commitment we covered when Microsoft and Anthropic volunteered to pay more for AI power.

And Anthropic is, by its own financial statements, a renter. Its business model runs on capacity owned by Amazon, Google, Microsoft, SpaceX and a stack of financing vehicles. The S-1 it filed on October 26 shows capital expenditures of $15 million in 2024, $131 million in 2025 and $482 million in the first half of 2026. The amended filing on November 2 shows $895.4 billion of compute and infrastructure commitments, with $69.8 billion due in the 12 months after September 30.

A company that rents nearly everything just bought 1.7 gigawatts of steel. That is the part worth explaining.

## Why Owning Beat Renting

Start with how the deal Anthropic walked away from would have worked.

In a power purchase agreement, the developer buys the generators, borrows against them, and earns its money back through a per-megawatt-hour price paid over 15 or 20 years. The lender is not really underwriting the engines. It is underwriting the customer’s promise to pay. The offtaker’s credit is the collateral.

Here the offtaker was Anthropic, and Anthropic’s credit is the weakest part of its finances.

- KBRA rated Anthropic BB+ in June, the top of junk and one notch below investment grade, according to documents reported by the FT.
- In the Apollo-led chip financing that followed, about $4.4 billion of junior debt exposed to Anthropic’s own credit priced at roughly 8.5%. The senior debt, guaranteed by Broadcom, priced at 5.75%.
- On October 30, the FT reported that Blackstone told investors Anthropic would likely land a BB-range rating from the big agencies after its IPO, reversing earlier guidance that investment grade was within reach.

That 275-basis-point gap is the market’s price for one question: whose name is on the guarantee? For chips, Anthropic solved it by borrowing a better rating. Broadcom stands behind the senior notes, a structure we took apart in how chip financing gets guaranteed. For data centers and cloud, the landlord is Amazon, Google or Microsoft, whose balance sheets do the borrowing.

For OnePWR’s generators, nobody with a better rating was in the room. The developer needed financing. Any lender would have priced the plant off Anthropic’s signature and OnePWR’s thinner balance sheet.

Anthropic, on the other side of the table, had $131.7 billion of cash and short-term investments at June 30, raised mostly through preferred stock, SAFEs and convertible notes rather than bank debt. That pile earned $762 million of interest in the second quarter alone.

Run an illustration, because the actual terms are not public. Financing $3.4 billion at the 8.5% the market charged for Anthropic-exposed paper costs about $289 million a year in interest, before the developer takes any equity return. Under a power contract, that cost comes back to Anthropic inside the electricity price for two decades. Paying from cash swaps it for the interest the cash would have earned, which is a lower number for a company holding more than $130 billion.

So the deal is less a pivot into energy than a capital-structure trade: Anthropic used the asset it has in abundance, cash, to replace the one it lacks, an investment-grade rating.

The scale against Anthropic’s own history is what makes it stand out.

Add up every dollar of capex in the S-1 and you get $628 million across 30 months. The generator deal is 5.4 times that. One September transaction outweighs Anthropic’s entire reported history of buying things it keeps.

The purchase also bought time, which Anthropic is already paying for in cash. The amended S-1 said the third-quarter operating loss was “primarily driven by $5.0 billion of compute under short-term commitments.” That is capacity bought on short notice because the long-term kind was not ready. The $3.4 billion generator deal costs 68% of what Anthropic spent on short-term compute in that one quarter, and it comes with a delivery slot that a new Caterpillar order would not match until 2028 or later.

## The Business Model Angle

The price works out to about $2,000 per kilowatt, installation capability included. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang has put the cost of a gigawatt of AI data center at $50 billion to $60 billion. On that yardstick, Anthropic’s generators cost $2 billion per gigawatt, or roughly 3% to 4% of the total bill. Rough, because 1.7 gigawatts of generation supports less than 1.7 gigawatts of servers once cooling and reserve margins are counted. Against Anthropic’s $895.4 billion of commitments, the deal is 0.38%.

Three lessons for founders, operators and investors:

**Capital structure decides make-or-buy.** Rent where a partner’s balance sheet is cheaper than yours. Buy where yours is the best one in the room. Anthropic rents chips through Broadcom’s rating and cloud through Amazon’s and Google’s. For power, its own cash beat every alternative on offer, so it owned. Any company that is equity-rich and credit-poor, which describes most late-stage startups, should run this test asset by asset.

**The service middleman’s margin lives in the customer’s financing gap.** OnePWR’s modelmonetized buyers who could not or would not spend capital. The moment its best customer could fund the equipment more cheaply than OnePWR could, the developer’s 20-year spread became a one-time sale price. If you sell anything “as a service” to customers bigger than you, your moat is their cost of capital, and it can disappear on a single funding round.

**Own the cheapest input that gates the expensive ones.** Power is a few percent of a gigawatt’s cost and decides whether the other 96% earns anything. When a small line item sets the schedule for a large one, it stops being a procurement decision and becomes a balance-sheet decision.

## The Risk

The strongest case against the deal is that Anthropic bought equipment and not the people who run it.

- **No operators came with it.** The FT says the deal included engineering and construction capability for installation. Running 1.7 gigawatts of gas generators for years is a different job: maintenance, spare parts, outage response, fuel logistics. Under a power contract, availability was OnePWR’s problem. Now it is Anthropic’s.
- **Fuel and price risk moved too.** A power purchase agreement can lock a per-megawatt-hour price. An owner buys natural gas at market and eats the swings.
- **Equipment is not electricity.** Sites, gas supply, air permits and in-service dates are all undisclosed. Generators still need all four. SpaceX disclosed a roughly $2 billion purchase of mobile gas turbines for xAI in its own IPO filing, and xAI’s turbine fleet near Memphis has already drawn litigation over air permits.
- **Cash is finite against the commitments.** Anthropic held about $131.9 billion of cash and short-term investments at September 30 against $69.8 billion of commitments due over the next year. Every dollar in steel is a dollar not available for compute, and a company whose credit rating may stay in junk territory needs its cash more, not less.
- **The credit explanation may be only half the story.** OnePWR needed financing and had a slot that could have been lost. Anthropic may simply have bought speed and control, and the rating gap made the decision easier rather than making it.

There is also a fair bull case on the other side: reciprocating generators are modular and resellable, the deal is a rounding error against the commitments, and if the grid arrives early the units still work as backup power.

## Quick Questions

**Did Anthropic buy OnePWR?** No. It bought the generator commitments and the installation capability. OnePWR’s employees stayed put, and Piper Sandler describes the transaction as the sale of OnePWR’s power generation platform.

**How much did Anthropic pay per kilowatt?** About $2,000, based on $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts. That figure includes engineering and construction capability, so it is not a pure equipment price.

**Why didn’t Anthropic just sign a power contract?** It planned to. A power contract would have required OnePWR to finance the generators against Anthropic’s promise to pay, and the market prices that promise at junk-grade rates. Anthropic had the cash to skip the lender.

**Does this change the Anthropic IPO?** Not the headline numbers. The roadshow was reported to start the week of November 9, though Reuters reported investors doubt it can still list in November. The deal does add a large owned asset that the S-1’s small capex history does not reflect, so watch the next amendment.

## The Business Model Analyst Take

Anthropic did not become a power company in September. It ran the same calculation it runs everywhere else and got a different answer.

For chips, the cheapest money in the room belonged to Broadcom. For data centers, it belonged to Amazon and Google. For 1.7 gigawatts of Caterpillar generators, it belonged to Anthropic, because a company sitting on $131.7 billion of cash does not need to pay 8.5% to someone else for the privilege of renting.

The real tell will come in the next few filings. If the generators show up as property and equipment, and more slot purchases follow, the most asset-light frontier lab is quietly becoming an owner of the one bottleneck its partners cannot finance for it.
