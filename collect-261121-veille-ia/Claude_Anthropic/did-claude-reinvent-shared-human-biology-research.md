# Did Claude Reinvent Shared Human Biology Research?

A Danish biologist says he shared the same viral enzymes with Anthropic's assistant long before its reported discovery.

## In a nutshell

When Anthropic claimed its Claude AI independently uncovered an unusual viral enzyme system, Danish researcher Mario Rodríguez Mestre challenged the breakthrough, revealing he had previously studied the same setup and pasted his unpublished work directly into the chatbot. While patent records verify Mestre's early work and Anthropic strongly denies using user conversations, public records cannot yet prove whether the software made an unassisted rediscovery or drew on human prompts.

## Highlights

- Anthropic announced roughly 950 automated AI agents found the enzyme system in about 21 hours of searching.
- Mario Rodríguez Mestre held an earlier patent on related enzymes and documented his extensive use of Claude to review unpublished drafts.
- Anthropic explicitly denied that user conversations were used to train Claude or were accessible to its biology team.
- When Anthropic repeated the automated genomic search ten times, the AI failed to spot the key repeated DNA structure.

## From the Editor’s Diary

True discovery requires more than spotting patterns in data; until companies allow independent audits of how their models learn, claims of automated scientific breakthroughs will always share the stage with the human work that came before them.

## In short

This dispute matters because it tests whether artificial intelligence can truly make original scientific discoveries on its own, or whether it simply reflects back the unpublished ideas researchers feed into it. It also tests how well technology companies protect the private work scientists share with their tools.

The most likely next step is an independent review of both sides' private records, comparing the researcher's saved transcripts with the prompts and data Anthropic gave its automated systems.

That outcome remains uncertain because neither side has agreed to an outside audit, and neither claim has been proven in public.

## How it unfolded

## The trail begins with human biologists

Long before software joined the search, human scientists laid the groundwork. In September 2021, researchers at Texas A&M University published a study showing that large viruses that prey on bacteria carry an enzyme known as a reverse transcriptase, which builds DNA from RNA templates. They did not map the full molecular machine Anthropic later described, but their paper established that the underlying family of enzymes was already known to science. The public argument over who discovered the broader system flared five years later, after Anthropic announced its finding and news reports revealed that a university biologist claimed he had already mapped it.

## Years of groundwork in plain sight

Over the next two years, the biological pieces steadily accumulated in public and private records. Chinese researchers published gene expression data from bacteria-infecting viruses in 2022, creating a public record that both Anthropic and Mestre would later use. Around the same time, Mestre began investigating the same viral enzymes, calling them jumbotrons. A United States patent granted to biotech firm ReNAgade Therapeutics in early 2024 listed Mestre as an inventor on related enzyme technology, confirming his early role in the field. But the patent did not describe the exact three-part system, leaving it unclear whether his team had fully characterized the setup before Anthropic did.

## An automated search claims a breakthrough

In September 2026, Anthropic revealed that it had opened a physical wet lab in California, stepping beyond digital code and into hands-on biology. Five days later, the company announced that roughly 950 automated Claude programs had combed through public genetic catalogs for 21 hours. The AI had flagged an enzyme sitting right next to repeated patterns of genetic code, an arrangement Anthropic named ART. Company researchers suggested the structure resembled CRISPR, the celebrated bacterial defense mechanism adapted by humans to edit genes. But Anthropic had not tested the system in living cells or proven it could edit anything, leading news reports to emphasize that Claude found ART’s architecture, not a brand-new enzyme.

## A scientist spots his own unpublished work

The triumph was quickly interrupted. Within days, The New York Times reported that Mestre believed Anthropic's discovery matched his own jumbotron research. Over the preceding year, Mestre had pasted unpublished dissertation drafts, charts and data into Claude to help analyze them. Reporters reviewed his chat logs, and colleague Seth Shipman confirmed their long discussions about the enzymes. Anthropic issued a flat denial, stating that customer conversations never train Claude and were off-limits to its biology staff. Outside experts noted that while Mestre clearly had early human insight, neither side had yet shown whether ART resembles CRISPR in architecture, not yet in function.

## Looking for proof in the prompts

By October, both sides faced the challenge of proving their claims. In television interviews, Mestre acknowledged that he had no hard evidence showing Anthropic took his findings, suggesting instead that company scientists might have guided the AI more heavily than their public announcements implied. A subsequent scientific analysis noted that when Anthropic repeated its automated search ten times, the software failed to spot the key DNA patterns again. On October 11, Anthropic released a detailed paper describing how Claude sorted through 1.9 billion protein clusters, but the paper had not been reviewed by outside scientists and lacked an independent audit of its data pipelines.

## Stock market realities soften the claims

The dispute entered a commercial phase when Anthropic filed paperwork to sell shares to the public on the stock market. In its legal disclosures to regulators, the company struck a far more cautious tone about its laboratory ventures. It warned prospective investors that physical biology experiments are costly, heavily regulated, and may never yield profitable medical treatments. Commentators quickly contrasted those sober financial warnings with the sweeping claims made during the initial discovery announcement. The filing offered legal realism rather than scientific clarity, leaving the core question of who found the enzyme system first completely unresolved.

## Where things stand

As of late October 2026, no independent evidence shows that Anthropic took or used Mestre's private research, and Anthropic maintains that its systems never trained on user conversations. At the same time, Mestre's earlier work is backed by a granted patent and verified records of his Claude chats, proving human researchers were tracking the system first.

Settling the question would require an independent audit of Anthropic's internal training logs alongside dated drafts from Mestre's laboratory. Biologists must also run living cell experiments to see whether the enzyme system can actually perform useful work.
