Before discussing what Anthropic has disclosed so far, it's worth considering: What is an S-1?
DEFINITION
An S-1 is the official document a company must file with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) before it can sell shares to the public for the first time via IPO. It works much like a legally required prospectus.
The UK equivalent is the prospectus approved by the FCA.
The S-1 explains in detail what the company does, how it makes (or intends to make) money and includes audited financial statements akin to those required to be published by public companies.
Management often likes to file an S-1 confidentially first so that the SEC can review the proposal without the company having to publish its finances, which only becomes a requirement when it goes public.
NOTE: Anthropic’s first public S-1, filed on 26 October 2026, did not include a share count or price range. The amended S-1 filed on 10 November 2026 sets out 825 million Class A shares at an estimated $70 to $78 each.

How does the Anthropic IPO allocation process work?
The general idea is that a company, in this case Anthropic, will decide (with the help of the investment banks it has hired) what they believe the valuation of the company is, what percentage of the ownership of the company it will sell, and from there, how many shares to issue, and at what price.
DEFINITION
IPO share allocation is how the underwriting banks decide how many shares each investor gets from those on offer.
Most shares in an IPO usually go to institutional investors, such as fund managers and pension funds, and most retail investors buy at the opening market price, not the offer price (see Trading 212’s SpaceX IPO guide).Anthropic expects up to 20% of the offering to be allocated to retail investors, according to reports on its amended S-1. The rest, at least 80%, is expected to go to institutional investors.As a point of reference, SpaceX’s IPO had a reported 70:30 institutional to retail split, with only about 5% of the company’s shares sold in the IPO. That’s an unusually high allocation to retail investors.Company insiders and early investors are usually subject to a “lock-up period”, which means they can’t sell their shares for a set time after the IPO, typically 90 to 180 days. Is Anthropic profitable? Revenue and losses from its S-1
No. According to its S-1, Anthropic is not profitable. Its amended S-1 shows revenue of $33.2bn for the first nine months of 2026, including $17.0bn in the third quarter, and a net loss of $104.0bn over the same period.
Most of that loss doesn’t come from running the business. About $94.3bn of it is an accounting charge for the rising value of convertible notes and similar financing instruments, which grew as Anthropic’s valuation increased. It’s a non-cash charge, meaning no money left the company. Looking at operations alone, Anthropic made an operating loss of about $1.3bn in the first nine months of 2026. That includes operating income of about $1.0bn in the second quarter and an operating loss of about $536m in the third quarter.
The S-1 also confirms Anthropic’s full-year figures for 2025: revenue of about $4.6bn, an operating loss of about $8.1bn and a net loss of about $42.0bn, of which $34.3bn was the same type of non-cash charge. Its biggest cost is computing power and infrastructure, which came to about $7.3bn in 2025 and $18.8bn in the first nine months of 2026.
How does Anthropic make money? Claude subscriptions, enterprise contracts, and API usage
Although there are likely bigger plans down the road, Anthropic makes money via its chatbot, Claude.
Claude subscriptions for individuals currently split into three categories (Free, Pro, Max) plus Team and Enterprise plans.
Claude API is used by developers and businesses, which pay per token used instead of a subscription because of the large amount of computing power used up on an enterprise level and for large projects.
- Cloud marketplaces: Claude is sold through Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud and Microsoft.
- Claude Code: reported run rate of about $1bn within roughly six months of launch (2025).
- Business customers made up about 80% of revenue in late 2025 (Reuters); more than 1,900 customers paying over $1m a year as of 30 September 2026, according to its S-1.
Anthropic’s revenue model: usage-based pricing vs subscriptions
For everyday use cases, a Claude subscription is going to be enough to give most people what they want and a typical subscriber costs Anthropic well under what they pay. At some high levels of usage, a subscription might be seen as a "loss-leader", but it’s likely subscription prices will rise over time alongside adoption to cover such instances.
The big money is made via the API customers who get billed per million tokens, but this revenue stream is less stable since it will rise and fall with usage, which might get cut when companies are cost-cutting or folding specific development projects.
From an investor’s perspective, subscriptions provide more predictable revenue via fixed fees, while pay-per-use is more profitable but more variable and subject to how compute costs affect margins.

How is Anthropic’s IPO valuation calculated?
First principles say that market value = share price x total shares outstanding.
The bankers working on the deal will put in the long hours to weigh comparable companies, revenue multiples, growth and demand from the bookbuild. Bookbuild is the process in which underwriting banks gather orders from institutional investors during the roadshow, showing how many shares they want and at what price, and use that demand to set the final IPO price. Roadshow is the marketing period before an IPO is priced, usually one to two weeks long. The company’s management and its underwriting banks present the business to institutional investors through meetings and presentations, while the banks collect orders for the bookbuild.
A company’s valuation can be quoted in two ways when it raises money. The pre-money valuation is what the business is judged to be worth before the new cash arrives. The post-money valuation is that figure plus the new investment.
