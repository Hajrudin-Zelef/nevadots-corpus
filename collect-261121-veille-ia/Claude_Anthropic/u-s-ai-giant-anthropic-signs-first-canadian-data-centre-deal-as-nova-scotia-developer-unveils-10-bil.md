An industrial site in rural Nova Scotia could soon become home to one of Canada’s most ambitious artificial intelligence infrastructure projects, following a major agreement between a U.S. technology company and a Canadian energy developer.

On October 16, 2026, Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI assistant, confirmed its first Canadian data-centre lease with EverWind at Point Tupper, Nova Scotia. The agreement coincides with EverWind’s announcement of plans for more than C$10 billion in private investment covering a data-centre campus and renewable energy development.

The proposal could create thousands of construction opportunities, hundreds of permanent jobs and substantial economic activity in Cape Breton.

However, its success will depend on construction timelines, financing, regulatory approvals and promises to protect electricity customers and local water resources.

## Anthropic Signs Its First Canadian Data-Centre Agreement

U.S. AI Giant Anthropic Signs First Canadian Data-Centre Deal as Nova Scotia Developer Unveils $10 Billion Plan

American artificial intelligence company Anthropic has taken a significant step into Canada’s physical technology infrastructure market. On October 16, the developer of the Claude AI assistant confirmed that it had signed a long-term lease with EverWind for data-centre capacity in Point Tupper, Nova Scotia. The agreement marks Anthropic’s first data-centre lease in Canada and represents an important expansion of its North American computing infrastructure. EverWind disclosed that the initial agreement runs for 20 years, providing a long-term commercial commitment behind the proposed development.

Jacob Glick, Anthropic’s head of public policy for Canada, emphasized the importance of bringing computing infrastructure closer to Canadian users of Claude. He also said the company intends to meet national and provincial expectations concerning environmental responsibility, electricity costs and community benefits. However, the agreement does not mean Anthropic has committed to financing EverWind’s entire development. The companies have not publicly disclosed the lease’s financial value, and the arrangement initially covers the project’s first phase rather than its entire planned capacity.

## EverWind Unveils More Than $10 Billion in Planned Investment

The announcement is particularly significant because of the scale of investment EverWind is proposing. The Nova Scotia-based energy infrastructure company outlined plans for more than C$10 billion in privately financed developments, describing the combined portfolio as the largest private investment in provincial history. Approximately C$8 billion would be allocated to developing, constructing, owning and financing the Point Tupper data-centre campus. Another C$2 billion would support new onshore wind generation projects across Nova Scotia, linking the technology development with an expansion of the province’s renewable electricity infrastructure.

EverWind founder and CEO Trent Vichie presented the investment as an opportunity to connect Nova Scotia’s established industrial capabilities with the growing demand for digital infrastructure. The company already owns and operates industrial facilities at Point Tupper and has been pursuing renewable energy and green hydrogen developments in the region. Importantly, the C$10-billion figure represents the announced development plan, not money already spent or a confirmed direct investment by Anthropic. The ultimate amount deployed will depend on construction, financing arrangements and the successful completion of the proposed projects.

## The Data Centre Could Reach 420 Megawatts by 2028

EverWind intends to develop the project in stages rather than constructing its entire proposed capacity simultaneously. The first phase would include approximately 18,600 square metres of data-centre space and require 95 megawatts of electricity. The company is targeting late 2027 for initial operations. A second expansion, planned for 2028, would add another 325 megawatts of capacity. If both phases proceed as proposed, the finished campus would reach 420 megawatts, making it a substantial new source of demand within Nova Scotia’s electricity system.

The phased approach matters because data centres require specialized infrastructure beyond buildings and computer servers. They need reliable high-capacity electricity connections, sophisticated cooling equipment and backup systems that can keep operations running during interruptions. Increasing capacity from 95 to 420 megawatts would require significant coordination among developers, utilities and regulators. EverWind has acknowledged that the expansion remains subject to commercial, technical and regulatory conditions. The announced operating dates are therefore development targets rather than guaranteed completion deadlines, and no part of the proposed facility was operating when the deal was announced.

## The Project Could Create 2,000 Construction Jobs and 215 Permanent Positions

Employment is one of the biggest selling points of EverWind’s announcement. The company estimates that the combined data-centre and wind developments could support approximately 2,000 construction positions during the phased build-out, followed by 215 permanent operating positions. According to figures provided by company officials to The Canadian Press, permanent data-centre jobs are expected to pay between C$135,000 and C$185,000 annually. For communities in the Strait of Canso region, the prospect of specialized employment could create opportunities for residents who might otherwise consider relocating to larger cities.

The projected economic benefits extend beyond direct employment. Based on estimates prepared by consulting firm Davis Pier, EverWind expects the completed data-centre development to generate approximately C$2 billion in provincial GDP during construction and C$137 million annually once fully operational. Provincial corporate income taxes are projected to reach approximately C$1.5 billion over the initial 20-year period. These estimates depend on successful project completion and future commercial performance. They should not be interpreted as guaranteed jobs, tax collections or economic activity already realized.

## Four Wind Projects Will Help Power the New AI Infrastructure

Electricity generation forms a substantial part of EverWind’s strategy. The company plans to direct approximately C$2 billion toward four existing renewable energy developments: Bear Lake, Windy Ridge, Kmtnuk and Upper Afton. Together, these projects form part of an onshore wind portfolio exceeding 650 megawatts of planned generating capacity. EverWind previously secured a US$175-million strategic investment from Nuveen Energy Infrastructure Credit in March 2026 to advance its renewable energy operations and associated developments. The new AI project could provide another major source of long-term demand for electricity generated in Nova Scotia.

However, the electricity supply arrangements will require careful coordination. EverWind officials acknowledged that the planned wind farms are not expected to be operational when the initial data-centre phase opens in late 2027. They said a study conducted by the province’s independent electricity system operator indicated that existing infrastructure could support the first phase. The wind portfolio is targeting commercial operations in 2028, although construction and grid integration remain important milestones. EverWind also intends to continue pursuing its previously announced green hydrogen and ammonia developments at Point Tupper rather than replacing those plans with the data centre.

## EverWind Promises the Project Will Not Increase Household Electricity Bills

The enormous electricity requirements of AI data centres have created concerns about whether surrounding communities might face higher utility costs. EverWind has responded by promising to cover the electricity infrastructure expenses directly attributable to the Point Tupper project, including grid connections, network upgrades and other required improvements. The company says these expenses will not be passed along to existing Nova Scotia electricity customers. It also intends to incorporate several hundred megawatt-hours of battery storage to help manage demand and reduce pressure on the provincial grid.

Another proposed safeguard would allow the facility to reduce or temporarily stop drawing electricity from the provincial network when necessary to protect system reliability. That capability could become particularly important during periods of high demand or supply constraints. However, the measures remain commitments rather than demonstrated operating results. EverWind has confirmed that detailed electricity-system assessments are continuing, including reviews of reliability, infrastructure expenses and impacts on other customers. The planned expansion cannot connect to the grid until the required technical studies and regulatory approvals are completed. For households already concerned about electricity affordability, independent verification will be essential.

## Water Consumption Emerges as a Major Concern for Nearby Communities

Cooling hundreds of megawatts of computing equipment requires careful water management, particularly in a region where industrial facilities and residents rely on shared resources. EverWind says its data centre will use a closed-loop cooling system that circulates a water-glycol mixture through sealed equipment, with air providing most of the cooling. Under current design assumptions, the first 95-megawatt phase would require approximately 100,000 litres of water daily. The full 420-megawatt campus is projected to use an average of approximately 500,000 litres per day.

The water would be sourced from the Landrie Lake system, which also supplies raw water for Port Hawkesbury’s drinking-water operations. EverWind cites an estimated daily sustainable yield of approximately 34 million litres for the lake, arguing that its planned cooling requirements represent a relatively small share. However, municipal officials have emphasized that existing users and other proposed industrial developments must also be considered. Before the announcement, Port Hawkesbury residents raised questions about the lake’s available capacity. Richmond County officials have also stressed that water supply commitments must be supported by proper assessment. A closed-loop design reduces routine discharge but does not eliminate the need for freshwater.

## Indigenous Partnerships and Community Benefits Are Central to the Proposal

EverWind is also pursuing Indigenous ownership and community participation as part of the development. A consortium of First Nations led by Membertou First Nation already holds a 51% ownership interest in the company’s first-phase wind portfolio. Separately, negotiations are underway for Membertou Development Corporation and its partners to acquire a proposed 9% equity stake in the data-centre project. That potential investment has not been finalized. Membertou Chief Terry Paul has welcomed the development while emphasizing the importance of responsible resource management and long-term opportunities for Indigenous communities.

Beyond equity participation, EverWind intends to establish a community development program worth tens of millions of dollars during the project’s initial phase. Potential priorities include housing initiatives, recreation facilities, healthcare, workforce training and local infrastructure. The company also projects more than C$400 million in municipal tax revenues over the respective lives of the data-centre and wind developments. Richmond County officials have welcomed the possibilities but emphasized that local residents must be consulted. Community engagement sessions are expected to begin in late October or early November, providing opportunities to examine the proposal more closely.

## Nova Scotia’s Government Faces Pressure to Establish Stronger Safeguards

The announcement arrives after a noticeable change in Nova Scotia’s political approach to data-centre development. In June 2026, Premier Tim Houston said his government was not actively pursuing large data-centre projects, partly because the province needed additional electricity generation capacity. By September, Houston had become more receptive, explaining that Canadian computing infrastructure was increasingly important to economic development and national security. However, he maintained that developers would need to protect electricity customers, water resources and host communities before receiving support.

The federal government has also introduced new expectations for large computing facilities. On September 3, Ottawa launched Canada’s Responsible Data Centre Development Principles, outlining requirements for local economic benefits, electricity affordability, environmental protection, transparency and strategic value. Anthropic supported the framework at its launch, while EverWind joined as a signatory on October 16. The principles do not replace provincial or municipal permitting requirements. Nova Scotia NDP Leader Claudia Chender has questioned whether government assurances are sufficient without stronger legislation or regulation. Her concerns underline the need to turn corporate promises into independently verifiable obligations before major development proceeds.

## Anthropic’s Canadian Expansion Highlights Both Opportunity and Unanswered Questions

The Nova Scotia agreement builds on Anthropic’s growing interest in Canada’s technology sector. In July 2026, the company committed C$10 million to Canadian artificial intelligence research, establishing partnerships with organizations including the Vector Institute in Toronto, Mila in Montreal and the Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute in Edmonton. It also announced support for research at universities and healthcare institutions. Anthropic’s own usage research found that Canadian adoption of Claude was disproportionately high relative to the country’s population, reinforcing the commercial appeal of locating additional computing infrastructure in Canada.

The Point Tupper lease represents a significant next step, but major questions remain. EverWind has indicated that it will use its own capital initially and pursue additional financing from banks and pension funds. The proposed expansion must still satisfy regulatory, environmental and technical conditions. Furthermore, building computing capacity in Canada does not automatically guarantee Canadian ownership, control of data or access for domestic researchers. Those outcomes depend on contractual arrangements and applicable protections. If the project advances as planned, it could strengthen Nova Scotia’s position in the emerging AI economy. Its long-term success, however, will depend on whether the promised economic benefits materialize without imposing unacceptable costs on surrounding communities.
