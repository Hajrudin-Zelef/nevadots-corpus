Research Note In Two MinutesNote synopsis · AI drafted · Analyst reviewed
Drafted by AI from the full research note and reviewed by a Greyhound Research analyst before publication. The analysis and conclusions are Greyhound Research’s own.
At Microsoft Ignite on November 17, 2026, Microsoft set out three confidential computing items. Confidential computing keeps data sealed while a computer works on it, so the cloud operator cannot read it. VR72, the first, is built to stretch that seal across a rack of GPUs, the chips that run large AI models. A second puts a smaller sealed space inside a virtual machine, alone or in containers on Microsoft’s Kubernetes service. The third is a clean room where several companies can analyze pooled data without seeing each other’s records. Microsoft’s summary calls all three previews. Its detailed post the same day is more exact. VR72 was a technology showcase, and the smaller sealed spaces are planned for a future release, so only the clean room, first previewed in June, is in preview. None of the three has a published price, region list, or general availability date.
Greyhound Research’s position is that Microsoft has shown how much bigger the sealed room can be, and the harder question is now who sits inside it. The seal keeps the operator out. It does not keep the customer’s own agent out, because the agent is the one reading the data. Buyers should judge these offerings by their proof, known as attestation, more than by their encryption. Greyhound Research reads this as a familiar stage for a security feature. Sealed computing is moving from a premium option toward a standard expectation, and as that happens buyers tend to stop asking whether it is present and start asking how its evidence is checked. The more exact status makes that advice firmer. There is less to sign today and more time to set terms.
Three findings carry the argument. First, Microsoft has not said whether the agent memory and cached context its own services keep sit inside the seal. Second, researchers have forged the hardware’s proof in the lab. Microsoft’s Ignite demonstration checked that proof with a local verifier, while Google already offers a verifier it does not own. Third, the clean room’s real risk is what an approved question may return, so it leaks by answer rather than by breach.
The note sets out a ten-move Greyhound CXO Runbook, with seven exhibits, six tables, and six questions for Microsoft. None of the moves waits on Microsoft. The first three are simple to state. Map which agent data sits inside the seal. Name who verifies the proof, and how often. Write the clean room’s allowed answers into the agreement before any data goes in.
The evidence has limits. This note first read the status from Microsoft’s summary and now follows Microsoft’s more exact detailed post, read on November 19. The 2025 attacks needed hands on the server, which large data centers make much harder. Greyhound Research names the signals that would change its view within twelve months. Until they arrive, the advice holds: pilot on what is generally available, and commit regulated data only when the proof, its checker, and the agent’s memory are documented.

Part 1What Ignite Changed
The Seal And The Reader: What Confidential Computing Keeps Out
Greyhound SpotlightConfidential computing seals the envelope against the courier. An AI agent working inside the envelope is the reader.
Confidential computing protects data while a computer is using it. The chip keeps a sealed space, called a trusted execution environment, that even the cloud operator cannot look into. That answers the question risk committees ask most about public cloud: can the provider see our data? It does not answer a newer question. What does our own agent do with the data it is allowed to see?
This changes what the old cloud objection is about. For more than a decade, boards asked of public cloud whether the provider could look, and contracts and audits grew up around that question. Confidential computing gives it a technical answer, which is real progress. It also moves the remaining risk somewhere less familiar. Once the provider is shut out, the main party left with access is software the buyer chose and switched on. Greyhound Research expects many risk registers to mark the provider risk as reduced and leave the agent risk unowned. That reallocation is easy to miss, because a control that answers one question well tends to be credited with answering the next one too.
Exhibit 1 sets the parties who might read sensitive data against the three Ignite items. A tenant is another customer sharing the same hardware. The seal keeps out everyone except the parties the customer lets in, and the agent is let in by design.
Every offering keeps the operator out, and the clean room also keeps the other collaborators out. None of them keeps the customer’s own agent out, because the agent is the workload being protected. The exhibit is Greyhound Research’s reading, not a vendor’s claim. It shows that the seal settles one question: who else can look. What the agent may do stays with the controls the buyer already owns.
Greyhound Standpoint:
Greyhound Research’s view is that confidential computing answers the provider question well and leaves the agent question where it was. The seal decides who else can look. It does not decide what the workload inside may do, and an agent is the workload. Whatever the agent is permitted to read, it reads in the clear, and whatever it is permitted to do, it can do from inside a fully sealed machine.
Practitioners have seen this shape before. When server virtualization arrived, isolation between machines held up well, and the problems that followed came largely from what administrators and applications were allowed to do inside them. Isolation and authorization are different controls, and each platform shift tempts buyers to let the newer one stand in for the older one. Microsoft deserves credit for showing how to take the seal to the largest models, and its own post says the seal does not replace permissions. The limit is one of design, not of quality, because the technology does what it was built to do. The view would change if Microsoft tied agent identity and permissions to the sealed boundary in its documentation.
Security leaders should pair every sealed workload with the agent controls it does not replace. Each agent needs its own identity, a written list of what it may read and call, and human approval before it acts on regulated data. Those terms belong in the pilot gate, not after it.

The mechanism. A trusted execution environment encrypts data in memory while it is being processed, and the hardware signs a statement, called attestation, that genuine chips are running the expected code. The clean room shares one sealed space between several parties, and Microsoft says it protects each participant’s raw data from the other collaborators and from cloud operators.

2Why is the agent inside by design?
The workload is the reader. An agent that answers a question must read the data the question concerns. The line that confidential computing does not replace application permissions can go straight into an internal design standard, so that no sealed workload is approved without its agent controls. It also signals that Microsoft expects the identity layer, not the seal, to carry an agent’s rights.
Part 2The Proof
Attestation: The Proof Matters More Than The Lock
Greyhound SpotlightAttestation proves what was running at one moment. An agent whose model, tools, or prompts change next week is no longer the thing that was proved.
Every large cloud now encrypts data while it is in use, so the lock no longer sets one provider apart. The proof does. Before a bank lets sensitive data or keys into a sealed space, something has to show that the space is genuine and running the right code. That proof is attestation, and auditors will lean on it.
What changes for buyers is the kind of evidence they hold. Encryption is a setting that is on or off, and an auditor can confirm it once. Attestation is a record, produced each time a check runs, and its worth depends on being kept, dated, and matched to the workload it describes. That turns confidential computing from a security purchase into an evidence discipline, closer to how banks record who approved each payment. Greyhound Research expects supervisors in regulated sectors to ask, in time, for the proof itself rather than for a statement that sealing was switched on. The cost few budgets show is the work of keeping and reading these records.
Exhibit 2 follows the proof through five steps, from the chip measuring what it runs to the proof being refreshed. Secure key release means the keys to the data are handed over only after the proof checks out.
Microsoft has written the first steps for its existing services. Its attestation service checks the proof, and its documents say keys can be released only after the check. The last step is the one an agent needs most. Agents change as models, tools, and prompts are updated. No Microsoft document read says how often the proof is refreshed, or whether an update forces a new check.
Greyhound Standpoint:
Greyhound Research holds that attestation, not encryption, is now the substance of confidential computing. All three large clouds encrypt data while it is in use, so encryption no longer separates one offer from another. What separates them is the proof: whether it covers the code that matters, who checks it, and whether it is renewed when the workload changes. A proof that cannot answer those three questions gives an auditor a signature without the context to rely on it.
The refresh question is where agents differ from earlier workloads. Security engineers have long known a class of flaw called time-of-check to time-of-use, in which a check passes and the thing checked then changes before it is used. An agent whose model, tools, or instructions are updated after start-up reproduces that flaw at the scale of a deployment. Microsoft has done careful work on the first steps, with an attestation service and documented release of keys only after a check. The documents read do not address refresh, which is not proof that it is missing. A published refresh policy would settle the point.
Chief information security officers should ask for the verifier and the refresh rule in writing before any pilot. A model or tool update should trigger a fresh check before an agent touches regulated data, and the proof should be retained as evidence for as long as the data it protected.

Part 2The Proof
When The Seal Was Opened: Three Lab Attacks In One Year
Greyhound SpotlightResearchers forged the proof that a sealed space is genuine with under USD 1,000 of equipment. They needed physical access to the server.
Three research teams opened the seal in 2025. Each placed a small device between the processor and its memory, and read or redirected what passed between them. One of them, TEE.Fail, extracted the keys that sign the proof and used them to forge it. None of the attacks has been reported in the wild, and each needed someone at the machine.
The attacks change the reading of the threat, not the case for the technology. Sealed computing was designed to keep out remote attackers and privileged insiders, and the vendors’ reply that physical attacks fall outside that design matches how they have long scoped it. What is new is how cheap the equipment has become, which moves physical access from a concern about well-funded attackers to one about anyone who can reach the server. Greyhound Research expects buyers to give more weight to where hardware sits, and to ask for site-level evidence alongside the proof. A quieter effect falls on sovereign and edge sites, where workloads moved for control can end up with weaker physical custody than a hyperscale hall.
Exhibit 3 places the three attacks on a 2025 calendar, from the first private warning to vendors to public release. An interposer is a device fitted between a chip and its memory to watch the traffic.
Battering RAM reached Intel and AMD in February, months before it went public, and both vendors answered that physical attacks fall outside what their products protect against. That answer is fair for a guarded data center and less reassuring for a colocation site, an edge cabinet, or a supplier’s rack. The exhibit records dates, not damage. Its lesson is that the proof needs a checker the buyer trusts, because the proof itself can be forged.
Greyhound Standpoint:
In Greyhound Research’s assessment, the 2025 attacks are a lesson about verification and physical custody, not a reason to step back from confidential computing. The mechanism explains why. Each attack placed a device between processor and memory, and the most serious extracted the keys that sign the proof. A forged proof does more harm than a broken seal, because it tells every check further along that all is well.
Hardware security has followed this arc before. Physical attacks on memory and chips have a long research history, and chip vendors have long drawn their threat models to exclude them, leaving physical protection to whoever runs the building. That is a reasonable division of labor in a guarded hyperscale data center, where access is controlled and logged, and none of these attacks has been reported in the wild. It is less reassuring for a colocation hall, an edge cabinet, or a partner’s rack, where the buyer often knows less about who can reach the hardware. The evidence is laboratory evidence and should be weighed as such. The view would ease if future chips protected the memory bus, and would firm if an exploitation outside the lab were reported.
Risk teams should add three questions to the next architecture review: where each sealed workload physically runs, who can reach that hardware, and how quickly the chosen verifier would stop accepting a signing key known to be compromised.

Greyhound Standpoint:

If those places sit outside the seal, the longest-lived copies of sensitive data are the least protected.

Part 3Agents Inside The Seal
The Clean Room: Leaks By Answer, Not By Breach
Greyhound SpotlightMicrosoft tells clean room users not to put personal or regulated data into the preview. The service is for testing only.
A clean room lets several companies pool data that none of them can see. Banks might check for fraud across accounts, or hospitals pool trial results. The seal protects the raw records. Answers still come out. If the agreed questions are loose, or an agent is allowed to ask open ones, the answers can reveal what the seal was meant to hide.
Clean rooms answer a long-standing commercial need. Banks, insurers, and hospitals have wanted to pool data for fraud, risk, and research for years, and legal teams have often slowed the effort over who could see what. Sealed hardware removes much of that objection, which is why Greyhound Research expects interest to rise once the service leaves preview. The harder work then moves to the agreement. Data-sharing terms were mostly written for people running known reports, and few anticipate software that composes its own questions. The effect that gets less attention is on liability. When an answer reveals too much, the parties will need to know whose approval let it out, and the agreement is where that is settled.
Exhibit 5 sets out the clean room’s controls in three groups: what Microsoft has written, what the parties must decide, and what is not yet stated. A post-filter drops results drawn from too few records to be safe.
Microsoft has written real controls. Every party whose data a question touches must approve it, each party lists the columns others may use, and small groups can be dropped from results. What it has not stated is how those controls work when an agent drafts the questions. The leak a buyer should plan for is by answer, not by breach.
Greyhound Standpoint:
Greyhound Research’s judgment is that the clean room leaks by answer, not by breach. The seal protects the raw records, and Microsoft’s approval and filtering controls are a sound base. A clean room exists to release answers, though, and answers carry information. A set of approved aggregate questions, each harmless alone, can together single out one customer or one patient. The risk sits in the terms of the room more than in its walls.
Statistical agencies have managed this for generations, suppressing small cells in published tables and limiting repeated queries, because aggregates leak when they are combined. Microsoft’s minimum counts follow that practice, and its rule that every affected party approves each query is a real safeguard. What the documents read do not address is pace. Approval built for analysts assumes a person drafts each query and others read it. An agent can draft hundreds, and careful review becomes harder as volume rises. Microsoft advises against personal or regulated data in the preview, which is responsible. A documented limit on the number and pattern of approved queries, agent-written ones included, would change this view.
Chief risk officers, with legal counsel, should write the allowed questions, minimum group sizes, query limits, and the role of agents into the data-sharing agreement before any data goes in. Until the service leaves preview, it should hold no personal or regulated data, as Microsoft itself advises.

The Strongest Objection: The Seal Is Enough For Now
Greyhound SpotlightThe strongest objection to this note is that sealing the largest models removes the objection risk committees raise most often. That part stands, once VR72 reaches customers.
The objection deserves its full weight. For years, risk committees have refused to send sensitive data to the largest models in a public cloud, because the provider could in principle read it. Rack-scale sealing, once it ships, answers that refusal. Microsoft was early to this field, and its smaller enclaves narrow what must be trusted, which is the right engineering direction. On this view, the agent questions can wait.
The objection has weight because it matches how risk committees work. They tend to clear one concern at a time, and the provider concern has been the one holding back regulated AI on the largest models. Removing it will let projects move, and Greyhound Research expects some regulated firms to plan sealed model capacity once VR72 has published terms. The difficulty is sequence. A committee that clears the provider question may not reopen it when agents arrive on the same machines. What tends to go unnoticed is that the approval itself becomes a precedent. Once a sealed platform is cleared for models, agent projects may be approved on the strength of that earlier decision.
Exhibit 6 tests each part of the objection against the evidence. Three parts stand. The claim that agent questions can wait falls short on timing.
Greyhound Standpoint:
Greyhound Research accepts the objection for the provider question and does not accept it for agents. Sealing the largest models will be a real step. It answers the concern risk committees have raised most often about public cloud, Microsoft earned its early position, and smaller enclaves narrow what must be trusted. Buyers should welcome all three.
Where the objection falls short is timing. Agents will run on the same sealed hardware that models run on, and the boundary documented at launch tends to become the boundary customers live with. Platform history is consistent on this point. Defaults set at general availability shape contracts, reference architectures, and audit expectations for years, and they are much harder to change once customers have built on them. With VR72 and the enclaves still in development, that boundary is still being drawn, which makes now the cheapest moment to settle agent memory, the verifier, and the clean room’s answers. The objection would win the timing argument if Microsoft documented those three matters before VR72 and the enclaves reach general availability, and Greyhound Research would say so.

Part 7The Final Greyhound Standpoint
Confidential Computing: The Final Greyhound Standpoint
Greyhound SpotlightFour signals within twelve months would move this position, and Microsoft controls all four.
Greyhound Research’s final judgment is that Ignite 2026 showed how much bigger Microsoft’s sealed room can become and left the harder question with the buyer. The seal keeps the cloud operator out, and Microsoft has shown it reaching across GPUs, which sets confidential computing on the path from a niche control to an option at scale. Inside the seal, the customer’s own agent still reads what it is given and acts on it. When agents work inside the seal, attestation becomes the control: proof of what ran, checked by someone the buyer trusts, and refreshed when the agent changes.
Credit is due on several counts. On sealing, Microsoft was early and is ahead on scale. Confidential H100 machines have been on sale since 2024, VR72 is designed to extend the seal to the largest models, and the enclaves will narrow what must be trusted, which is the right engineering direction. Google is ahead on proof, with a verifier it does not own, and AWS has made isolation a default building block. No provider documents agent memory inside the seal. The clean room is a sound idea whose risk lies in the answers it returns.
The shape of this moment is familiar. Security features in enterprise technology tend to follow a common arc, from premium option to default to a question of evidence. Encryption of stored data followed it, and confidential computing is now moving from the first stage to the second. Within twelve months it should be a standard line in regulated AI tenders, set by buyers, not regulators, and the questions will turn to who checks the proof, how often, and where the agent’s memory sits. Buyers who set those terms early will negotiate from documents. Those who wait will negotiate from defaults.
The evidence has limits. This note first read the Ignite items as three previews, following Microsoft’s summary, and now follows its detailed post, which shows one limited preview and two items in development, none with a price, region list, or release date. That makes the advice firmer, with less to sign and more time to set terms. The 2025 attacks needed physical access, and none has been reported in the wild. Much of what this note calls missing is not stated, rather than shown to be absent. Statuses are as each company published them through November 19, 2026.
Four signals would move this position within twelve months. They are VR72 confidential virtual machines opened to customers with a release date, published prices against ordinary GPU capacity, an attestation service customers can verify without trusting Microsoft alone, and documentation placing agent memory and cached context inside the boundary.
Exhibit 7 sets those signals side by side with what each would change. All four sit with Microsoft.
One event outside Microsoft’s control could reset the comparison: an announcement on sealed computing or attestation at a rival’s conference. Of the four signals, documented agent memory inside the boundary would matter most, because it decides whether the seal covers the reader’s notes.
Chief information security officers in regulated sectors have reason to welcome the bigger seal and to stay exacting. Before any pilot, they should obtain Microsoft’s written answers on which agent data stays inside the seal, who verifies the proof, and how cached prompts are deleted on request. Procurement should then write the verifier, the refresh rule, and evidence retention into the contract at signing. Until the signals arrive, companies should pilot on what is generally available, check the proof, and keep regulated data out of anything the seal does not cover.
