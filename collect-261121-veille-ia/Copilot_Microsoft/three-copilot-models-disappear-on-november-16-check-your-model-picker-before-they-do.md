GitHub published the notice on October 15 and gave it thirty-two days. On November 16, 2026, three models leave GitHub Copilot: Claude Opus 4.8, Claude Opus 5, and GPT-5.6 Terra. The removal covers every Copilot experience — Chat, inline edits, ask and agent modes, and code completions — which is the part worth underlining, because the first time I read a deprecation notice like this I assumed it meant chat only and kept working. It does not mean chat only. It means the model names vanish from the picker everywhere Copilot runs.

Here is the table, straight from GitHub's changelog, because there is no reason to paraphrase a table this short:

| Model leaving on Nov 16 | GitHub's suggested replacement | 
|---|---|
| Claude Opus 4.8 | Claude Opus 5.5 | 
| Claude Opus 5 | Claude Opus 5.5 | 
| GPT-5.6 Terra | GPT-6.1 Sol or GPT-6.1 Luna | 

One footnote, and it is the only exception in the whole notice: Claude Opus 4.8 stays available to individual GitHub Copilot subscribers on annual plans, so they keep an Opus option. GitHub's wording is that the deprecation of Opus 4.8 "does not apply to these customers." That exception does not cover Opus 5 or GPT-5.6 Terra, and it does not cover Business or Enterprise seats. If you are on a monthly individual plan, a team plan, or a company plan, you lose all three. If you are the one person in your group on an annual individual plan, you keep one of them, and you should still check, because "individual subscriber on an annual plan" is a narrower category than most people assume their billing is.

The notice also says something that sounds reassuring and is worth reading twice: "No action is required to remove the models after they have been deprecated." That is true, and it is the trap. Nothing in the notice will remind you to look. There is no banner, no countdown, no migration wizard. On November 16 the names are gone, and whatever you had pinned falls back to whatever Copilot decides is the default. If you pinned a model deliberately — and if you pinned Opus 5 or Terra, you probably did, because nobody pins a model by accident — the fallback is not your choice. It is the absence of your choice. That is why the useful window is this week, not the 16th.

The two swaps are not the same kind of decision, and treating them the same way is the first mistake.

The Opus swap is easy, and the numbers are on your side. Opus 5.5 replaces both Opus models, and GitHub's rate card lists it cheaper than either of them: $4 input and $20 output per million tokens, against $5 and $25 for Opus 4.8 and Opus 5. Those are reference prices — GitHub changes its rate card, and the figures are from the card as reported in late October — but the direction is what matters. You are not being pushed onto a more expensive model. You are being pushed onto a newer, cheaper one in the same class, and the only real cost is the afternoon you spend confirming that Opus 5.5 handles your codebase the way Opus 5 did. I would run that test on a real task, not a toy prompt: a refactor across two or three files, something with a test suite you can run after, because that is where a model downgrade would actually show up, and a five-line completion test tells you nothing.

The Terra swap is the one where the choice bites, because GitHub offered two replacements and they are not interchangeable. GPT-6.1 Sol is the like-for-like option: $2 input and $10 output per million tokens, against Terra's $2 and $12. GPT-6.1 Luna is the cheap option: $0.10 input and $0.50 output — twenty times cheaper than Sol on list price. Twenty times is not a rounding difference. It is a different class of model doing a different job at a different price, and "suggested alternative" is doing a lot of work in that sentence when one of the two suggestions costs a twentieth of the other.

So the honest way to pick is to stop asking which model is better and start asking what Terra was doing for you. If Terra was your everyday model — the one you left selected for completions and quick chat answers — Luna is probably fine, and the savings are real enough to notice on a Pro plan, where Copilot spends AI credits at each model's token rate. If Terra was the model you reached for when a task was too big for the small model and too small to justify Opus — the middle of the range, the "this refactor is fiddly and I want a strong model on it" slot — then Sol is the replacement, and Luna will feel like a downgrade in exactly the places where you were paying for Terra's judgment. The way to tell the difference is to look at what you actually used Terra for in the last two weeks, not what you think you used it for. I have a bad habit here myself: I remember the one hard task a model nailed and forget the forty easy ones. The usage history is more honest than my memory.

There is a third option that arrived after the notice, and I am mentioning it because it changes the Terra decision for some people. GitHub made GPT-6.1 Terra — the plain Terra, not the 5.6 — generally available on October 26, pitched as a lower-cost option than GPT-6.1 Sol. It was not in the October 15 changelog, so it is not one of GitHub's suggested replacements, but it exists now, and if you liked Terra's balance of capability and cost, the new Terra is worth a look before you commit to Sol or Luna. I have not tested it against 5.6 Terra head to head, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. It is a candidate, not a recommendation.

The second thing worth knowing is that none of these models is dying. Only the Copilot route to them is closing. Anthropic's own model deprecations page lists claude-opus-5 as active, with a tentative retirement date of "not sooner than July 24, 2027," and claude-opus-4-8 as active with "not sooner than May 28, 2027." OpenAI's deprecations page does not list gpt-5.6-terra as deprecated at all. After November 16 you can still call all three from the vendors' own APIs. What you lose is calling them from inside Copilot, in your editor, on your plan. That distinction matters if you are on a team that is already paying for Claude or OpenAI API access separately — the model is not gone, the convenient route is — and it matters even more if you are not, because then the Copilot route was the only route you had, and the replacement is the whole decision.

The part of the notice that team admins are most likely to miss is the auto-enable line. Under default model enablement, GitHub says the suggested alternatives are automatically enabled for Copilot Enterprise and Copilot Business customers, unless an administrator has turned off the global default or explicitly disabled the model. If your admin turned off the global default — and plenty of admins did, back when model lists were growing every month and the instinct was to lock the list down — the replacements will not appear for your team on November 16, and nobody will get an error message explaining why. The fix is in Copilot settings, in the model policies: enable Opus 5.5 and whichever of Sol or Luna your team is standardising on, before the 16th, and then check that they actually show up in the picker. I would also check the workspace-level settings, because a pinned model can live in more than one place: your user settings, a workspace .vscode folder, a team policy. The retirement hits all of them at once, and the one you forgot is the one that breaks on a Monday morning.

There is some irony in the timing that is worth a paragraph, because it tells you where Copilot is going. On November 4, two weeks after the deprecation notice, GitHub announced that Copilot is now available inside Xcode's built-in coding assistant — public preview, starting with Xcode 27.3 beta, macOS Tahoe 26.6 or later, all Copilot plans supported, set up under Xcode > Settings > Intelligence. The existing Copilot for Xcode extension stays available as a separate option. So in the same month, Copilot is expanding to a new surface and shrinking its model list on every existing one. That is not a contradiction. It is the shape of the product now: more places to use Copilot, fewer models to choose from inside it, and the model list changing on a schedule GitHub sets. The Fusion change from late October points the same way — Copilot took away the model picker in some experiences and started choosing for you. The direction of travel is clear, and it is not toward you hand-picking a model and keeping it forever. It is toward GitHub managing the list and you managing the policy around it.

Which brings me to the part that is actually about you, not GitHub. This is the third wave of Copilot model removals in about six weeks. The first wave, on October 2, took Gemini 3.5 Flash, Gemini 3.6 Flash, Kimi K2.7 Code, and Claude Opus 4.7. The second, on October 19, took six more, including GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4, and Grok 4.5. Now the third wave takes three more on November 16. Thirteen models in six weeks, if you count them all. That is not a one-off cleanup. It is the cadence. A model you pin today has a shelf life measured in months, and the notice period is about a month, and the notice itself is a changelog entry you have to go looking for. The practical response is not to be angry about it — the replacements have been cheaper or equivalent every time, and the old models stay available at the vendors — it is to stop treating a pinned model as a permanent part of your setup. Pin deliberately, write down why you picked it, and check the changelog when a new model appears, because a new model appearing is usually the signal that an old one is about to leave.

So here is the short version, the checklist I ran for myself this morning. First, open the Copilot model picker in your editor and look at what is actually selected — not what you think is selected, what is there. If it is Opus 4.8, Opus 5, or GPT-5.6 Terra, pick the replacement now: Opus 5.5 for either Opus model, Sol or Luna for Terra, and make the Sol-versus-Luna call based on what Terra was actually doing for you. Second, if you are on a team plan, check with whoever owns Copilot settings that the replacements are enabled in the model policies, and do it before November 16, not after someone reports the model is missing. Third, run one real task on the replacement before the deadline — a refactor with a test suite, not a toy prompt — so the 16th is not the first time you find out how it behaves on your code. Fourth, if you are the annual-plan individual who keeps Opus 4.8, decide whether keeping it is a strategy or just inertia, because it is the only one of the three with an exception, and exceptions get removed in later waves too.

The deadline is ten days away, and the work is about twenty minutes. That ratio is the whole story. The models are not being taken from you in secret, and the replacements are not a trap. But the notice does not chase you, the fallback is not your choice, and the one person who is going to check your model picker before November 16 is you.
