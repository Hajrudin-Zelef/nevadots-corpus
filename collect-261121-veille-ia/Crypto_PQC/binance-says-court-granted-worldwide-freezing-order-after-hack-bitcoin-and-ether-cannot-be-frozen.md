Binance said Wednesday, Nov. 18, that the Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM) Courts granted it a $6.5 billion worldwide freezing order against unidentified people accused of the Oct. 29 theft of about $6.34 billion, the largest crypto theft on record. The order can bind only exchanges, issuers and other intermediaries that act on it, so it reaches stablecoins that a company can freeze but not the bitcoin and ether that Elliptic says cannot be frozen.

Anyone who keeps crypto on an exchange, or holds dollars in USDT or USDC, should read the line this case draws: whether stolen or held funds can be frozen depends on how a token is built, not on the size of a court order. Binance says it has covered affected assets by committing more than $6.34 billion from its treasury, its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) and insurance funds, which is separate from recovering the stolen crypto itself.

### Court Order Reaches Exchanges and Issuers, Not Bitcoin Itself

Justice William Stone SBS KC issued the injunction, according to The Block. The package, as Cointelegraph described it, includes a worldwide freezing order, interim orders over assets frozen by certain named parties, and orders requiring stolen assets to be handed over, with Gibson Dunn representing Binance. Some interim orders name defendants that have already frozen stolen funds, and Binance does not allege wrongdoing by them.

Binance's lawyers maintain a continuously updated list of 26,000 wallet addresses that they say hold stolen assets. M. Kendall Day of Gibson Dunn said the orders can be served via the blockchain and called the injunction "one of, if not the, largest publicly known freezing orders ever made." That list is far wider than the one law enforcement published: the FBI's Oct. 31 alert named 628 addresses, 608 on Ethereum, 19 on Bitcoin and one on Solana.

A freezing order, also called a Mareva injunction after a 1975 English case, stops a defendant from dissipating assets before a court can rule, and it can have worldwide effect. ADGM, Abu Dhabi's financial free zone, applies English common law directly, which is why English-style remedies are available there. English courts have acted against crypto thefts before: in 2021 London's High Court ordered Binance to freeze accounts linked to an alleged $2.6 million hack of Fetch.ai.

### Issuers and Partners Froze Billions Before Any Court Order

Most of what has been frozen so far predates the ADGM order by weeks. Elliptic says $2.85 billion has been frozen or seized, and that about $786 million of stablecoins was converted before the freezes landed. Tether's first major freeze came 37 minutes after the stolen funds reached the attacker's main Ethereum wallet, and Circle froze about $823 million three minutes later. Tether's Paolo Ardoino said the company froze more than 1.567 billion USDT.

Native assets were a different story. TRM Labs' on-chain counts from Oct. 30, as calculated by Stablecoin Insider, show about 100% of the stolen USDC and 71% of the USDT frozen, against none of the 18,270 bitcoin, 233 million XRP or about 970,500 SOL. The U.S. Justice Department said Nov. 12 that it has court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the stolen assets, part of more than $2.7 billion frozen by government and private-sector efforts. That is authorization, not a completed seizure; AP reports the government typically files a forfeiture claim, and a judge then decides who has a legitimate claim.

### Does Freezing Stolen Crypto Mean Binance Gets It Back?

No. The $6.5 billion figure is the amount the injunction covers, not a tally of recovered assets, and Binance did not disclose how much has been returned. Binance says some issuers, exchanges and partners require a court order before they release or return frozen funds.

Experts disagree about how much the order changes. Irena Heaver of the law firm NEOSLegal said freezing keeps hackers from moving funds but does not automatically return money to Binance, and that issuers can cancel frozen tokens and reissue them to rightful owners with proof of ownership and legal grounds. Anton Golub of Forte said it is not yet known whether Binance will need a court order. Elliptic's Tom Robinson told The Block in an earlier interview that issuers would want a clear legal basis before reissuing frozen stablecoins, and that the process could take weeks or months.

One commentator at TFTC set a benchmark: recovery above 70% of the total within 90 days would show the legal layer working, while stalling near 45% would suggest the order only works on freezable assets. That is one author's test, not an industry standard.

### Attackers Swapped Into Assets That No Issuer Can Freeze

Part of the haul was never freezable: the 18,270 bitcoin, 233 million XRP and about 970,500 SOL. The rest was stablecoins, $3.46 billion by Elliptic's count, and Elliptic says the attacker's first priority was to swap those for assets that cannot be frozen, such as ether and TRX. The FBI said the group was converting stolen assets primarily into Ethereum.

Bitcoin shows why. Neither bitcoin nor ether has an issuer that can be ordered to act, though exchanges and other intermediaries can restrict accounts. As of Elliptic's Nov. 6 update, Decrypt reported Elliptic's figures: the 18,270 bitcoin sat in 19 addresses and had not moved since the day of the hack, and about $3.11 billion remained in attacker-linked addresses, mostly ether and bitcoin.

Estimates of what remains out of reach vary by date and method. TRM Labs put the attacker's holdings at about $2.4 billion on Oct. 30. Elliptic estimated that attackers still controlled about $3.33 billion that issuers could not immobilize as of 7:00 UTC Friday. Robinson's earlier estimate was that about $3.3 billion remained in bitcoin, ether and XRP, and TFTC put the outstanding share at roughly 55%, or about $3.5 billion.

A court order cannot stop a transaction already broadcast to a network, but it can bind the places where the funds must eventually turn into something else. The FBI asked THORChain node operators, instant exchanges, DeFi services, RPC node operators and blockchain analytics firms to refuse transactions linked to the flagged addresses. TRM expects bridges, swap services and over-the-counter brokers in the next laundering stage, a forecast rather than an observed event, and Elliptic's analysis of cash-out says that is where funds can potentially be identified and stopped.

### Critics Say Issuer Freezes Undercut Crypto's Censorship-Resistance Promise

The speed of the stablecoin freezes drew praise and criticism at the same time. Izabella Kaminska of The Blind Spot argued freezable assets are "just another incarnation of tradfi." Amanda Fischer of Better Markets said the freezes cut against crypto's core values and that issuers freeze when convenient, sometimes citing a court order and sometimes not. Ardoino defended Tether's freeze, saying protecting the industry and its partners is the company's main priority.

Circle's role stood out. Its CEO, Jeremy Allaire, said earlier this year that Circle would not freeze USDC without a court order or law-enforcement direction, and this time Circle froze more than $800 million within roughly an hour of the first movements of stolen funds, according to Gizmodo. Golub contrasted that speed with smaller projects, noting that after the roughly $290 million Drift hack in April, Circle was criticized for not promptly freezing linked USDC.

For ordinary holders, the point is not limited to thieves. The same issuer discretion applies to any USDT or USDC balance, and Tether says its cumulative freezes now exceed $5 billion across 2,900 investigations, a figure the company itself reported.

### Fake Job Interview Opened Path to Wallet Signing Service

Binance's Nov. 3 incident report, as summarized by BleepingComputer, describes an intrusion that began in September, when a fake recruiter contacted a Binance software engineer and arranged two fake interviews. The second interview had the engineer download a coding assignment that contained custom malware that antivirus tools missed, and by Oct. 20 the attackers had used the engineer's computer and an internal AI tool to compromise two more wallet-infrastructure employees, obtaining credentials and a container deployment token.

On Oct. 29 the attackers used those credentials to deploy a malicious script and reach the wallet signing service, and they abused Ethereum's EIP-7702 authorization mechanism to keep stealing after Binance suspended withdrawals. TRM Labs said preparation began at least 36 hours earlier, with small Tornado Cash withdrawals starting Oct. 28.

The FBI attributed the theft on Oct. 31 to North Korea's TraderTraitor group, which it previously blamed for the $1.5 billion Bybit hack in February 2025, and TRM attributes it to the group with high confidence. North Korea has denied involvement, Decrypt reported. Totals differ by counting method: Binance says about $6.34 billion, TRM $6.36 billion across ten blockchains, and Elliptic $6.43 billion across 11. Because the ADGM order names unidentified defendants, it works through the intermediaries that hold or move the funds rather than through the people behind the attack.

### Binance Sought Order in Abu Dhabi, Where Its Global Platform Is Licensed

ADGM's Financial Services Regulatory Authority granted Binance three licenses in December 2025, covering exchange, clearing and broker-dealer services. The regulator said on Oct. 30 that it was aware of the incident, which it described as unauthorized withdrawals of assets. Binance co-CEO Richard Teng is a former ADGM chief executive who also held positions at the Singapore Exchange and the Monetary Authority of Singapore. Binance pleaded guilty in November 2023 to U.S. charges that included Bank Secrecy Act violations and agreed to pay $4.3 billion.

Teng's public technology-policy record centers on two themes. He has said at Consensus Hong Kong that clear and transparent regulation is a prerequisite for sustainable innovation, and he has said Binance uses AI "on an extensive basis," according to CNBC syndicated reporting, for customer service, security and compliance monitoring. Binance's own incident report names a compromised internal AI tool as part of the attack chain. Day, who leads the Gibson Dunn effort, co-chairs the Fintech and Digital Assets Practice Group at the firm and led a Justice Department section on money laundering and asset recovery from 2014 to 2017. Binance deputy general counsel Dugan Bliss said the company will keep using every lawful tool to trace, freeze and recover the stolen assets.

The case draws one practical line. Freezes work where a central party sits in the path of the money: an issuer, an exchange or a swap service. They fail where a protocol has no such party. Whether the $6.5 billion order recovers anything beyond the freezable perimeter will depend on whether issuers act on it, whether Binance keeps its 26,000-address list current as funds move, and whether the stolen bitcoin and ether ever touch an intermediary that obeys.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a court freeze bitcoin or ether?

A court can order people and companies to freeze assets they control, but it cannot change how the networks work. Elliptic says ether and bitcoin cannot be frozen, because neither has an issuer that can be ordered to act. A court order only bites when the funds reach an exchange or service that obeys it.

### How much of the Binance theft has been frozen or recovered?

Binance said about $2.85 billion frozen or intercepted of the roughly $6.34 billion affected. The Justice Department said more than $2.7 billion has been frozen and that it has court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the total. Binance has not disclosed how much has been returned to it.

### Are Binance customers' funds safe after the hack?

Binance said it committed more than $6.34 billion from its treasury, SAFU and insurance funds, and that 98% of impacted token assets had been replenished as of Oct. 31. Binance.US, which serves American residents, said it was not affected by the incident.

### Who is behind the Binance hack?

The FBI attributed the theft on Oct. 31 to North Korea's TraderTraitor group, and North Korea has denied involvement. The ADGM order itself names unidentified people, so it is enforced through exchanges, issuers and other intermediaries rather than against the attackers directly.
