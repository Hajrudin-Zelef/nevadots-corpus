# CISA, NSA, Australian cyber agencies urge early vendor readiness for PQC transition across critical systems

The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), the Australian Signals Directorate’s Australian Cyber Security Centre (ASD’s ACSC), and the National Security Agency (NSA) have released joint guidance and a quick-reference list to help organizations assess whether their vendors are ready to transition to post-quantum cryptography (PQC). As quantum computing advances, widely used public-key cryptography will become vulnerable to compromise by cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQCs). Organizations must therefore plan and move to PQC, which uses algorithms that can withstand both classical and quantum-enabled attacks, before CRQCs arrive.

Many organizations depend on third-party vendors for managed services, cloud capabilities, and other cryptography-dependent products. Cryptography is also embedded in hard-to-update systems with long asset lifecycles, such as industrial control systems and Internet of Things (IoT) devices, which often require vendor support or replacement. A successful PQC transition therefore hinges on vendor readiness, and early, structured engagement with suppliers is critical to planning.

Organizations must understand their cryptographic dependencies and vendor-related risks, use the recommended questions to assess vendor readiness, and engage suppliers early to support transition planning. They should also build PQC considerations into procurement, contract renewals, and vendor assurance activities.

Titled ‘Post-quantum questions to ask your vendors,’ the guidance offers vendor-neutral questions for use during procurement, contract reviews, and vendor assurance activities. They help organizations determine whether a vendor understands quantum-related risks, maintains transparency over its cryptographic implementations, and can support a secure, cost-effective, and timely transition. The questions follow the key stages of a PQC transition such as identifying cryptographic dependencies, assessing risk, prioritizing systems, implementing PQC, and managing change. Written for cybersecurity leaders, procurement and vendor management teams, and technical stakeholders, the guidance supports informed decisions about third-party products and services.

The first phase of the PQC transition is to locate and inventory cryptographic dependencies, which means identifying where and how a vendor uses cryptography in its products and services, including for authentication, key exchange, signing, and protecting data in transit and at rest. This may also cover vendor-managed or cloud-based services that rely on cryptography to authenticate, protect, or access organizational systems, such as software as a service, managed service providers, identity services, backup services, and other third-party platforms.

Understanding these dependencies early helps organizations avoid unexpected constraints during the PQC transition, especially where dependencies are embedded, inherited, or difficult to change. Organizations should prioritize these questions early in vendor engagement, particularly during initial stages of PQC planning and before entering long-term contracts or cloud service agreements, where cryptographic choices may be hard to change.

The guidance calls upon vendors to be able to show that they maintain a current, actively updated inventory of their cryptographic dependencies, such as a cryptographic bill of materials (CBOM), which extends a software bill of materials with the algorithms, libraries, protocols, parameters, and configurations that cryptographic components use. The inventory should cover third-party and embedded components and show where cryptography underpins authentication, signing, and data protection in transit and at rest. Vendors should also identify, without relying on assumptions, where cryptography is implemented across application, operating system, middleware, hardware, and firmware layers, since this determines how difficult a PQC transition will be.

Since hardcoded, fixed, or hardware-bound cryptography can dictate transition timelines and force system replacement, vendors should either confirm that none exists or identify affected components and provide a feasible remediation roadmap, such as patching, firmware upgrades, or replacement. They should likewise disclose all third-party dependencies that introduce cryptography, including the libraries those dependencies use, and track them in a CBOM or equivalent inventory, with standardized libraries preferred over bespoke or proprietary implementations.

Vendors should also explain how cryptography is updated, as patch-based updates delivered through supported maintenance releases with clear long-term support commitments promote cryptographic agility and help avoid large-scale system replacements. They should identify any hardware roots of trust and describe their role in cryptographic operations, because hardware trust anchors and the certificates or cryptographic material embedded in them can lock in cryptographic choices and are hard to replace.

Also, their responses should note any resulting PQC transition constraints, affected components, and feasible remediation approaches. Finally, vendors should describe how they verify firmware authenticity, for example through Secure Boot or the Unified Extensible Firmware Interface, since long-lived firmware signing mechanisms are a key PQC constraint. They should show that firmware and boot processes can be updated to support PQC signature schemes without hardware replacement, or else provide a roadmap for supporting PQC-compatible signature schemes.

When it comes to assessing risk to individual systems, the agencies highlighted that the focus is on identifying which data and trust decisions rely on traditional asymmetric cryptography, the sensitivity and longevity of that data, and how vendors evaluate the risks posed by a CRQC. The agencies provided couple of questions to help determine how quantum-related risks apply to vendor products and services, particularly those protecting sensitive or long-lived data or exposed to external threats.

Vendors should map which customer data flows rely on traditional asymmetric cryptography across interfaces and protocols, specifying whether each protects authentication, key establishment, or data integrity, since this mapping is needed to assess exposure, select transition approaches, and identify edge cases during testing. They should also state their assumptions about the lifespan of the data they process or store, including default retention behaviors and the controls customers have to manage those lifespans.

Data with long-lived confidentiality requirements is at risk from ‘harvest now, decrypt later’ attacks enabled by future CRQCs and may need to move to PQC sooner, so these assumptions should be reflected in the vendor’s PQC roadmap and customer configuration guidance and weighed against customers’ own data lifespans and cryptographic needs. Vendors should further provide a documented, product- or service-specific assessment of CRQC risks that clearly states its scope, assumptions, and outcomes and draws on authoritative guidance, demonstrating an understanding of quantum-related risks, anticipated timelines, and mitigation strategies.

Vendors should distinguish between signing, encryption, and authentication risks, with separate risk assessments, transition timelines, and mitigation strategies for each, because their failures have different impacts and timelines, and integrity and authentication risks may require different transition sequences and controls than confidentiality risks. They should also state whether customers can choose stronger cryptographic settings today, offered as supported, documented configurations rather than bespoke or one-off implementations. Such measures, including larger key sizes, may offer only marginal, short-term risk reduction and do not address the threat from a CRQC, so they are no substitute for transitioning to PQC.

Finally, vendors should identify which authentication mechanisms rely on traditional asymmetric cryptography, such as certificates, smart cards, and tokens, along with their lifecycles and replacement timelines, because authentication systems often have long replacement cycles and can become critical bottlenecks.

When it comes to triaging and prioritizing systems for transition, the focus is on ensuring the vendor’s transition sequence aligns with organizational risk priorities and PQC milestones recommended by the relevant authoring agency. This involves identifying potential blockers early and ensuring that any post-quantum/traditional (PQ/T) hybrid approaches are treated as temporary measures.

Here, the agencies called upon vendors to commit to specific, versioned dates for PQC-ready products and services, with offerings available well before the end of 2030 so that customers can test, transition in stages, and roll out to production in time to complete their own transitions by then. They should also disclose which components require hardware replacement or re-architecture, including the reasons for the redesign, known constraints or bottlenecks, and any effects on delivery timelines, since hardware changes are long-lead items that can significantly affect timelines, cost, and interoperability. Where vendors support PQ/T hybrid modes, they should define them clearly as a temporary measure for short-term interoperability or resilience, not a long-term solution, because a CRQC would render the traditional elements obsolete and require a move to pure PQC algorithms. They should provide a documented plan and timeline for de-hybridization and transition to PQC-only.

Vendors should also explain how customer risk shapes their prioritization of PQC work, using a documented, risk-based approach that weighs system exposure, data sensitivity, and transition complexity rather than customer size or revenue, so that higher-exposure customers or those with more sensitive data are not delayed for commercial or operational reasons.

Finally, they should describe the internal governance that oversees their PQC transition, including products and services delivered through managed or shared platforms. Defined structures with executive ownership, documented decision forums, and regular review cycles should oversee PQC planning, delivery, and customer impact, since clear governance and executive accountability reduce implementation risk and support consistent prioritization across products and services.

When implementing post-quantum cryptographic algorithms, the agencies’ focus is on ensuring that PQC algorithms are deployed securely by avoiding premature, proprietary or experimental cryptography, adopting standardized and reputable libraries, and planning for performance, key management and safe rollback. This phase is particularly important before enabling PQC in live environments or committing to major upgrades that are difficult to reverse.

Vendors should state which PQC algorithms they will support and when those will be generally available to customers, committing to standards-based algorithms approved by the appropriate authoring agency, since this underpins interoperability, assurance, and the long-term sustainability of PQC deployments. They should confirm that their implementation is production-ready, offered as a supported, customer-deployable release with defined support arrangements, such as service-level agreements, and documented guidance for deployment and operation, so that organizations do not have to rely on preview, experimental, or unsupported functionality.

Vendors should also explain how they validate and maintain their cryptographic libraries, using reputable, actively maintained libraries with evidence of validation or independent scrutiny and avoiding bespoke or proprietary implementations where possible, to reduce security, compliance, and long-term maintenance risks.

Vendors should further explain how key management, certificate sizes, and lifecycle processes will change under PQC, including the effects on existing systems and how these will be supported in production, because larger keys and certificates can affect operational processes and system performance, and poor planning may cause performance or compatibility problems. They should document how customers enable PQC in their product or service, including configuration and deployment steps, prerequisites, and known compatibility or interoperability considerations for relevant protocols and integrations, which reduces deployment risk and supports predictable, safe transitions.

Finally, they should confirm that PQC is included in base licensing, without additional fees or premium add-ons, so that security controls are not paywalled, which could delay adoption and leave customers with uneven risk levels.

When it comes to communicating with vendors and educating relevant stakeholders, the agencies prescribed institutionalizing change and sustaining the security posture beyond the initial PQC transition. This involves maintaining clear communication with vendors, establishing well-defined cryptographic deprecation roadmaps and ensuring support staff receive appropriate training. This phase is critical to managing cryptographic change, minimizing customer impact and supporting long-term operational readiness.

Vendors should explain how customers are notified of cryptographic changes, using formal and predictable mechanisms such as security advisories, pre-release notes, and transition notices, so that organizations can assess impact, schedule testing, and maintain security and compliance requirements. They should provide customer-ready PQC transition guides with clear instructions, examples, and testing guidance for enabling and validating PQC, which reduces integration effort and errors and supports safer, more efficient adoption.

Vendors should also set out how traditional cryptography will be deprecated, including any temporary use of PQ/T hybrid approaches, with clear timelines covering warning periods, support windows, interim hybrid use, and final removal, along with a defined path to PQC-only operation. Clear timelines help organizations avoid abrupt change, plan testing, and ensure hybrid methods are used only briefly.

Vendors should show that their support and engineering teams are trained to handle PQC, through evidence of staff training, updated runbooks, and documentation of known issues, since trained teams reduce support risk and speed up incident resolution during the transition. They should demonstrate that their roadmaps align with relevant guidance and include clear milestones for customer testing, transition, and production rollout before the end of 2030, which helps avoid dead ends and ensures customer transition plans can be completed within expected timeframes.

Finally, vendors should provide evidence that they contribute to or track relevant PQC standards bodies and explain how this shapes their roadmaps and implementation decisions, as such engagement supports alignment with emerging standards, reduces incompatibility risks, and lets vendors share implementation challenges to improve practicality and interoperability.

When engaging vendors on PQC readiness, organizations may encounter responses that indicate differing levels of maturity, visibility, or preparedness. These concerns do not necessarily indicate unacceptable risk, and organizations should consider them in context, including vendor’s risk level, sensitivity and lifetime of the data involved, and the vendor’s willingness to engage transparently. Used alongside the questions in this publication, these considerations support risk-informed decisions when managing cryptographic dependencies across the supply chain.

Common concerns in vendor responses include limited visibility of cryptographic use, reliance on general assurances, and unclear or deferred transition timelines. They also include using proprietary or opaque cryptography, treating PQC as an optional or premium function, over-reliance on compensating controls, and a lack of governance or ownership.
