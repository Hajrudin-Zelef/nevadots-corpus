SpaceX completed its all-stock acquisition of Anysphere, the company behind the Cursor code editor, on August 14, 2026, at an implied equity value of $60 billion, and Cursor is now a wholly owned SpaceX subsidiary. SpaceX’s Note 20 filing records the April option and the June exercise, its closing 10-Q describes the completed merger, CNBC reported the signed deal on June 16, and MarketReview puts the effective date at August 14. Below: the timeline, what Cursor’s holders received, and what a Cursor customer should do about plans, data and models.

## Option in April, signing on June 16, effective August 14

Note 20 says SpaceX received an option in April 2026 giving it “the right, but not the obligation, to acquire Anysphere”. The same arrangement included a compute agreement: SpaceX would provide Cursor with GPU cluster capacity, and the two companies would collaborate on improving existing models, including Grok. In June 2026 SpaceX exercised the call option and entered into an Agreement and Plan of Merger, with consideration paid in Class A common stock at an implied equity value of $60 billion.

On structure, Euronews identified the merger vehicle as a SpaceX subsidiary, X67 Inc., merging into Anysphere. The closing 10-Q confirms the outcome: the merger subsidiary merged with and into Cursor, which survived as a wholly owned subsidiary. The parent’s own IPO closing release says its Class A shares began trading on the Nasdaq on June 12, 2026, four days before the signing, and Euronews gives the IPO price as $135. For the listing itself, see our report on SpaceX’s IPO filing.

| Date | Event | Source | 
|---|---|---|
| April 2026 | SpaceX receives the option to acquire Anysphere, with no obligation to buy, alongside a GPU compute and model collaboration agreement | SEC Note 20 | 
| June 2026 | SpaceX exercises the call option and enters the merger agreement | SEC Note 20 | 
| June 12, 2026 | SpaceX Class A shares begin trading on the Nasdaq | SpaceX IPO closing release |

One safety net is now moot. According to CNBC, citing SpaceX’s IPO filings, SpaceX had agreed that if the deal was not consummated it would pay Cursor a termination fee of $1.5 billion plus $8.5 billion in computing resources. The merger closed, so neither amount applies.

## 389,289,254 shares, 3.4% dilution and a 16% move on announcement day

MarketReview’s account of SpaceX’s August 14 Form 8-K gives the conversion terms a memo can quote.

| Cursor instrument | Converted into | 
|---|---|
| Common and preferred shares outstanding before the merger | 389,289,254 SpaceX Class A shares | 
| Unvested restricted stock units | About 29.1 million SpaceX restricted stock units tied to Class A stock | 
| Stock options | About 44.4 million options to buy SpaceX Class A shares | 

CNBC says the $60 billion of Class A stock represented about 3.4% dilution at SpaceX’s IPO valuation, so the measuring point is the IPO, not a later price. A slice that small explains why stock was the natural currency: SpaceX paid for a company with paper it had just listed.

CNBC says SpaceX shares gained roughly 16% on June 16, topping Amazon and Microsoft by market capitalisation and making SpaceX the fourth most valuable company in the U.S. For holders the practical point is that a stock deal pays in shares: the $60 billion is an implied value at signing, and what a Cursor shareholder or employee holds now is SpaceX stock priced by the market.

One more figure circulates with less certainty. CNBC reports, citing a source who requested anonymity, that Thrive Capital’s combined SpaceX and Cursor stake is now worth more than $10 billion. Treat it as a single-source number.

## A $60 billion price set against $2.6 billion of B2B revenue and a shrinking share

The valuation trend is steep. Cursor’s Series C post gives $900 million at a $9.9 billion valuation from Thrive, Accel, Andreessen Horowitz and DST in June 2025, and its Series D post gives $2.3 billion at a $29.3 billion post-money valuation in November 2025, with Coatue, NVIDIA and Google welcomed as new partners. The Wall Street Journal, as relayed by PCMag, puts Cursor at $2.5 billion in January 2025, and PCMag adds that the week before the SpaceX deal Cursor was looking at a funding round that would push its estimated value to over $50 billion. SpaceX’s $60 billion is 24 times the January 2025 mark, reached in 17 months.

The revenue evidence is thinner than the price. CNBC notes that Cursor said in November 2025 that it had crossed $1 billion in annualized revenue. Euronews, citing company data shared with Reuters, puts annualized business-to-business revenue at roughly $2.6 billion when the deal was announced. On that figure $60 billion is about 23 times, but B2B revenue is only part of the business, so the multiple depends on the denominator you choose.

Market share points the other way. CNBC, citing Ramp spending data, says Cursor’s share of AI coding fell from 41% in June 2025 to about 26% in May 2026, and that Anthropic now controls half of that category.

The price buys more than a revenue line. The April agreement ties Cursor to SpaceX GPU capacity and joint model work, and in its own post on the partnership Cursor said its training had been bottlenecked by compute and that its team would use Colossus infrastructure to scale up its models. Euronews reads the deal as handing xAI a stronger position in AI-assisted coding, where it has trailed rivals, while giving Cursor far more compute. With dilution at 3.4%, the deal looks expensive as a headline and modest as a cost to SpaceX. PCMag names the risk plainly: if the compute does not close the gap with rivals, SpaceX “could be saddled with a company that peaked before it was purchased”.

## Grok 4.5 was trained on Cursor usage data

The detail most relevant to a paying customer sits in Cursor’s own Grok 4.5 post. Cursor says it trained the mixture-of-experts model jointly with SpaceXAI and that “Training included trillions of tokens of Cursor data which capture a wide-range of user interactions with codebases and software tools.” MarketReview dates that disclosure to July, a month before the merger became effective, and the April agreement had already provided for collaboration on models including Grok.

The sentence speaks of “a wide-range of user interactions”; whether that covers business tenants as well as individual users, and what a customer can opt out of, is a question for Cursor in writing. Read Cursor’s current data-use and privacy documentation for your plan, and ask Cursor to confirm how your organisation’s code and prompts are used, including for model training with SpaceX.

## Cursor plans and prices as checked on November 13, 2026

As of November 13, 2026, the $20 Pro plan and the $40 Bugbot seat are both legacy. Cursor’s legacy plans page says “New customers start on The X Subscription”, and it is written for anyone still on a legacy plan. Cursor’s Bugbot legacy pricing page documents the $40 per user per month seat price as legacy, and the current plan documentation lists code review with Bugbot as part of Premium and above.

| Plan or line item | Monthly price | Status in Cursor’s docs on November 13, 2026 | 
|---|---|---|
| Plus | $8 | Listed in the plans table on Cursor’s models and pricing page | 
| Premium | $30 | Listed in the same table; the comparable plan to Pro on The X Subscription; Bugbot code review is part of Premium and above | 
| Pro | $20 | Legacy plan that new customers cannot start | 
| Bugbot seat | $40 per user | Documented as legacy Bugbot pricing | 

For a team still on legacy Pro, the comparable Premium plan is $10 more per seat per month, or $120 a year. A team paying the legacy $20 for Pro plus $40 for a Bugbot seat is at $60 per seat per month at list, against $30 for Premium, which includes Bugbot code review. Check seat counts and usage terms before treating that as a saving.

## The model picker after the deal

Cursor’s documentation home, checked on November 13, 2026, lists Composer 2.5 alongside Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 3.8 Flash and the Gemini 4 models Argon, Carbon and Hydrogen. It shows no Gemini 3.7 Flash and no Kimi. Cursor’s models and pricing page says it “supports frontier models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, SpaceXSI, and more”, so the multi-vendor selling point survives, now with a SpaceX-owned model line in the list. (The Grok post says SpaceXAI and the model docs say SpaceXSI; both appear in Cursor’s own wording.)

Concentration risk has moved rather than grown. Anthropic, which CNBC says holds half the AI coding category, and OpenAI supply models into a product whose parent now has its own models. Cursor’s docs still list both vendors among supported models, which is the current evidence a buyer can point to. Whether routing, defaults or pricing tilt toward SpaceX’s models over time is a procurement question, and the answer belongs in the contract, not in the deal narrative.

## What to do before the next Cursor renewal

Start with the plan. If any team is on legacy Pro, decide whether Plus at $8 or Premium at $30 fits it, and take Bugbot off the order form as a separate $40 line unless your contract still prices it that way. Then settle the data question before signing: ask Cursor to state in writing how customer prompts and code are used, whether any of it feeds SpaceX model training, and what that means for your plan tier, and put the answer in the order form or data processing terms. Our assessment is that the data question, more than the $60 billion price, is what this deal changes for a buyer, because Cursor’s Grok 4.5 post describes interaction data used in training a model built jointly with SpaceXAI.

Keep a second route funded as well, whether direct API access to a model vendor or another AI coding tool, so that a change in model defaults or terms does not leave your engineers without one.
