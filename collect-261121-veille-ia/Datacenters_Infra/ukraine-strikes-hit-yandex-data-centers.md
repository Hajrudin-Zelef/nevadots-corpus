Soon after the attacks, a claim went viral that Russia’s online bot army had suddenly gone quiet. Some European officials repeated it, and President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said in a speech that “everyone noticed” a drop.

The evidence is thinner than the headlines suggested. Much of the buzz was based on internet traffic data showing that automated requests from Yandex’s network had almost vanished by October 11. But most automated traffic from Yandex is ordinary search engine crawling, the software that scans websites so they show up in search results. Switching off the servers would stop that whether or not any propaganda account went silent. Cloudflare, the internet company whose data was used, later said its numbers do not appear to support the bot claim, though it could not rule out a link.

Ukraine’s Center for Countering Disinformation then published a report on October 15. It tracked 5,201 accounts it considered suspicious on Facebook and Telegram. On October 8, the day Sasovo went offline, their messages fell by 83.6%, from 4,032 to 660. Activity was back above normal by October 10, then dropped 42.6% again on October 12, the day after the Vladimir strike. The agency itself called the effect “substantial but short-lived,” and its data has not been independently checked. Zelenskyy’s office has since said he was describing the general mood on social media, not a measured fall in bots.
