At 22:31 UTC on October 7th, Yandex Cloud's public status page posted a notice that read like routine maintenance: "Power outages are observed in the ru-central1-b availability zone."<sup>[1]</sup> Thirteen hours later the same page gave the cause. "The cause of the incident was a fire that occurred as a result of a UAV attack on the Yandex data center in Sasovo," it said, adding that "The data center has been completely shut down."<sup>[1]</sup>

NASA's fire-tracking satellites picked up a blaze on the site at about 2 a.m. local time,<sup>[2]</sup> and the region's governor said a roof had caught fire and windows had been blown out in several homes in the district.<sup>[3]</sup> The data center had opened in 2014, and local officials had once called it "an extremely promising high-tech enterprise," in our translation of The Insider's report.<sup>[2]</sup>

Over the next four days Yandex's status page recorded two more strikes and the company's scramble to move customers elsewhere. Overnight into October 9th, drones hit a second Yandex site, in Kaluga, where the company said "several data center modules have been completely disabled."<sup>[4]</sup> That morning Yandex told its cloud customers it had arranged for three Russian rivals, Selectel, K2Cloud and VK Cloud, to take in their workloads, and gave them a code word to use when they applied: "CloudBro."<sup>[1]</sup>

The CloudBro arrangement was Yandex's first answer to who bears the loss: it passed its customers to competitors. Yandex isn't the first cloud provider to keep this kind of log. In March, during the war between Iran and the United States and Israel, drones struck two Amazon Web Services facilities in the United Arab Emirates and damaged a third in Bahrain, and in September AWS said it couldn't restore the data that customers had kept only in its Bahrain region. Two operators, each on the receiving end of a different war, have now told customers on their own status pages to fall back on backups: AWS said the damage exceeded what its regional and multi-zone services were designed to withstand, and Yandex urged customers to use other disaster recovery plans. The contracts behind those status pages leave the data, and on their face the work of recovering it, with the customer, and the filings and insurance markets around them show how thinly the rest of the loss is covered.

On October 10th Yandex moved to "fully manual management of quotas for computing resources and disks."<sup>[1]</sup> Early on Sunday, October 11th, came the third strike: "As a result of the UAV attack, the infrastructure of Yandex's data center in Vladimir was damaged. The data center's operation has been completely suspended."<sup>[5]</sup> In the same notice Yandex told the customers still running on its platform that its "operating mode is emergency" and recommended "using any other alternative disaster recovery plans."<sup>[5]</sup> Fontanka reported that two substations supplying 5,000 residents had been switched off and that people were being evacuated from the homes closest to the site.<sup>[6]</sup> Satellite images published over the following week showed damage across a third of the Sasovo building, its roof blackened at the western end,<sup>[7]</sup> and to at least five of the Vladimir center's buildings.<sup>[8]</sup>

By Monday evening the incident was still open, with 82 services attached to it, the YandexGPT API among them, and the platform "operates in a limited mode."<sup>[5]</sup> Yandex's payments arm spent the weekend reassuring people. "The money itself isn't stored in the data center," said Dima Pirogov, who runs the company's fintech business. "That's where the servers that power our services are located."<sup>[9]</sup> Under Pirogov's own post, people left more than 5,200 comments about mortgages, credit cards and payments they couldn't make, Mediazona found in a roundup of the complaints; the translations are ours. "My mortgage payment is due today, tomorrow I have to pay back the credit card, and I'd like to eat," one wrote. Elsewhere in the roundup, a smart-home user put it more briefly: "I went to sleep in a smart home and woke up in a dumb one."<sup>[10]</sup>

Alexander Savitsky, whose dating app Pulse ran on Yandex Cloud, told Reuters he couldn't reach his backup there. "We have no confirmation of actual data loss, but the situation is serious: the project represents three years of work," he said.<sup>[11]</sup>

Recovery has been partial. Selectel, one of the three rivals Yandex had sent customers to, was soon short of room. By October 14th its status page was headed "Limited capacity" and warned, "we cannot currently guarantee availability or reserve resources"; on October 22nd it stopped the creation of new storage buckets in all its pools, reopening it region by region from the 26th.<sup>[12]</sup> On October 23rd Yandex began opening a replacement availability zone "in a new location," with access rolled out in stages, in our translation of its Russian announcement.<sup>[13]</sup> On October 29th it withdrew its forecast for the year, citing "additional external factors making it difficult to assess its financial results for the remainder of the year." The Moscow Times reported that the financial impact of the strikes "was not reflected in the third-quarter earnings report."<sup>[14]</sup>

Kyiv hasn't issued a formal military claim for the Yandex strikes.<sup>[15]</sup> Russia had been hitting Ukrainian data centers and telecommunications networks for a month, and on October 8th President Volodymyr Zelensky said, in Reuters' translation: "We always respond in mirror-like fashion." The next day he called the Kaluga strike "our tit-for-tat response."<sup>[11]</sup> A private Ukrainian drone maker, Fire Point, has said its FP-1 drones attacked the Vladimir site.<sup>[16]</sup> The campaign then widened beyond Yandex. On October 14th drones hit a Sberbank data center in Balakovo, which Reuters called Russia's biggest and which Marko Milanovic and Michael Schmitt, writing in Just Security, listed as "under construction"; Sberbank said its systems were "operating normally."<sup>[17]</sup><sup>[18]</sup> A VK data center outside St. Petersburg came under attack on October 18th; VK said its services were working normally, though Cybernews reported users seeing outages, with the cause unclear.<sup>[19]</sup> On October 23rd, a Ukrainian monitoring channel reported an attack on a Federal Tax Service data center in Gorodets, one of two duplicate sites for tax and civil registry records. Local officials reported "drone debris" and three injuries, and any damage to the facility hasn't been established.<sup>[20]</sup>

## What the instruments saw

Yandex's account can be checked against data that doesn't depend on either side. IODA, an internet-outage project at Georgia Tech, constantly probes blocks of addresses on every network and counts how many answer. On Yandex Cloud's network, an average of about 518 address blocks responded in the nine days before the first strike. Between the Sasovo and Vladimir strikes the average was 341, about 66% of that pre-strike baseline, and after Vladimir it was 112, about 22%.<sup>[21]</sup> The count stayed near that level for two weeks, then began to climb after October 25th; by November 4th it averaged about 180, roughly 35% of the baseline.<sup>[21]</sup> Those are our figures, computed from IODA's raw ten-minute data; they measure whether addresses answer, not how many workloads ran or what data survived.

The timing matches the status page closely. The Vladimir drop came in two ten-minute steps on October 11th, from 321 responding blocks at 01:40 UTC to 200 at 01:50 and 120 at 02:00, about an hour before Yandex's 03:08 UTC notice.<sup>[21]</sup> The routing data barely moved: the number of address ranges Yandex Cloud announced to the rest of the internet stayed at 857 or 858 through the strikes and the days after them, even though the addresses inside those ranges had stopped answering.<sup>[21]</sup> That pattern fits a facility outage rather than a routing fault, though IODA can't see the cause; the status page names it.

Another chart has been read more loosely. Cloudflare's count of automated traffic from Yandex's main network collapsed after the strikes, and Zelensky said that after them "it suddenly became a little easier to breathe on many social media platforms."<sup>[22]</sup> A researcher who runs the @antibot4navalny account, which tracks Russian online propaganda, told RFE/RL that the chart "has little to do with malicious propaganda bots on social media. It measures automated requests to websites."<sup>[22]</sup> Cybernews pointed out that Cloudflare's figure for bot traffic across Russia as a whole was "largely the same as in any other week."<sup>[23]</sup>

## The Gulf came first

AWS's public health dashboard tells the earlier story in a drier register. On March 1st it reported that one availability zone in its UAE region had been hit by "objects that struck the data center, creating sparks and fire," and that the fire department had shut off power to the facility.<sup>[24]</sup> The next day it was explicit: "In the UAE, two of our facilities were directly struck, while in Bahrain, a drone strike in close proximity to one of our facilities caused physical impacts to our infrastructure."<sup>[24]</sup> AWS never named the attacker. Iran's Revolutionary Guards claimed responsibility and said they targeted the Bahrain facility for Amazon's support of the US military, CNBC reported,<sup>[25]</sup> and Iran's Fars News Agency said the strike was meant "to identify the role of these centers in supporting the enemy's military and intelligence activities."<sup>[26]</sup>

The outage reached the region's payment apps. The payments company Alaan blamed a "critical AWS outage caused by the ongoing regional situation," CNBC reported, listing Abu Dhabi Commercial Bank among the banks that also reported disruptions.<sup>[27]</sup> ADCB's mobile app was down for two days; its chief executive, Alaa Eraiqat, apologized "for the inconvenience experienced over the past 48 hours," but the bank did not say what caused the disruption, and The National reported it was unclear whether the AWS fire was involved.<sup>[28]</sup>

By April 30th AWS was telling Bahrain customers to "recover their resources in other Regions from remote backups."<sup>[24]</sup> On September 15th it gave its conclusion. "After a thorough assessment, we have determined that we are unable to restore access to the resources and data hosted exclusively in this Region," the Bahrain notice said, and the UAE notice said the same of the zone hit on March 1st.<sup>[24]</sup> The damage, AWS wrote, "exceeded what our regional and multi-AZ services are designed to withstand."<sup>[24]</sup> It added that when the first zone was damaged it had begun recommending that customers move their workloads, "and most did so before the Region became unavailable."<sup>[24]</sup>

UNIDIR, the UN's disarmament research institute, called the March strikes "the first documented time that such commercial data centres have been deliberately targeted as part of a military operation."<sup>[29]</sup> That needs one footnote of its own: in February, Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces said they had hit a data center in Russian-occupied Prymorsk, a claim that rests on their own statement.<sup>[30]</sup> In April, Iran's military named the next target. In a video reported by TechCrunch, it said that "if the U.S. went ahead with its threats to hit its civilian infrastructure," Iran would strike back at US "energy and tech infrastructure" in the region, the Stargate AI campus in the UAE among it.<sup>[31]</sup> In October, Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, named data centers, undersea internet cables and AI facilities in the Persian Gulf as targets "if the United States and Israel resume hostilities," Iran International reported.<sup>[32]</sup> The Register drew the comparison on October 9th, noting that if Yandex had to write off Sasovo it would be "the second cloud provider to lose facilities to drone attacks this year."<sup>[33]</sup>

## Who pays

Start with the contract a cloud customer signs. The force majeure clause of the AWS Customer Agreement says that "except for payment obligations," neither AWS nor the customer is liable for failures caused by events beyond their control, a list that ends with "acts of terrorism, or war."<sup>[34]</sup> The same wording was already in the version of the agreement dated December 23rd, 2024, more than a year before the Gulf strikes.<sup>[35]</sup> The same agreement makes the customer responsible for taking "appropriate action to secure, protect and backup your accounts and Your Content."<sup>[34]</sup> The service-level agreement for AWS's compute service, which pays credits when servers go down, excludes outages "caused by factors outside of our reasonable control, including any force majeure event."<sup>[36]</sup>

On the face of those terms, a war excuses the outage while leaving the customer's payment obligations in place and its backups its own job, subject to the rest of the contract and local law. AWS waived charges anyway. In March it emailed UAE customers that it was "waiving all usage-related charges in the ME-CENTRAL-1 Region for March 2026," which an AWS spokesperson confirmed by email in response to questions from CSO, as Network World reported.<sup>[37]</sup> Network World describes the waiver as covering customers of both the UAE and Bahrain facilities, though the email it quotes names only the UAE region.

Since April 30th the dashboard has said that "relevant billing operations are currently suspended" in both regions while AWS restores normal operations.<sup>[24]</sup> And we found no public statement from an AWS customer saying it lost data.

Yandex's next move came through its terms. On October 10th, two days after Sasovo, it updated its user agreement to exclude liability for failures caused by drone strikes and other attacks.<sup>[38]</sup> The new force majeure section lists "military actions and terrorist acts (including with the use of UAVs, missile weapons and other means of destruction)," in our translation of the Russian.<sup>[39]</sup>

The next day it republished the contract for Yandex Cloud, in force from October 22nd. The previous cloud contract already excused "military actions," but it required whoever invoked force majeure to supply "official documents certifying the existence of these circumstances."<sup>[40]</sup> The new one drops that requirement and adds a presumption: "Circumstances expressly listed in clause 11.2 are presumed to be force majeure," and a party disputing that has to prove otherwise.<sup>[41]</sup> Yandex told the Russian outlet RBC that the changes "are clarifying in nature and do not change existing legal mechanisms."<sup>[42]</sup>

Whether such wording holds up is contested. Sarkis Darbinyan, a cyber lawyer, argued in The Moscow Times on November 4th that Yandex's October 10th user agreement doesn't reach obligations breached before that date, and that under Article 428 of the Russian Civil Code a business that accepts a take-it-or-leave-it contract can demand changes to burdensome terms limiting the stronger party's liability. He also argued that a company that wrote drones into its contract will struggle to tell a court the risk was unforeseeable. Courts are split so far: one Moscow arbitration court accepted in June that a drone strike could be force majeure, another rejected a supplier's attempt in May, and neither ruling has entered legal force. It's one lawyer's opinion, written about Yandex, Wildberries and Beget together rather than about Yandex Cloud's contract in particular.<sup>[43]</sup>

Like AWS, Yandex also waived its bills of its own accord: on October 13th it made all its cloud services in Russia "free of charge for existing customers from October 8 through October 31, inclusive."<sup>[5]</sup>

A cloud can also fail without a drone. In the same week as the Sasovo strike, ransomware took down part of the East Japan cloud region of IDC Frontier, a Japanese data-center and cloud operator, affecting 495 companies and local governments.<sup>[44]</sup> When it became clear the platform couldn't be restored, customers such as the blogging-software maker Six Apart began rebuilding from their own backups on a rival's cloud.<sup>[45]</sup> On October 27th IDC Frontier said the attacker had got in through a vulnerability in open-source software it used, and that it had found no sign that data inside customers' virtual servers had leaked.<sup>[46]</sup>

The operators say little about what the damage cost them. In its first quarterly report after the March strikes, Amazon reported $447 million of other operating expense, up from $308 million a year earlier, and said it "was primarily related to charges associated with damaged data centers in the Middle East in Q1 2026, asset impairments, and the amortization of intangible assets."<sup>[47]</sup> It gave no separate figure for the data centers, and its next two quarterly reports dropped the reference: other operating expense came to $90 million in the second quarter and $134 million in the third, put down to asset impairments and amortization.<sup>[48]</sup><sup>[49]</sup> Of nineteen annual and quarterly reports filed since early 2025 by Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle that we read, including the quarterly reports filed in late October, only Oracle's annual report for fiscal 2026 added a new risk-factor sentence on the subject, warning that "physical damage to data centers or key energy infrastructure from natural disasters, geopolitical events, terrorist events or other catastrophic events could adversely affect our profitability."<sup>[50]</sup>

The companies that own the buildings many clouds rent are blunter. Equinix's annual report says its "insurance policies contain industry standard exclusions for events such as war and nuclear reaction."<sup>[51]</sup> Digital Realty's says it does "not carry insurance for generally uninsured exposures such as loss from war or nuclear reaction."<sup>[52]</sup>

Exclusions like these are standard, and the March strikes showed what happens to the cover that does exist. Joe Peiser, who runs Aon's Risk Capital business, told Semafor that before the war, data-center developers could buy up to $3 billion of war coverage for a single Gulf property. "Today the max we could probably do is $100 million," he said.<sup>[53]</sup> In Russia, Darbinyan wrote, Article 964 of the Civil Code exempts insurers from war losses unless the policy says otherwise.<sup>[43]</sup>

Where cover does exist, the wording of its war exclusion matters, and one appellate ruling shows how such a clause can be read. In Merck v. Ace, a New Jersey appeals court held that an exclusion for "hostile or warlike action by a government or sovereign power" "requires the involvement of military action," which let the drugmaker recover its losses from the NotPetya cyberattack.<sup>[54]</sup> A drone strike by a state's armed forces would meet that test far more plainly than NotPetya did, but that assumes the strike can be pinned on a state, and attribution is what's unsettled for the Yandex strikes: Kyiv hasn't issued a formal military claim,<sup>[15]</sup> and a private Ukrainian drone maker, Fire Point, has said its drones attacked the Vladimir site.<sup>[16]</sup> Any real claim would also turn on its own policy wording and facts.

Governments have their own answers. Ukraine's is the one it began in 2022, moving data out of reach. Mykhailo Fedorov, Ukraine's digital transformation minister, said in February 2025 that the country's first cloud-services law "was adopted a week before the start of a full-scale war. It allowed for the transfer of registries abroad and the storage of data in the clouds."<sup>[55]</sup> The government has now set aside "UAH 518.3 million (about US$11.5 million)" for "backup environments at separate locations, cloud infrastructure, and additional sites for state information systems," Ukrainska Pravda reported on October 4th.<sup>[56]</sup> That protects records better than it protects the networks people use every day, and Kateryna Chernohorenko, a former deputy minister of defense for digital transformation, warned that "you cannot move everything underground."<sup>[57]</sup>

In Russia, the state has turned to the military and to the private operators. Maksut Shadayev, the digital development minister, told a State Duma committee on October 12th that his ministry, "together with the Defense Ministry, is working on the issue of protecting these facilities."<sup>[58]</sup> On November 2nd President Vladimir Putin ordered owners of private data centers to "reserve 10% of their capacity" to keep the country's IT infrastructure running, and allowed temporary use of data centers abroad, "subject to approval by the FSB."<sup>[59]</sup>

Who owned and insured the buildings that were actually hit hasn't been disclosed.

## What the law allows

A cloud customer's exposure to a strike depends partly on who else is in the building, and the law of targeting turns on exactly that. At Sasovo, the data center sits on the grounds of the Sasta machine-tool plant, which is on several Western sanctions lists; the governor's statement spoke only of "one of the enterprises," and nobody has said what the drones were aimed at.<sup>[60]</sup> Whether the drones were aimed at the cloud or at the factory around it, the law offers no list of targetable buildings, only a test, and the rules on proportionality and precautions apply after it. Article 52(2) of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions limits military objectives to objects that "by their nature, location, purpose or use make an effective contribution to military action and whose total or partial destruction, capture or neutralization, in the circumstances ruling at the time, offers a definite military advantage."<sup>[61]</sup> Article 52(3) adds a presumption: where it's in doubt whether an object normally used for civilian purposes is contributing to military action, "it shall be presumed not to be so used."<sup>[61]</sup> Russia and Ukraine are both parties to the protocol; the United States and Iran signed it in 1977 and never ratified it, so for the Gulf strikes the question is one of customary law.<sup>[62]</sup>

The hard case is a building that does both. The prevailing view, as the international lawyer Michael Schmitt summarized it in 2019, is that any military use makes a civilian object a military objective, except for parts that are "clearly separate and distinct components."<sup>[63]</sup> Writing with Marko Milanovic in October, he applied that exception to buildings divided into halls: where the servers put to military use sit in a hall that can be separated from the rest, "then only the servers put to military use, and only the hall in which they are located, are military objectives."<sup>[18]</sup> The International Law Association's study group on targeting, quoted in a Just Security analysis after the March strikes, put it more starkly: once an object is used that way, "the entire object becomes a lawful target."<sup>[64]</sup> The International Committee of the Red Cross takes a narrower view. "Not every such use renders ICT infrastructure a military objective, but it may increase the likelihood of it being targeted," Wen Zhou, an ICRC legal adviser, wrote in April, urging precautions "such as separating components used for military purposes from those serving only civilian functions."<sup>[65]</sup>

The explanations on the record differ in kind. Russia's defense ministry said officially that the Ukrainian data centers it struck "process and transmit intelligence data for the Ukrainian Armed Forces."<sup>[66]</sup> For the Gulf strikes, the Revolutionary Guards pointed to Amazon's support of the US military, and Fars to the centers' role in "supporting the enemy's military and intelligence activities."<sup>[26]</sup> For the Yandex strikes Kyiv put no comparable rationale on the record; beyond Zelensky's remarks, the closest is a line in a BBC report that "Kyiv said it was targeting places that provide revenue streams for Moscow's war machine," attributed to no named official.<sup>[67]</sup> After the VK strike, Ukraine's military said VK's assets provided "information support for the Kremlin's policies, as well as for the needs of the Russian army," which Milanovic and Schmitt called "a claim about the corporation, not about the particular facility struck." Weighing the reasons each side gave, they found that Russia's, "if borne out by the facts," would "satisfy a traditional military objective analysis under IHL," though Russia "has offered no evidence whatsoever" for them. "Ukraine's justifications so far do not satisfy such an analysis, even if interpreted charitably," they wrote, adding that Ukraine "may have stronger legal arguments to justify its strikes than it has offered."<sup>[18]</sup> None of these assertions about what the targeted buildings were doing has been backed by public evidence.

Location can count under the test too. Stargate UAE's campus is at "a site near Al Dhafra Air Base," which "hosts U.S. troops," Reuters reported in September.<sup>[68]</sup> On the usual reading of Article 52, being next to a base doesn't by itself make a campus a military objective, just as Sasta's sanctions listing doesn't settle the question at Sasovo.

Workloads count as well. "A data center that is used solely or primarily for military applications is targetable," the international lawyer Ioannis Kalpouzos told The Intercept in March, "and a center that supports the Pentagon's JWCC falls in that category," referring to the Pentagon's Joint Warfighting Cloud Capability contract.<sup>[69]</sup> We found no public record that puts US military workloads in AWS's Gulf regions; AWS's own Defense Department compliance page lists authorizations only for its US regions.<sup>[70]</sup>

Then comes proportionality: whether the expected harm to civilians would be "excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated."<sup>[61]</sup> The open question is which harm counts. Schmitt argued that the analysis need not account for "the temporary disruption or loss of civilian services" that depend on the infrastructure "unless that loss placed civilians at risk of physical harm."<sup>[63]</sup> Shundai Akiyama, a major in the Japan Air Self-Defense Force, argued in September for West Point's Lieber Institute that the analysis can reach past the point of impact, since such a strike can do limited damage on site while "interrupting services used by geographically distant civilian systems." What counts, he wrote, is the resulting civilian death, injury or damage, not the lost service in itself.<sup>[71]</sup>

Opinio Juris framed the shared-tenancy problem in March: "The same data centres providing the digital infrastructure for a country's hospitals might also host its military targeting algorithms."<sup>[72]</sup>

## What was hit, on both sides

When Yandex unveiled its three supercomputers in November 2021, built on Nvidia A100 chips, it said two of them, Chervonenkis and Lyapunov, were in its Sasovo data center and the third, Galushkin, was in Vladimir.<sup>[73]</sup> They are named for Russian scientists; Chervonenkis, the company says, was "one of the greatest theorists of machine learning," and all three are "dedicated specifically to machine learning tasks."<sup>[74]</sup> On October 26th Yandex said the three machines had not been damaged and had been moved out of the data centers that were hit, to new sites it did not name.<sup>[75]</sup> Refitting the sites is another matter: as of October 12th, The Moscow Times reported, a source told Kommersant that delivering replacement equipment to the damaged facilities "could take as long as six months."<sup>[9]</sup>

Russia's strikes on Ukrainian data centers began weeks before Sasovo. After strikes on September 23rd, its Ministry of Digital Transformation said "around 100,000 households in Kyiv and the region experienced internet problems."<sup>[76]</sup> Russia's defense ministry called its campaign a "mandatory digital detox."<sup>[66]</sup> On October 10th Tenet, an internet provider in Odesa, said "Significant damage was recorded" at its data center, in particular to the "cooling systems and power generation equipment."<sup>[77]</sup> On October 19th a Russian ballistic missile strike destroyed Datagroup's data center infrastructure in Kyiv, the company said. Ukraine's prime minister said Russian attacks had damaged "around 10 data centers in Kyiv in September alone."<sup>[78]</sup> "Since September, they have started taking it out systematically," Anatolii Frolenkov of the internet exchange point 1-IX told the Kyiv Independent on September 26th. "There will be outages, there will be problems."<sup>[79]</sup>

## Ten addresses

The same question reaches the AI industry's own buildings. The largest entry by compute in Epoch AI's database of frontier data centers is Colossus 2 in Memphis, Tennessee: by Epoch's estimate, about 1.1 million H100-equivalents of compute and about 950 megawatts of IT power, some 7% of everything the database tracks, in one place.<sup>[80]</sup> Much of the AI industry's computing power sits in a handful of sites like it. The database tracks 96 sites with about 15.8 million H100-equivalents of compute,<sup>[80]</sup> and by our count from Epoch's data, the ten largest of them hold about 43% of that. Epoch estimates that its database covers 45% of the world's deployed AI computing capacity, with a 90% confidence range of 30% to 78%. A rough extrapolation from that puts the ten campuses at around a fifth of the world's AI compute, anywhere from about 13% to 33% on the coverage uncertainty alone.<sup>[80]</sup> Eight of the ten are in the United States, at sites run by Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and the company Epoch lists as SpaceXAI; the other two are DayOne campuses in Johor, Malaysia.<sup>[80]</sup>

Companywide capital spending is still climbing. In their late-October results, Amazon said it expects about $220 billion of cash capital spending this year, Alphabet kept its range of $195 billion to $205 billion, and Meta narrowed its range to $135 billion to $145 billion: $550 billion to $570 billion across three companies, not all of it on data centers.<sup>[81]</sup><sup>[82]</sup><sup>[83]</sup> Alphabet said its spending would "increase significantly in 2027."<sup>[82]</sup>

RAND saw the possibility early. A 2024 report on securing AI model weights warned that "if specific frontier models gain strategic national security importance, one could start seeing foreign military attacks on them as well, as has already occurred with a nuclear power plant in Ukraine."<sup>[84]</sup> The protections written since are mostly aimed elsewhere. RAND's own top security level is built around stopping theft, with measures such as "supervised access for everyone."<sup>[84]</sup>

America's AI Action Plan calls for data centers "resistant to attacks by the most determined and capable nation-state actors," but only in its section on facilities for the military and intelligence community.<sup>[85]</sup> The EU's directive on the resilience of critical entities lists "providers of data centre services" as critical, but routes digital infrastructure to its cybersecurity counterpart, the NIS2 directive, instead of the chapter that sets physical measures such as "perimeter monitoring."<sup>[86]</sup> A Senate bill that would let infrastructure operators counter drones, S. 4380, covers facilities the Department of Homeland Security designates "as a high-risk site," with power plants and grid sites as the minimum; a data center would qualify only if DHS designated it.<sup>[87]</sup> Meanwhile, under a presidential order directing the Defense Department "to lease land to private developers," bases such as Fort Bliss in Texas are being lined up to host hyperscale data centers.<sup>[88]</sup>

Stargate UAE, which Iran's military has already named, is the clearest public case of an AI campus being redesigned with war in mind. Reuters reported in September that the UAE is quietly revising the 5-gigawatt Stargate UAE project into a network of sites spread across the country, with "drone and missile interceptors and electronic jamming equipment" built into the redesign, and that officials are considering putting the facilities that host the most sensitive data, "such as that used by the military, inside mountains."<sup>[68]</sup>

## What to watch

Stargate UAE has its "first 200 megawatts of capacity due to come online this year,"<sup>[68]</sup> and Iran's threats now cover the whole Gulf. In Russia, private operators have until November 12th to set aside capacity the state can call on, and must hand it over within a day of a government subcommission's decision.<sup>[89]</sup> Watch, too, for how the market responds: whether any provider offers a region kept contractually separate from military workloads, in the spirit of the ICRC's advice, or one sold with war cover, and whether any insurer will write it.

Both companies' status pages ended up recommending the same minimum. AWS told Bahrain customers in April to recover from "remote backups." Yandex told its customers in October to use "any other alternative disaster recovery plans." In both cases the advice was to keep a second copy of the data outside the region that was hit.
