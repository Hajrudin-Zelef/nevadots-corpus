The Hangzhou AI lab is offering web search, file parsing, voice and safety checks to third-party builders, anchored by a new deepseek-flash model

DeepSeek just handed developers the keys to the parts of its consumer app that people actually use. On November 13, 2026, the Hangzhou-based AI lab extended the features of the DeepSeek App to its API, so outside builders can now plug them directly into their own products.

## What DeepSeek is shipping

The feature list reads like a tour of the consumer app’s settings menu. Developers can now tap into web search, image search, a deep-thinking search mode, file parsing, URL reading, safety checks and voice functions, all through the API.

At the center of the release is a new model called **deepseek-flash**, identified as V4.1-Flash-1017. It powers these functions across three endpoints: Messages, Responses and Chat Completions.

DeepSeek is also breaking several capabilities out into standalone APIs. Those cover Search, Fetch, OCR, ASR and TTS.

The acronyms deserve a quick decode:

**OCR** (optical character recognition) turns images of text, like a scanned receipt or a photo of a whiteboard, into text a machine can work with. **ASR** (automatic speech recognition) converts spoken audio into words. **TTS** (text-to-speech) runs the other direction, turning written output into a spoken voice.

Search and Fetch round out the set. One finds information on the web, while the other pulls the contents of a specific page.

## The fine print: safety checks and a demo

The rollout comes with one notable condition. Public-facing apps built on the API are required to include safety checks.

DeepSeek also released an open-source frontend demo alongside the API expansion. A frontend is the part of an app users see and click on, so the demo gives builders a working reference for how the pieces fit together.

## What this means for developers and the AI market

The most immediate effect falls on smaller teams. Building a polished assistant that searches, reads files and speaks has traditionally required real engineering resources, and DeepSeek’s bundle could shrink that workload considerably.

The mandatory safety checks are the piece to watch. They signal that DeepSeek wants a say in how its tools appear in public, which may reassure some enterprise buyers while adding steps for builders who just want to move fast.

The things to track from here are practical ones: how quickly third-party apps built on deepseek-flash appear, whether the standalone OCR, ASR and TTS services see uptake independent of the main model, and how developers respond to the safety-check requirement once their products go live.
