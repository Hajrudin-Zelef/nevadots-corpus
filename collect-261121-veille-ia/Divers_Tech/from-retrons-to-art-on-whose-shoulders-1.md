# From retrons to ART:

an enzyme system found by AI agents

In September 2026 Anthropic announced that its Claude agents had found, in vast metagenomic data, a previously undescribed family of reverse-transcriptase systems it calls ART: a reverse transcriptase, a neighbouring partner gene and a long array of roughly 200-nucleotide repeats. The preprint was posted on bioRxiv on 11 October and has not been peer reviewed. According to the paper, the agents ran 949 sessions over about 21.5 hours across 1.9 billion protein clusters; people set the research brief and reviewed the results. What ART does is not yet known.

The finding rests on decades of human work: in 1987 Yoshizumi Ishino and colleagues first saw odd repeats in E. coli that were later recognised as CRISPR; retrons, anti-phage defence systems and the diversity of bacterial reverse transcriptases were sequenced, named and tested one by one. The databases the agents searched are themselves sequences submitted by researchers worldwide over many years. The preprint lists 103 references. We traced the people behind every one.

Some of it is not visible. According to press reports, a University of Copenhagen team led by Mario Rodríguez Mestre says it has studied the same enzymes since 2022 but has not yet published; Anthropic says it knew of no published work. The preprint cites three earlier papers on bacterial reverse transcriptases that Mestre co-authored. It also notes that the 2021 genome report of the phage carrying this locus had already identified its reverse transcriptase, without describing the repeats or the partner gene.

In retrons, for example, the RT copies a dedicated ncRNA into single- stranded DNA that functions with an effector protein in antiphage defense (37, 38).

In retrons the RT and the single- stranded DNA that it produces (msDNA) hold the effector in an inactive complex until phage infection or damage to the msDNA releases it, killing the cell (37, 38).
