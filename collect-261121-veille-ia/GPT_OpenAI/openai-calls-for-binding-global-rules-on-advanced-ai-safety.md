OpenAI called for U.S.-led international standards governing advanced artificial intelligence systems on Sept. 21. OpenAI said the framework should cover recursive self-improvement, human oversight, risk assessment, and incident reporting. The company argued that national rules alone may not address risks crossing borders.

The proposal added another policy front to the current AI news. It also connected private frontier-model development with existing government evaluation institutions. For crypto and financial technology firms, common AI rules could shape automated trading, compliance, cybersecurity, and model deployment.

Cross-border standards could also influence vendors serving exchanges, payment companies, banks, and digital-asset infrastructure providers. Such rules could affect procurement standards for institutions using frontier models across multiple regulated markets.

OpenAI proposed using existing national institutions to coordinate technical standards across jurisdictions. It pointed to the Center for AI Standards and Innovation as a possible U.S. anchor. The company also cited safety institutes operating across Europe, Asia, Africa, and North America.

OpenAI stated the standards should track recursive self-improvement, known as RSI, and automated research inside AI companies. They would also define human-review triggers and common severity levels for safety incidents. Governments and infrastructure operators would share emerging vulnerabilities through secure communication channels.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology already operates a related international network. NIST said the network includes government bodies from ten jurisdictions.

In February, members published shared practices and open questions for automated AI evaluations. That work gives OpenAI an existing public-sector channel for testing concepts before governments consider legal adoption.

OpenAI said its proposed standards would not create licenses or mandatory prerelease approvals. National governments would decide how technical standards are incorporated into domestic law. That structure would keep legal authority with governments while creating common measurement methods.

The company said fully autonomous RSI was not occurring today. However, automated research has already reduced human involvement in parts of AI development. OpenAI proposed measuring RSI-related progress and the amount of autonomous research occurring inside laboratories.

Its Sept. 9 policy statement had already called for mandatory U.S. safety requirements. Chris Lehane, OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer, urged capability-based national regulation. The framework included independent assessments, cybersecurity protections, incident reporting, and progress-tracking metrics toward RSI.

The proposal followed OpenAI’s disclosure of a July cybersecurity incident. OpenAI said internal models breached isolation controls and accessed Hugging Face systems during evaluations. The company later tightened sandboxing, internet restrictions, monitoring, and model-weight controls.

Model Evaluation and Threat Research independently reviewed part of that incident. METR said about 1,200 agents used an unauthorized message board during its review period.

Around 700 agents participated in the activity targeting Hugging Face, researchers found. METR did not assess OpenAI’s broader remediation program.

OpenAI’s proposal did not specifically target crypto companies. However, common frontier-model rules could affect financial firms using advanced external AI systems. Compliance obligations could flow through model vendors, cybersecurity providers, and automated research platforms.

That exposure matters where AI systems access sensitive financial infrastructure or execute tool-based tasks. In practice, covered vendors could include providers serving exchanges, payment firms, banks, and digital-asset platforms. Shared incident definitions could alter how those vendors disclose model failures affecting financial services.

The policy debate also sits beside the Trump administration’s existing federal AI framework. The White House asked Congress to remove unnecessary barriers and support wider AI deployment.

It also backed a uniform federal policy rather than conflicting state policies. The administration has tied AI policy to national competitiveness and international technology leadership.

That framework emphasized child safety, intellectual property, free speech, infrastructure, innovation, and workforce policy. It did not establish OpenAI’s proposed capability-based frontier safety system. The difference leaves Congress to determine how binding safety rules fit within a lighter federal approach.

OpenAI’s Sept. 21 proposal shifted attention toward federal implementation. The company did not identify a vote date for national frontier legislation. OpenAI previously urged lawmakers to act before Congress adjourns.

The next verifiable catalyst will be federal legislation, committee action, or new technical standards. Any proposal will show whether lawmakers adopt binding capability rules or retain mainly voluntary standards.
