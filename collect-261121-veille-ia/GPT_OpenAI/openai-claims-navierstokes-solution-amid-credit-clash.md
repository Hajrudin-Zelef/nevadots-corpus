OpenAI announced a solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem, but mathematicians and rival AI firms contest the originality and attribution of the work.

OpenAI has proclaimed that it has solved the Navier–Stokes problem, the second of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Millennium Prize Problems, following an 88‑hour experiment with a swarm of 10,000 autonomous bots driven by the new Astra model. The announcement builds on months of internal work that used the Codex platform to test conjectures, and arrives at the same time as a parallel effort by Tristan Buckmaster of New York University and Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge, who are also developing their own proofs with Claude and Codex.

OpenAI’s claim sparks a broader debate about how to credit AI‑assisted research in foundational mathematics.

Buckmaster has publicly raised the possibility that OpenAI might have accessed drafts from Codex sessions he and Alpöge shared, potentially using them as training data. He said a conversation with OpenAI mathematician Sébastien Bubeck offered him two options: co‑author a partial development and let OpenAI publish the full solution, or write a solo paper that cites an LLM’s assistance. OpenAI maintains that it did not ingest user data, so the dispute turns on whether shared AI tools contributed to the work.

The controversy highlights questions about data provenance, the legality of using third‑party AI outputs in proofs, and how credit should be assigned in AI‑assisted research. If OpenAI’s claim stands, it would mark the first Millennium problem solved outside a traditional academic setting and deepen existing tensions between AI vendors and the mathematics community.

New developments add urgency and context. On September 10, Anthropic publicly warned that its Claude model detected attempts to generate a guide for weaponising biological agents, demonstrating that safety‑engineering measures can intercept dual‑use requests. The same day, the company announced a >10 % chance, according to a prominent safety researcher, that artificial intelligence could threaten humanity within the next decade, renewing calls for robust safety plans.

Meanwhile, OpenAI’s CEO confirmed that the company will not pursue an IPO in 2026, deeming an initial public offering ill‑advised at present due to safety concerns and the risk of uncontrolled AI development. The decision to keep the firm privately held underscores the broader stakes: breakthroughs powered by AI are arriving faster than the community can assess their rigor or safety implications.

Independent verification of the Navier–Stokes proof remains pending, and the rapid deployment of large‑scale models has intensified the call for transparent, auditable research practices.
