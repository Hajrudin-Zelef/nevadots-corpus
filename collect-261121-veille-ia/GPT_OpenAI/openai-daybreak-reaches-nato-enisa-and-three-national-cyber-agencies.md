Dek: Six European defenders now run models that ship with tighter cyber limits in the consumer app. The results they published are specific, and none of them has been checked from outside.


## Which agencies got OpenAI Daybreak access

OpenAI named the recipients on Tuesday. NATO's Communications and Information Agency, known as NCIA, is using the models. So is the EU's ENISA, along with national cyber agencies in France, Germany and Poland, and Ukraine's Ministry of Digital Transformation for civilian infrastructure.

France's agency is ANSSI, which matters because ANSSI is the body other European agencies copy. When it adopts a tool, procurement teams in smaller member states notice.

This is an access story before it's a technology story. Nothing in the announcement describes a signed framework contract, a price, or a token budget that anyone outside the deal can inspect. OpenAI's language is that it's providing access to the agencies, which leaves the commercial terms where such terms usually sit: undisclosed.

## What OpenAI cybersecurity work means inside Daybreak

Daybreak isn't a chatbot with a security prompt taped to it, at least not in OpenAI's description. Defenders use the models for four jobs: reviewing software for weaknesses, investigating suspicious activity, validating a suspected vulnerability, and developing and testing a fix.

Scope is the part that matters. This is authorized defensive work, run by agencies against their own systems or systems they're cleared to test, not an open assistant anyone can aim at a target. OpenAI applies tighter cyber safeguards in consumer products, and the trade it's making is explicit: fewer limits where a defender answers to a government, full limits everywhere else.

That trade is why the same model family appears in two very different stories this week. In one, Astra ships with a Critical cyber rating and restricted requests. In the other, allies get access for offensive-adjacent defense work under supervision.

## NATO AI at 300 billion tokens a month

The number OpenAI attached to NATO is the one that will travel furthest, and the one most likely to be quoted without the sentence around it. NCIA has used up to 300 billion raw input and output tokens per month in this work, according to the company. That measures how much code and text passes through the model. It doesn't measure how many intrusions were stopped, and the gap between those two things is where most security marketing lives.

Dylan Browne, NCIA's general manager, called it a generational leap in cyber defence. An agency head praising a vendor at a launch isn't evidence, and Browne's quote is no exception to that rule.

The exercise is the more useful detail. OpenAI says it supported Crossed Swords 2026, run by the NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence, which added a training stream where participants built, deployed and evaluated AI agents for cyber operations with human oversight. Teaching officers to run agents under supervision will outlast any single procurement.

## ENISA, CERT-EU and the 95 percent figure

ENISA's case is the most concrete. It used the models to find four vulnerabilities in software used across EU institutions, and all four were fixed and published as CVEs, so the record sits in public view.

Four holes in European government software is a small number. A real one, all the same. Those systems carried the flaws for as long as nobody looked.

The joint report is bigger. ENISA and CERT-EU reviewed 27 software projects this month using frontier models, OpenAI's among them, and asked maintainers to assess what came back. Of 88 potential issues, maintainers confirmed 95 percent as genuine.

That's the strongest evidence in the announcement and the weakest kind of evidence, at once. A maintainer-confirmed rate tells you the findings were real. It doesn't tell you how many real bugs the models missed, because nobody counted those.

How would an outside researcher check it? By asking the same maintainers, which is exactly where the number came from.

## Vulnerability research with a human still in the room

CERT Polska's case reads like a thriller compressed into two sentences. It used OpenAI's models to help find six vulnerabilities in MikroTik RouterOS that were being actively exploited. MikroTik shipped fixes.

Six exploited holes in router firmware is a bad month for the internet, and RouterOS sits in a lot of small offices and school networks across Europe. That case alone justifies the program in the eyes of the agencies involved.

The framing still needs care. Vulnerability research has always been a human craft, and none of this replaces the analyst who decides which finding matters and what happens next. OpenAI and NCIA both describe human oversight as part of the design, which is also a polite way of saying the model proposes and the defender disposes.

## Who checks OpenAI Daybreak's results

Every case study above comes from OpenAI's announcement or from the agency that ran the work. None has been independently audited, and OpenAI's own rating for cyber capability is Critical, which is why consumer products are locked down harder than this deployment.

That tension is the real subject, because governments want frontier models for defense and OpenAI wants distribution to defenders it considers trustworthy. Between those two wants sits a question the announcement doesn't answer: who reviews the findings, and who reviews the reviewer.

Some of the answer is already being built. EU operators in critical sectors report incidents under NIS2, and agencies that file CVEs leave a trail any researcher can follow months later. That trail is the closest thing to an audit available today.

Four vulnerabilities, fixed.

Watch three things. Whether the access list grows beyond the six named bodies, whether any token budget or price becomes public, and whether a third party ever tests the 95 percent figure. Until then, the claims that hold up best are the smallest ones, the ones with a CVE number attached.
