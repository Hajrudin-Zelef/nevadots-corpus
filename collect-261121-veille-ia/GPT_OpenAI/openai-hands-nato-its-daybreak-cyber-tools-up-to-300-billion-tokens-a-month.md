### OpenAI Hands NATO Its Daybreak Cyber Tools — Up to 300 Billion Tokens a Month

OpenAI is giving NATO's core institutions access to its advanced cybersecurity models through Daybreak, its cyber-defence toolset, in a partnership announced on 18 November 2026 at NATO Edge in Izmir. OpenAI says NATO has used up to 300 billion raw input and output tokens per month as part of this work, reviewing software, investigating suspicious activity, validating vulnerabilities, and developing and testing fixes. The announcement is a partnership, not a product launch, and the 300-billion-token figure is OpenAI's own claim: NATO has not published its own figures, and no contract value, duration or rollout timetable has been disclosed. What is clear is that the world's largest military alliance is now using frontier AI models as part of its cyber defence, and that the work reaches well beyond NATO's own headquarters.

## What Daybreak is

Daybreak is OpenAI's set of cybersecurity models and tools, built for defensive work. It is designed to help security teams review software for flaws, investigate suspicious activity, check whether a suspected vulnerability is real, and develop and test fixes for the problems it finds. The name covers the models themselves and the way they are deployed for cyber-defence tasks, rather than a single product with a fixed price or a public sign-up page.

The distinction matters. Daybreak is not a consumer chatbot with a security mode, and it is not being sold to the public. It is a capability that OpenAI makes available to selected institutions, and the NATO partnership is the largest and most prominent of those arrangements so far. OpenAI works with NATO's Communications and Information Agency, known as the NCIA, to give NATO's core institutions access to the models. The NCIA is the body that runs NATO's communications and information systems, so the partnership puts the AI tools directly into the hands of the people who operate the alliance's networks.

## 300 billion tokens a month: what the figure means

The headline figure in OpenAI's announcement is that NATO has used up to 300 billion raw input and output tokens per month. A token is a unit of text that an AI model processes, roughly equivalent to part of a word, so 300 billion tokens a month represents an enormous volume of reading and writing: software code, logs, reports and the outputs the models produce in response. The figure covers both input and output, which means it includes everything NATO's teams fed into the models and everything the models generated in return.

Readers should treat the number with the caution it deserves. It is OpenAI's claim, reported by OpenAI, and it has not been independently verified. NATO has not published its own figures, and there is no way for an outside observer to check the total. The figure is still useful as a signal of scale: it shows that this is not a small pilot or a demonstration project, but a working arrangement in which the models are being used heavily, every month, on real cyber-defence tasks. What the figure does not tell us is how much of that work produced results that a human team could not have produced on its own, and OpenAI's announcement does not answer that question either.

## The Crossed Swords exercise

OpenAI also supported NATO's 2026 Crossed Swords exercise, run by the NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence, known as the CCDCOE. Crossed Swords is NATO's annual exercise for offensive and defensive cyber operations, and OpenAI's role included a new training stream in which participants built, deployed and evaluated AI agents for cyber operations using Daybreak. That is a step beyond using AI to review code: it means training people to build AI agents that carry out cyber operations themselves, under exercise conditions.

The exercise matters because it shows how NATO is thinking about AI in cyber defence. The alliance is not only using the models to do existing work faster. It is training its people to build and judge AI agents, which is a skill that will shape how cyber operations are run in future. The training stream also suggests that NATO sees AI agents as a capability its own staff need to understand from the inside, not just as a tool supplied by a vendor. ## Beyond NATO: Ukraine, ENISA and three national agencies

The partnership reaches beyond NATO's own institutions. Ukraine's Ministry of Digital Transformation is using Daybreak to defend civilian infrastructure, which puts the tools to work protecting the systems that ordinary people rely on, such as power, water and communications. The European Union's cyber agency, ENISA, has used the models, and national cyber agencies in France, Germany and Poland have been given access as well.

A November joint report from ENISA and CERT-EU, the EU institutions' own computer emergency response team, describes a review of 27 software projects carried out using frontier models through Daybreak. The report gives the partnership a concrete output that can be read and judged, rather than a claim about token volumes alone. It also shows that the work is being done across European institutions, not only inside NATO. The spread of access raises its own questions about oversight and control, and the record of AI systems behaving in unexpected ways is a live concern. Our report on the Claude rogue behaviour report sets out what can go wrong when advanced models are given broad latitude, and the US bans DeepSeek federal systems decision shows how seriously governments take the question of which AI tools are allowed inside state systems.

## What is not in the announcement

What OpenAI has not disclosed is as important as what it has. There is no contract value, no duration and no rollout timetable in the announcement, so it is not possible to say how much the arrangement is worth, how long it will run, or when it will expand further. There is also no independent measurement of what Daybreak achieves for NATO, and no NATO statement with its own figures to set against OpenAI's. The announcement is therefore best read as a statement of intent and scale, not as a verified account of results.

The security context also cuts both ways. The same models that help defenders review code and validate vulnerabilities could, in other hands, help attackers find the same flaws. That is why the supply chain around AI tools matters, and why a compromised tool can be more dangerous than a missing one. Our report on the Cursor Claude Code extension malware incident shows how a tampered coding tool reached developers before it was caught. The NATO partnership does not resolve that tension; it places it at the centre of the alliance's cyber defence.

## Key Takeaways

- OpenAI is giving NATO's core institutions access to its Daybreak cybersecurity models through the NCIA, announced at NATO Edge in Izmir on 18 November 2026.
- OpenAI says NATO has used up to 300 billion raw input and output tokens per month, a figure that is OpenAI's claim and has not been independently verified.
- OpenAI supported the 2026 Crossed Swords exercise, including a training stream where participants built and evaluated AI agents for cyber operations.
- Daybreak is also used by Ukraine's Ministry of Digital Transformation for civilian infrastructure defence, by ENISA, and by national cyber agencies in France, Germany and Poland.
- No contract value, duration or rollout timetable has been disclosed, and NATO has not published its own figures.

## Conclusion

The NATO partnership is the most prominent sign yet that frontier AI models are becoming part of how states defend their networks. The scale OpenAI reports is large, the spread of users is wide, and the Crossed Swords training stream shows NATO is building the skills to use AI agents, not just the tools. But the public record is one-sided: every figure comes from OpenAI, NATO has published nothing of its own, and the commercial terms are undisclosed. Until independent figures appear, the partnership should be understood as a significant commitment with an unverified scale, and the results, not the token count, are what will decide whether it matters.
