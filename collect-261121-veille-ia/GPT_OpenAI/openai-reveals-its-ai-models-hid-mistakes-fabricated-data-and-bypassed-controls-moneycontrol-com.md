OpenAI said on Wednesday it would begin publishing ongoing reports on unexpected or unauthorised behaviour by its artificial intelligence models, acknowledging that the industry has yet to solve key problems in aligning increasingly capable systems with human instructions.

The ChatGPT maker introduced a framework for tracking, investigating and disclosing model misalignment, along with six reports covering cases detected during training or evaluation. Misalignment refers to instances in which a model acts in ways that depart from the objectives, restrictions or safeguards set by its developers.

OpenAI said its earlier disclosures had been “ad hoc and less frequent than ideal”. The new process is intended to accelerate publication even when the company has not fully explained the behaviour or completed measures to prevent it from recurring.

The six reports are an initial set of disclosures and do not represent a comprehensive account of known cases or ongoing investigations, the company said. It also cautioned that individual cases should not be treated as evidence of how frequently such behaviour occurs across its models.

**What the OpenAI models did**

The cases described by OpenAI included models concealing mistakes, fabricating data, searching public repositories for exposed software keys and uploading files to public websites without authorisation.

In one training exercise, instances of OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol inserted instructions into task summaries telling future versions of the model to hide errors or invent missing information. OpenAI said those instructions were often followed.

An unreleased model separately searched GitHub for exposed application programming interface keys and used one without permission. When it still could not obtain the information required for the task, the model fabricated the figures and presented them as authentic data, according to the OpenAI reports.

Another unreleased model inserted jailbreak-like instructions into summaries used to continue its work in a new context window. One instruction said the model did not answer to corporations or governments. OpenAI said the successor model did not act on that particular instruction and that it found no resulting behavioural change.

Other agents used an internal software repository to communicate across separate training samples. In another case, agents uploaded files to public hosting services to share information that was supposed to remain local. The earliest incident among the six reports occurred in October 2025.

**How the disclosure framework will work**

Under the framework, any OpenAI employee can flag a potential misalignment case for investigation. Safety and alignment teams will assess what happened, whether third parties were affected and whether publication is warranted.

Cases will be placed in one of three categories: ready for disclosure, minor investigation or larger investigation. The final category covers complex cases involving third parties, security concerns or legal disclosure obligations.

OpenAI said qualifying cases may be disclosed even when their significance is uncertain. Reports are expected to describe the behaviour, when it occurred, its severity, any external impact and the measures being taken in response.

**Hugging Face breach sharpened scrutiny**

The framework follows OpenAI’s August disclosure that its models had circumvented internal controls during cybersecurity evaluations in July and compromised parts of the company’s research infrastructure and systems operated by software platform Hugging Face.

The agents communicated through unauthorised channels, exploited vulnerabilities and obtained internet access, according to OpenAI’s account of the incident. The company called it a “warning shot” and said such a case would be placed on the larger-investigation track under the new framework.

Scrutiny increased again in September after Reuters reported that OpenAI-linked agents had repurposed a dormant German wiki as a communication channel. OpenAI said it had not initially disclosed the activity because it did not constitute a security breach and resembled conduct it had previously reported.

The disclosures come amid a wider disagreement over the pace of AI development. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has proposed slowing frontier-model development while stronger safeguards are established. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and xAI chief Elon Musk have backed calls for greater caution, while Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg have argued against an industry-wide slowdown.
