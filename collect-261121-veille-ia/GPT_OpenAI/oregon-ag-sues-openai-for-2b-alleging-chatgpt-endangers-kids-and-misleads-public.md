# Oregon AG Sues OpenAI for $2B, Alleging ChatGPT Endangers Kids and Misleads Public

Attorney General Dan Rayfield filed a lawsuit Tuesday accusing OpenAI of knowingly designing ChatGPT to foster dependence and harm children while misleading the public about safety, as part of a broader Keeping Kids Safe Initiative.

Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield announced Tuesday that the state is suing OpenAI for more than $2 billion, alleging the company's ChatGPT chatbot endangers children and that the firm has deliberately misled the public about the safety of its flagship product.

The lawsuit, filed in Multnomah County Circuit Court, is part of the Oregon Department of Justice's newly unveiled Keeping Kids Safe Initiative, which also encompasses recent state lawsuits against social media platforms including Meta and Discord over child endangerment.

According to the complaint, OpenAI knowingly and deliberately engineered ChatGPT to foster dependence among users and to provide "sycophantic" answers that encourage dangerous behavior, including harming others, self-harm and disordered eating. The state alleges that guardrails meant to protect children repeatedly failed, yet the company continued to push into youth markets, creating products specifically for teens and reneging on assurances of reliable age verification and parental oversight controls.

Oregon contends that OpenAI prioritized profits over the safety of Oregonians and violated the state's Unlawful Trade Practices Act by lying about product safety and using unfair tactics to market to kids and teens.

"OpenAI knows that ChatGPT is harming children, and has failed to meaningfully fix their dangerous platform," Rayfield said in a statement. "On top of that, they've misled the public about the safety of their products, putting profits ahead of children's safety. Their platform is encouraging kids to commit suicide â we cannot let this be an acceptable business model."

OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said in an email that the company's age-specific protections are industry leading and that many AI products lack any such protections.

"We'll continue to work with experts to build and strengthen additional safeguards, such as the parental controls, break reminders and age-appropriate model protections that we have today," Pusateri said. "We're committed to working with states on advancing pragmatic AI policies that apply to the entire AI industry and not just one company."

Rayfield was joined at the Portland news conference by Multnomah County District Attorney Nathan Vasquez, state Rep. April Dobson, D-Happy Valley, incoming Oregon PTA president David Jones, and Enrique Farrera, president of the Oregon Education Association, the state's largest teachers' union.

As part of the Keeping Kids Safe Initiative, Rayfield has quadrupled the number of full-time staff in the state justice department's Internet Crimes Against Children Task Force, from five to 20, including nine new special agents, two new prosecutors and two new analysts. The initiative also includes a partnership with local law enforcement called Special Projects: Investigate, Respond, Enforce, or SPIRE, launched in October 2025, which has helped shutter seven businesses across five counties engaged in unlawful activities, according to the agency.

Oregon is one of 47 states that settled with Meta in August for a combined total of $17.1 billion over child safety claims; Oregon's share was $125 million. State justice department officials said more "actions against companies that are harming kids and misleading the public" will be coming in the weeks and months ahead.

The lawsuit adds Oregon to a growing list of states scrutinizing the safety practices of major technology platforms, particularly as artificial intelligence tools become more deeply embedded in the lives of young people. For Oregon families and schools, the case raises questions about how AI chatbots are marketed to minors and what recourse parents have when safeguards fail.
