## Oregon points to failures in age checks and parental alerts

In its complaint, Oregon accused OpenAI of violating its Unlawful Trade Practices Act by misrepresenting ChatGPTâs safety for children. The state also alleged negligence, unjust enrichment, and public nuisance, citing inadequate age verification and safeguards against harmful conversations.

Common Sense Media testing cited in the complaint found missing or delayed parental alerts during discussions of self-harm and eating disorders. The tests also raised concerns about age prediction and crisis referrals. OpenAI disputed how the assessment was conducted.

## What the $2.035 billion claim actually covers

Oregon says it has incurred and expects to incur costs addressing harm to residents. The complaint seeks at least $2.035 billion, subject to proof at trial. The figure is a requested amount, not a fine or damages award.

The state also wants court orders against allegedly misleading safety claims, plus effective age assurance, protections against harmful interactions, accurate risk disclosures, and reliable parental alerts. It is requesting civil penalties and legal costs.

The filing follows lawsuits by eight other states in October, which similarly challenged ChatGPTâs safety for minors. Oregonâs case adds a specific multibillion-dollar claim and detailed demands concerning teen protections.

## What parents and school IT teams should check

The lawsuit does not require families or schools to change their accounts today. It does make checking existing protections more useful than assuming they work automatically.

- **Parents:** In eligible personal accounts, check Settings > Account > Age to review the accountâs age category. Explore parental controls and discuss with teens what to do if a chatbot gives harmful or distressing advice. Do not rely on alerts alone.
- **School IT teams:** Inventory which chatbots students use, determine whether accounts are school-managed or personal, and verify which age protections actually apply. Document how students and staff can report dangerous responses.
- **Education leaders:** Ask AI vendors for age-assurance and safety-test documentation, clarify who receives incident alerts, and reassess controls when products change.

For schools, approving an AI tool can be a decision about the assignment as much as the product. An optional brainstorming exercise gives a student room to close the chatbot and ask a teacher for help.

A required assignment that students complete at home may leave them without that option in the moment. If a lesson requires a chatbot, schools can give students an equivalent way to finish the work if they need to stop using it.

Districts can test that fallback before rolling out a tool. Ask teachers to walk through what happens if a student abandons a required chat halfway through an assignment. Can the student switch to another way of completing the work without losing credit or sharing a sensitive conversation to explain why?

A safeguard students can use without worrying about their grade is more likely to help when they need it.
