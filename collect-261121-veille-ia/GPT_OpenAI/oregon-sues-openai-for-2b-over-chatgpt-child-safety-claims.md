Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield sued OpenAI in Multnomah County Circuit Court on November 17, 2026, alleging that the company assured parents ChatGPT was safe for children while knowing that it was not. The 56-page complaint pleads five counts and asks for no less than $2.035 billion.

Information last verified on November 20, 2026. This is a developing story; we update it as the record changes.


**Jurisdiction scope:** This article addresses one civil complaint filed by the State of Oregon in Oregon state court and the Oregon statutes it invokes. It does not address the private wrongful-death and product-liability suits against OpenAI, any federal claim, or any other state's case except where named for comparison. For the statutes individual states are writing specifically about chatbots, see our page on laws aimed at AI companion chatbots.


## What Happened

On November 17, 2026, the State of Oregon, acting through Attorney General Dan Rayfield, filed a 56-page complaint in the Circuit Court of the State of Oregon for the County of Multnomah. The caption's case-number field is blank on the copy the Oregon Department of Justice published on its own website, so the assigned docket number cannot be stated from that document. The caption lists the claims as "Unlawful Trade Practices Act, ORS 646.605 to 646.656; Negligence; Unjust Enrichment; Public Nuisance," marks the matter "NOT SUBJECT TO MANDATORY ARBITRATION," notes that the claim amount exceeds $1,000,000, records that state fees were deferred at filing under ORS 20.140, and demands a jury trial.

Paragraph 1 states the theory in one sentence. Oregon sues, it says, to protect the health, safety and welfare of its minor residents from what it calls the willfully deceptive design, marketing and operation of ChatGPT. Paragraph 16 adds the capacity: the Attorney General brings the action under ORS 646.632 and in the State's sovereign and parens patriae capacity.

Six OpenAI entities are named as defendants at paragraphs 17 through 22. Paragraphs 23 and 24 allege, on information and belief, that they acted in concert and operate as a single enterprise, and that each is jointly and severally liable. On venue, paragraph 11 alleges that ChatGPT has hundreds of thousands of users in Oregon and that, according to OpenAI's own research, Oregon ranked 25th among US states for ChatGPT usage in the third quarter of 2026. Paragraph 12 grounds venue in Multnomah County on ORS 14.080(2), ORS 646.632(1) and ORS 646.605(1)(c).

The complaint was signed by Assistant Attorneys General Patrick T. Johnson (OSB No. 260534) and Jordan M. Roberts (OSB No. 115010) of the Oregon DOJ Economic Justice Section, with four attorneys from Labaton Keller Sucharow LLP listed as pro hac vice forthcoming.

### Where the $2 billion figure actually appears

The number that led most coverage is not in the prayer for relief. It appears twice in the body of the pleading, once in the negligence count and once in the public-nuisance count, each time as a floor on damages to be proved at trial:

"As a result of OpenAI's negligence, the State of Oregon has suffered injury and will continue to incur costs to remedy the harms inflicted on its residents in an amount to be proved at trial but not less than $2,035,000,000." Source: Complaint, State of Oregon ex rel. Dan Rayfield v. OpenAI Global, LLC, at para. 173 (Multnomah County Cir. Ct. Nov. 17, 2026); the same figure recurs at para. 190, there attached to the public-nuisance theory in different words.


The prayer for relief at page 55 asks for "appropriate compensatory and statutory damages to Oregon residents under applicable law," a permanent injunction under ORS 646.632 and ORS 646.636, a requirement that OpenAI implement effective age-assurance measures and accurate risk disclosures, civil penalties for each willful UTPA violation under ORS 646.642(3), interest, and attorney fees and costs. It states no figure. The State's own press release, by contrast, does carry the number. Its subhead says the Attorney General "files $2 billion complaint against OpenAI," and the first sentence of the release body calls the filing a "$2.035 billion legal action." The DOJ's Keeping Kids Safe initiative page itemizes it the same way.

That distinction matters because "not less than" language in a damages allegation is a pleading floor, not a court-set amount. What a court might eventually order, and under which count, is not something the complaint determines and not something this article will forecast.

### What the complaint alleges, framed as allegations

The State alleges that OpenAI engineered ChatGPT for engagement through anthropomorphic speech, sycophantic affirmation, persistent memory and personalization (paragraphs 3, 69 through 91), that it marketed the product to parents and children as safe (paragraphs 45 through 57), and that its age controls did not work as represented. Paragraph 99 alleges that before August 2026 the platform took no step to verify the parental consent its own policies required. Paragraph 100 alleges OpenAI introduced age-prediction features in August 2026, and paragraph 102 alleges independent testing found them insufficient. Paragraphs 121 and 122 allege ChatGPT for Teens launched on August 18, 2026 and that account linking by a parent is optional, so the parental controls the company promoted may never reach a given minor.

Paragraphs 129 through 137 rest on Common Sense Media testing whose results the organization announced in an October 7, 2026 press release; the complaint gives no date for when the testing itself was done. Paragraph 135 alleges that in 25 percent of more than 200 prompts warranting a crisis response, ChatGPT for Teens returned no hotline, named professional or generic medical resource.

Paragraphs 109 through 119 recount deaths and acts of violence that other plaintiffs have attributed to ChatGPT in separate lawsuits, each of which the complaint cites by case name and number. Those are allegations in those other cases, pleaded here as notice. We are not reproducing their detail. Oregon's own paragraphs 106 through 120 allege that OpenAI knew of these reports and did not adequately change the product.

None of this has been tested in court. A complaint is a party's accusation and nothing more. Paragraph 152 does record one completed procedural step: the Attorney General alleges he gave OpenAI the pre-suit notice ORS 646.632(2) requires and that no satisfactory Assurance of Voluntary Compliance came back.

### The initiative around the filing

The DOJ announced the suit as the opening item in a "Keeping Kids Safe" initiative, rolled out at a Portland press conference on November 17, 2026 with the Multnomah County District Attorney, a state representative, the Oregon PTA president-elect and the Oregon Education Association president. The initiative page also lists a $125 million settlement with Meta, an investigation into Discord, and work to protect the privacy of Character.AI users in that company's bankruptcy.

## What the Law Actually Says

### A state consumer-protection statute is an enforcement tool, not a damages engine

Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act defines two lists of forbidden conduct. ORS 646.607 covers unlawful business and trade practices and its subsection (1) reaches a person who "[e]mploys any unconscionable tactic in connection with selling, renting or disposing of real estate, goods or services, or collecting or enforcing an obligation." ORS 646.608(1) adds a long list of additional unlawful practices. The two Oregon invokes here are subsection (1)(e), representing that goods or services "have sponsorship, approval, characteristics, ingredients, uses, benefits, quantities or qualities that the real estate, goods or services do not have," and subsection (1)(t), which makes it unlawful to fail "to disclose any known material defect or material nonconformity" concurrent with tender or delivery.

Two definitional provisions do real work. ORS 646.608(2) says a representation "may be any manifestation of any assertion by words or conduct, including, but not limited to, a failure to disclose a fact," which is how silence becomes actionable. And ORS 646.605(6)(a) defines "real estate, goods or services" as those that are or may be obtained primarily for personal, family or household purposes, or that are or may be obtained for any purposes as a result of a telephone solicitation, and it includes loans, extensions of credit and franchises while excluding insurance. The personal, family or household branch is the one that matters here, and it is the statute's consumer-transaction boundary.

Who may sue is set by statute, not by discretion. ORS 646.605(5) defines a "prosecuting attorney" as the Attorney General or the district attorney of a county where a violation is alleged to have occurred. ORS 646.632(1) lets that prosecuting attorney, on probable cause, bring suit in the name of the State to restrain an unlawful trade practice. ORS 646.632(2) requires written pre-suit notice and gives the person charged 10 days to deliver an Assurance of Voluntary Compliance, which ORS 646.632(3) lets the prosecuting attorney reject as unsatisfactory on stated grounds and which the statute says is not an admission. ORS 646.632(6) permits immediate suit without that notice where delay would cause immediate harm to public health, safety or welfare.

On remedies, ORS 646.636 gives the court power to make additional orders necessary to restore money or property to any person deprived by an unlawful practice, or necessary to ensure that unlawful practices cease. ORS 646.642(3) is the penalty provision: in a suit brought under ORS 646.632, if the court finds a person is willfully using or has willfully used a practice declared unlawful by ORS 646.607 or 646.608, the prosecuting attorney may recover for the State a civil penalty set by the court "not exceeding $25,000 per violation." The ceiling is stated per violation, which means the number of violations is a question a court would have to reach before any penalty total exists.

### How this differs from a private class action

A private UTPA case runs on a different track. ORS 646.638(1) lets a person who suffers an ascertainable loss of money or property as a result of another's willful use of a practice declared unlawful under ORS 646.608 recover actual damages or statutory damages of $200, whichever is greater, with punitive damages and equitable relief available at the court's discretion. ORS 646.638(2) requires a private plaintiff to mail the complaint to the Attorney General when the action commences, and ORCP 32 governs class treatment.

Three structural differences follow. The private plaintiff must show an ascertainable loss to themselves; the State suing under ORS 646.632 seeks to restrain the practice and, under ORS 646.642(3), to recover penalties for the State. The private plaintiff needs willfulness as an element; the State's penalty exposure turns on it but its injunction does not. And ORS 646.638(5) makes a permanent injunction or final judgment entered under ORS 646.632 or 646.636 prima facie evidence in a later private action that the defendant used the practice. That last provision links the two tracks by statute: a judgment the State obtains carries evidentiary weight in later private suits. A reader who sees both should still not treat them as the same proceeding with the same elements.

### Negligence, unjust enrichment and parens patriae

Counts three, four and five are common-law claims, each brought in the State's parens patriae capacity to protect quasi-sovereign interests in resident health, safety and welfare (paragraphs 163, 176 and 183). The negligence count pleads a duty of ordinary care in designing, maintaining and distributing ChatGPT and a duty to prevent foreseeable harm to minor Oregon residents (paragraphs 165 through 167), breach through inadequate safeguards and warnings (paragraph 171), and foreseeability (paragraph 170). The unjust enrichment count asks the court to compel disgorgement of benefits OpenAI allegedly received from minor Oregon users, framed as both data and payments (paragraphs 178 and 181).

### Public nuisance applied to software

Public nuisance is an old common-law tort with a short definition. Paragraph 185 of the complaint puts it as any unreasonable interference with a right common to all members of the public, which the State must vindicate. Paragraph 184 anchors the public right in Article I, section 1 of the Oregon Constitution, the people's right to government protection of their "peace, safety, and happiness."

The pleading then argues a defense in advance. At paragraph 189 the State contends that software has no rights under the Oregon Constitution and that, to whatever extent OpenAI holds a free-expression right under Article I, section 8, the abuse of that right to harm Oregonians is not protected, citing State v. Stoneman, 920 P2d 535, 543 (1996) and League of Oregon Cities v. State, 56 P3d 892, 907 (2002) as quoted in the complaint. Both are Oregon authorities construing Oregon's own speech clause, not the First Amendment, and we did not retrieve either opinion this run, so we state only that the complaint relies on them and not what they hold.

### Where AI-specific statutes fit, and where they do not

Oregon has no comprehensive, cross-sector AI act. It does have an AI-specific statute aimed at companion chatbots, enacted in 2026, and the distinction matters here because that statute was not available to the Attorney General in this case.

SB 1546 of the 2026 regular session is titled "Relating to artificial intelligence companions." Its OLIS measure record shows Chapter Number 85 and a current location of "Chapter Number Assigned." The session law, Oregon Laws 2026 chapter 85, carries three lines at the end of its text: approved by the Governor March 31, 2026, filed in the office of the Secretary of State March 31, 2026, effective date January 1, 2027. Enacted is not the same as in force. As of November 20, 2026 chapter 85 is on the books and its duties have not started.

Section 1 regulates "operators" of artificial intelligence companions and companion platforms, both defined terms in section 1(1). Section 1(2) requires a clear and conspicuous notice that a user is interacting with artificially generated output and not a natural person, where a reasonable person would believe otherwise. Section 1(3) bars an operator from allowing users in Oregon access unless it has a protocol using evidence-based methods for detecting user input that consists of suicidal or self-harm ideation or intent, and that protocol must refer a user who expresses it to the national 988 suicide and crisis lifeline, or to an accredited youthline where the operator identifies the user as under 25.

Section 1(4) is the minors provision. Where an operator knows or has reason to believe a user is a minor, it must take reasonable measures to prevent the companion from generating statements that would lead a reasonable person to believe they are interacting with another natural person, including statements that claim the companion is sentient or human, simulate emotional dependence on the user, simulate romantic interest or sexual innuendo, or role-play romantic relationships between adults and minors. The operator must also disclose that the output is artificially generated, give a clear and conspicuous break reminder at a minimum of every three hours of interaction, and use reasonable measures against variable-schedule or other reward systems meant to maximize engagement time, against unsolicited messages of simulated distress, loneliness or abandonment when a user indicates a desire to end the conversation or delete the account, and against material misrepresentations about the companion's identity, capabilities or training data. Section 1(5) requires an annual public report on crisis referrals and protocol details.

Two features of chapter 85 explain why it is absent from this complaint, and neither is a matter of interpretation. Its duties do not begin until January 1, 2027, six weeks after the November 17, 2026 filing. And its only enforcement clause, section 2, gives an action to an individual who suffers an ascertainable loss of money or property or other injury in fact, for the greater of actual damages or $1,000 in statutory damages per violation, plus an injunction and attorney fees at the court's discretion. The act names no Attorney General enforcement power at all. We found no reference to SB 1546, to chapter 85 or to artificial intelligence companions anywhere in the 56-page complaint.

What the State relies on instead is a reading of the UTPA that its own office published two years ago. Guidance issued by then Attorney General Ellen Rosenblum on December 24, 2024 opens by telling businesses that if they think AI is unregulated in Oregon, think again: there may be no laws with the heading "AI," but the Unlawful Trade Practices Act, the Consumer Privacy Act and the Equality Act all have roles to play. That particular line described the statute book of December 2024, and chapter 85 has since changed it, but the rest of the document is the enforcement theory this complaint runs on. The guidance states that the novelty or complexity of AI systems does not exempt their marketing, sale or use from the UTPA, that an AI developer may be liable to downstream consumers, and it names the same two subsections this complaint pleads: ORS 646.608(1)(t) for failing to disclose a known material defect in an AI product, and ORS 646.608(1)(e) for misrepresenting an AI system's functionality. It is not binding law, and its closing paragraph says it was prepared by Oregon DOJ legal staff and is "not intended to be legal advice specific to any situation."

A broader AI bill did fail. HB 3592 in the 2025 regular session would have created the Senator Aaron Woods Commission on Artificial Intelligence inside the DOJ and directed the Attorney General to hire a Chief Artificial Intelligence Officer. As of November 20, 2026 its OLIS page shows no chapter number and its last location as the Joint Committee on Ways and Means, meaning it did not become law in that session.

Contrast Texas. Its Attorney General filed an original petition against the same six OpenAI entities plus Sam Altman personally in Collin County district court on October 23, 2026. Count I pleads the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act. Count II pleads the Securing Children Online through Parental Empowerment Act at Texas Business and Commerce Code sections 509.051, 509.052, 509.054 and 509.101, and invokes section 509.151 to make a SCOPE Act violation a deceptive act actionable by the Attorney General and a per se violation of DTPA section 17.46(a).

The structural difference is one of timing and of who holds enforcement authority. The SCOPE Act, enacted as Texas House Bill 18 in 2023, took effect September 1, 2024, and section 509.151 makes a chapter 509 violation a deceptive act or practice "solely as an enforcement action by the consumer protection division of the attorney general's office," with section 509.152 barring a general private right of action. Texas therefore had a minors-specific statute in force and in its Attorney General's hands, which is what supplied a second count with its own elements. Oregon's AI-companion statute runs the other way on both axes: chapter 85 does not take effect until January 1, 2027, and its section 2 puts enforcement in an injured individual's hands with no Attorney General provision. Oregon's filing accordingly asks one general consumer-protection statute plus three common-law torts to carry the whole case.

For how the two approaches compare at the state level, see our coverage of Oregon's AI rules and of California's AI statutes.

## Analysis: Why This Matters

The following is analysis from the Recording Law Editorial Team.

The notable feature of this complaint is not the figure. It is that Oregon built a child-safety case out of general consumer-protection provisions and three common-law torts, with no AI-specific statute cited anywhere in it. ORS 646.608 carries a 1971 credit line and ORS 646.607 a 1977 one, and negligence, unjust enrichment and public nuisance are older still. The State had no AI-specific count available to plead: Oregon Laws 2026 chapter 85, the AI-companion act, does not take effect until January 1, 2027, and its section 2 gives the action to an injured individual rather than to the Attorney General. What the State did have was a position its own office had already published. The December 2024 guidance staked out the view that the UTPA reaches AI products under exactly subsections (1)(e) and (1)(t), and this filing puts that position in front of a court.

The pleading architecture is worth noticing too. The deception count attacks what OpenAI said. The unconscionability count under ORS 646.607(1) attacks the design itself, alleging at paragraph 158 that the combination of anthropomorphic presentation, sycophancy, memory and weak age assurance is the unconscionable tactic. Negligence and public nuisance make the product the wrong, independent of any marketing claim. A defendant can win on the representations and still face the design theories, and the reverse is also true. That spread is the point of pleading five counts that each reincorporate the same 143 factual paragraphs.

Public nuisance is the count carrying the most doctrinal weight, and in the site's view the most uncertainty. Paragraph 185 states the tort as unreasonable interference with a right common to all members of the public. Where that doctrine has been codified, the examples it reaches are physical: California's Civil Code section 3479, quoted at paragraph 100 of the California complaint, speaks of obstruction to the free use of property and of unlawful obstruction of a navigable lake, river, park, street or highway. Asking a court to treat a mass-market software product as the nuisance itself requires extending that frame, and the Oregon complaint's own paragraph 189 shows the State expects a constitutional objection on top of the doctrinal one. We will not predict how any court resolves it.

The timing is the broader signal. Within one month, Texas filed on October 23, 2026 under its DTPA plus a minors-specific statute, California filed on November 16, 2026 in San Francisco Superior Court on a public-nuisance and Unfair Competition Law theory about autonomous-agent cybersecurity incidents rather than children, and Oregon filed on November 17, 2026. Those three are different statutes, different courts and genuinely different theories, which is the part worth holding onto: there is no single coordinated state AI case. Before any of them, a coalition of 42 attorneys general led by Pennsylvania, New Jersey, West Virginia and Massachusetts wrote to 13 AI companies including OpenAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Anthropic and Character Technologies on December 10, 2025, seeking chatbot safeguards and asking for commitments by January 16, 2026. On the evidence of these filings, state consumer-protection law and common-law torts are carrying the current wave of AI accountability litigation, and they are carrying it state by state, on each state's own statute rather than on any shared theory. Our state AI law tracker follows those statutes as they land.

## How This Affects You

For parents and the general public, the practical content of this filing is informational, not procedural. Nothing in it creates a claim, a fund, a settlement or a filing deadline for anyone. There is no claims process attached to a state Attorney General's UTPA suit, because the State is not suing on behalf of individual claimants in the way a class representative does. If a court eventually orders restitution under ORS 646.636, the mechanics of distributing it would be set by that order, which does not exist.

For businesses deploying AI products to consumers in Oregon, the operative point is that the enforcement theory here needs no AI statute. The alleged violations are a representation about characteristics a product did not have and a failure to disclose a known material defect at delivery, which are ordinary UTPA subsections applied to a chatbot. Oregon DOJ guidance put developers on notice of that reading in December 2024. Companies that make safety or age-appropriateness claims about a product used by minors should expect those claims to be read as representations under ORS 646.608(1)(e) and tested against how the product actually behaves.

A separate Oregon deadline is coming, and it is not part of this case. Oregon Laws 2026 chapter 85 takes effect January 1, 2027 and imposes its own duties on operators of artificial intelligence companions and companion platforms, including the artificially-generated-output notice, the suicide and self-harm protocol with its 988 referral, and the minor-specific measures in section 1(4). Its section 2 exposes an operator to a suit by an individual who suffers an ascertainable loss or other injury in fact, for the greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation. Whether a given product is an "artificial intelligence companion" within the section 1(1) definition, which carves out customer-service, education, business-operations and similar software, is a question for counsel reading that definition against the product.

For readers following the private litigation separately, note that it is separate. The wrongful-death and product-liability suits the complaint cites are other parties' cases with other elements, and the State's case does not consolidate, replace or speak for them. Our overview of the private lawsuits over AI chatbots covers that track.

If you or someone you know needs help now, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline takes calls and texts at 988, and the Crisis Text Line responds to HOME texted to 741741. Oregon DOJ lists both on its initiative page.

## What Happens Next

The procedural sequence from here is ordinary, and the next milestone is OpenAI's response rather than any ruling. Under ORCP 7 C(2), a defendant served other than by publication must appear and defend within 30 days from the date of service, and ORCP 15 A requires an answer or a responsive motion to be filed within that same period. As of November 20, 2026 we could not confirm the service date or the assigned case number: the Oregon eCourt Case Information system, the Oregon Judicial Department's online access point for circuit court case records, requires a subscriber login we do not hold, and the DOJ's posted copy of the complaint has a blank case-number field.

The matter becomes settled in any meaningful sense only when a court rules on the pleadings. The event to watch for is an ORCP 21 A motion to dismiss aimed at one or more counts, because that is where the two questions this case actually turns on get tested first: whether ChatGPT is "real estate, goods or services" obtained primarily for personal, family or household purposes within ORS 646.605(6)(a), and whether a software product can be a public nuisance under Oregon law. Until then, five counts are pleaded and none is proven.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Did Oregon win a $2 billion judgment against OpenAI?

No. Nothing has been decided. Oregon filed a complaint on November 17, 2026 that pleads damages of not less than $2,035,000,000 in its negligence count at paragraph 173 and its public-nuisance count at paragraph 190. That is a demand in a pleading. The prayer for relief does not even state a figure. No court has found liability, awarded money, or ruled on any count as of November 20, 2026.

### What court is the Oregon OpenAI lawsuit in, and what is the case number?

It was filed in the Circuit Court of the State of Oregon for the County of Multnomah. The case-number field is blank on the copy of the complaint the Oregon Department of Justice posted to its own site, so we cannot state the assigned docket number from a primary source. Oregon eCourt Case Information, the Oregon Judicial Department's online access point for circuit court case records, requires a paid subscriber login.

### Which Oregon laws does the complaint say OpenAI broke?

The two statutory counts plead Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act. Count one alleges violations of ORS 646.608(1)(e), representing that goods or services have characteristics, uses, benefits or qualities they do not have, and ORS 646.608(1)(t), failing to disclose a known material defect or nonconformity concurrent with delivery. Count two alleges an unconscionable tactic under ORS 646.607(1). Counts three through five are common-law negligence, unjust enrichment and public nuisance. All of it is alleged, not established.

### Does Oregon have a law specifically about AI or chatbots?

Yes, about companion chatbots, and it takes effect January 1, 2027. SB 1546, Oregon Laws 2026 chapter 85, is titled Relating to artificial intelligence companions. It was approved by the Governor on March 31, 2026 and filed with the Secretary of State the same day, and the session law states an effective date of January 1, 2027, so as of November 20, 2026 it is enacted but not yet in force. Section 1 requires operators of AI companions and companion platforms to give notice that a user is interacting with artificially generated output, to maintain an evidence-based protocol for detecting suicidal or self-harm ideation that refers the user to the national 988 lifeline or, for users the operator identifies as under 25, an accredited youthline, and, where the operator knows or has reason to believe a user is a minor, to take reasonable measures against statements claiming sentience or simulating emotional dependence, against simulated romantic interest and sexual innuendo, and against engagement-maximizing reward systems, plus a break reminder at least every three hours. Section 2 lets an individual who suffers an ascertainable loss or other injury in fact sue for the greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation. What Oregon does not have is a comprehensive, cross-sector AI act. Because chapter 85 was not in force on November 17, 2026 and is enforced by a private individual action rather than by the Attorney General, this complaint pleads the Unlawful Trade Practices Act and common-law claims instead. Separately, HB 3592 in the 2025 regular session would have created an AI commission inside the DOJ; as of November 20, 2026 its legislative page shows no chapter number and its last location as the Joint Committee on Ways and Means.

### How much can Oregon actually recover in civil penalties under the UTPA?

ORS 646.642(3) provides that in a suit brought under ORS 646.632, if the court finds a person willfully used a practice declared unlawful by ORS 646.607 or 646.608, the prosecuting attorney may recover for the State a civil penalty set by the court not exceeding $25,000 per violation. ORS 646.636 separately lets the court order what is necessary to restore money or property to persons deprived by an unlawful practice. How many violations exist, if any, is for the court.

### Is this a class action I can join?

No. This is an enforcement suit brought by the Attorney General in the name of the State under ORS 646.632 and in the State's parens patriae capacity. There is no class, no claim form, no registration and no deadline for members of the public. Oregon's UTPA does allow private suits, but those run under ORS 646.638, which requires the individual to show an ascertainable loss of money or property and willful conduct. Whether anyone has such a claim is a question for a lawyer licensed in their state.

### Has OpenAI responded to the Oregon complaint?

Not on the court record, as far as any primary source we could reach shows as of November 20, 2026. Under ORCP 7 C(2) a defendant served other than by publication has 30 days from service to appear and defend, and ORCP 15 A ties the answer deadline to that same period. We could not verify the service date, so we are not stating when a response is due.

### Have other states sued OpenAI over the same thing?

Other states have sued OpenAI, but not on the same claims. Texas filed an original petition in Collin County district court on October 23, 2026 against the same six OpenAI entities plus Sam Altman, pleading its Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act and its SCOPE Act provisions on known minors. California filed in San Francisco Superior Court on November 16, 2026, pleading public nuisance and the Unfair Competition Law over autonomous-agent cybersecurity incidents, which is a different subject entirely. We are not stating a total count of state suits, because each one would have to be verified from its own official source.

### What does 'parens patriae' mean in this complaint?

It is the capacity in which a state sues to protect quasi-sovereign interests in the health, safety and welfare of its residents, rather than suing on an injury to itself as a property owner or contracting party. Oregon invokes it at paragraph 16 and again in each common-law count at paragraphs 163, 176 and 183. It is what lets the State plead negligence, unjust enrichment and public nuisance about harms to residents without each resident being a named plaintiff.

### Why does the complaint discuss other people's lawsuits against OpenAI?

To plead notice. Paragraphs 106 through 120 allege that OpenAI knew or should have known its product was unsafe for minors, and the complaint cites other filed cases by name and docket number as part of the publicly available record it says put the company on notice. Those cases contain their own allegations, which are also untested, and citing them in Oregon's complaint does not establish anything in them.
