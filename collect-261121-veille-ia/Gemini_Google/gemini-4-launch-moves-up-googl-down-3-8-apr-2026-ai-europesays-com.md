What Kavukcuoglu Said About the Gemini 4 Launch

Three claims sit at the center of the story. First, Gemini 4 has entered the early stages of post-training, the phase where a base model gets refined so it behaves reliably. Second, Kavukcuoglu expects the model to launch much earlier than the end of 2026. Third, Google plans to put out an early post-training output rather than wait for a fully polished release.

His own words on the third point, as carried by The Verge, were: “Our intention is to, like, as soon as possible, to release an early post-training output because we see the results and we are excited.” He added that Google DeepMind would “continue the fast-paced iterations” once that first output is out. That phrasing signals a staged rollout, with a rough first release followed by quick updates, instead of one big launch day.

Who Said It: Kavukcuoglu, Not Hassabis

Some headlines on this story credit Demis Hassabis. That attribution is wrong. The Verge and The Decoder both identify Koray Kavukcuoglu as the speaker, and both describe the summit appearance as his first public outing in the new role. Hassabis stepped down as DeepMind CEO in August, according to those reports.

The Decoder adds that Kavukcuoglu, previously the CTO, runs day-to-day operations without the CEO title, and that Hassabis now chairs the unit and serves as Alphabet’s chief scientist. The same report says all Gemini development is moving to the Bay Area. That detail matters for reading the speed pledge. A division that treats Gemini as a product line, with a product chief at the helm, will talk about shipping dates differently than a lab built around research milestones.

The shift in tone showed up in how Kavukcuoglu handled the AGI question. He told the audience that whether the field has achieved AGI is “not the right conversation,” and steered toward whether labs can build intelligent agents people can trust. His predecessor had spent years framing DeepMind’s mission around AGI. The change in vocabulary tells you where the incentives now sit.

What Early Post-Training Means for Gemini 4

Frontier models move through two broad stages. Pre-training builds the base model from huge datasets. Post-training then shapes that base into something usable, through supervised tuning, preference learning, safety work and tool-use training. Stocktwits, in the piece syndicated on Yahoo Finance, described post-training as the process of refining a base model for reliability and performance.

Why “early” is the operative word

Kavukcuoglu did not promise a finished model. He promised an early post-training output, which implies a checkpoint that Google considers good enough to show users while the work continues. Labs have used this pattern before, shipping a preview and then tightening behavior over several weeks. The risk is obvious. Early checkpoints can ship with rough edges in tool calling, refusals and long-task stability, exactly the traits that agent users notice first.

What the reports leave out

None of the named outlets gave a parameter count, a training-compute figure or a benchmark number for Gemini 4. Some aggregator pages quote precise pre-training dates and durations. We could not tie those to a named primary source, so we leave them out. The same rule applies to any leaked score that circulates before Google publishes a model card.

Why Google Needs a Gemini 4 Launch Now

The urgency comes from the calendar. The Verge noted that Google has not released a new flagship since the Gemini 3 series in November 2025. The Decoder filled in the gap: Gemini 3.1 arrived in February, followed by several smaller and cheaper Flash models. CEO Sundar Pichai announced Gemini 3.5 Pro at I/O in May for a June release, yet it never shipped. The Decoder, citing the Financial Times, reported that the project missed three deadlines.

Kavukcuoglu acknowledged the detour. He said the company “took a little bit of a step back” and prioritized faster Flash models. He did not say whether Gemini 3.5 Pro is officially canceled. When asked about falling behind, he answered, “In my mind, it’s a certainty that we are always gonna be at the frontier.” That is a confident statement from an executive whose company has no new flagship on the shelf, and readers should weigh it as a goal, not a result.

Market Impact: Alphabet Shares and the Agent Trade

The market reaction gives the clearest read on how much weight investors put on the Gemini 4 launch. Stocktwits reported that Alphabet closed 3.8% lower in the regular Wednesday session, its worst drop in more than a month, and lost nearly 5% across two straight sessions. After Kavukcuoglu’s remarks, GOOGL rose 0.6% overnight. Retail sentiment on the platform improved over the week and read “extremely bullish” late Wednesday, though some traders argued the Muse threat was overstated.

Those numbers describe one week in September, not the current price, so don’t read them as a November quote. They do show how a single timing comment from a research chief can move sentiment on a megacap. The broader backdrop has stayed jumpy. Our coverage of the AI sell-off that hit the Nasdaq shows how quickly sentiment on AI names can flip, and a delayed or underwhelming Gemini 4 would feed that nervousness.

Capital intensity sits underneath all of this. OpenAI’s reported $11 billion raise at a $1.25 trillion valuation shows how much money rivals are pulling in, and Google is spending to keep pace on infrastructure too, including its plan around a $17 billion Wyoming data center site. The Decoder also reported that Google began selling its TPUs directly to customers this year, and that Kavukcuoglu said new models show hardware engineers how to design the next two or three generations of chips. A strong Gemini 4 therefore carries a hardware angle, not just a software one.

Competitive Comparison: Google, OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta

Rivals moved first. Stocktwits reported that Anthropic unveiled Claude Fable 5.1 on September 1 and OpenAI launched GPT-6 Astra on September 3. The Decoder wrote that Anthropic released its Mythos and Fable family in the spring and updated it, along with the Opus line, in September, and that OpenAI has been rolling out GPT-6 models since early September. The Verge said both outperform Gemini 3 and give those providers a sizable lead.

Meta plays a different game. Its Muse line spans a model tier (Muse Spark, Muse Wonder) and a product tier (the Muse agent), and it ships on a weekly rhythm. Google has the opposite profile. It has reach through Search, Android and Workspace, plus the TPU business, but a flagship gap. Anthropic’s path to public markets adds another pressure point, which we covered in our look at the Anthropic IPO and its 22 banks.

The takeaway: Google’s weakness is cadence, not capability. The Decoder noted that Gemini 3 was such a leap that OpenAI declared a “Code Red” and only pulled level again with GPT-5.2. If Gemini 4 repeats that pattern, the shipping gap will matter less. If it lands as a modest step, the gap widens, because Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta all keep moving.

Historical Context: From Code Red to Gemini Factory

DeepMind’s role inside Alphabet has changed twice in four years. After ChatGPT arrived, Google merged Google Brain and DeepMind into Google DeepMind during its first “Code Red,” according to The Decoder, and the Gemini name came from that merger. The London team kept a special status for years. The August reshuffle, with Kavukcuoglu running operations and Gemini development moving to the Bay Area, ends that era in the eyes of the report’s sources.

The Decoder quoted a former Google executive, speaking to The Guardian, saying the era of DeepMind as an independent actor is over. It also cited the Financial Times on executive frustration that Hassabis gave commercial needs less weight, with the open release of AlphaFold as one example. The report called it unclear how voluntary Hassabis’s exit was, and said Google had not commented on claims from an industry newsletter.

Read against that history, the Gemini 4 pledge is a product-org statement. The company that built AlphaFold now measures itself by release cadence against OpenAI and Anthropic. Whether that shift speeds up good research or squeezes it is a debate, and The Decoder framed the change as complete.

DeepMind’s Leadership Reset and the Talent Question

Departures reported around the reset

The Decoder listed a string of exits it attributed to the talent drain: Noam Shazeer to OpenAI in June, AlphaFold Nobel laureate John Jumper to Anthropic, and Gemini researchers Jonas Adler and Alexander Pritzel to Anthropic as well. It also cited Fortune for a hiring statistic. DeepMind’s ratio of new hires to departures fell from about 12 to 1 in the second quarter of 2023 to roughly 2 to 1 in the third quarter of 2026.

Why it bears on the launch date

A launch promise depends on the people who train and tune the model. Post-training in particular rewards experience, since small choices in data mixes and reward design change how a model behaves in agent loops. Kavukcuoglu told the audience he is fully confident in his team, and The Decoder quoted him calling it insanely strong. Outside observers can’t test that claim until a checkpoint ships, which is another reason an early release helps Google: it replaces assurances with something users can run.

What Developers and Buyers Should Do Now

Practical advice starts with a plain rule: plan around what is shipping. If you build on the Gemini API today, keep your integration tied to the current model versions and abstract the model name behind configuration, so a swap to a new Gemini 4 checkpoint takes a config change and an eval run, not a rewrite. Google’s own Gemini model page is the place to confirm official availability once a release happens.

Teams should also build a small regression suite before any new checkpoint arrives. Pick 30 to 50 prompts that match your workload, record current outputs and scores, and rerun them the day a new model appears. Early post-training outputs can shift behavior, so a repeatable test beats first impressions. Our blind-test method for ranking AI chatbots shows one way to run that comparison without brand bias.

FAQ: Gemini 4 Launch, Kavukcuoglu and Meta’s Muse

When is the Gemini 4 launch date?

Google has not announced one. Koray Kavukcuoglu said the launch should come much earlier than the end of 2026, and that Google wants to release an early post-training output as soon as possible. That is a stated intent, not a scheduled date.

Who announced that Gemini 4 is coming earlier?

Koray Kavukcuoglu, who leads Google DeepMind. The Verge and The Decoder both name him, and both say the remarks came at The Information’s AI Agenda Live Summit. Some headlines credit Demis Hassabis, but that attribution is not supported by the cited reports.

What does early post-training mean?

It is the refinement stage after a base model is built. Stocktwits described it as the process of refining a base model for reliability and performance. Google plans to release an early output from this stage and keep improving it in quick iterations.

Is Gemini 4 a response to Meta’s Muse?

That is unconfirmed. Google has not tied Gemini 4 to Muse. The link comes from financial coverage, where Stocktwits tied Alphabet’s September selloff to investor worry about Muse and then noted Kavukcuoglu’s comments as a new data point.

What happened to Gemini 3.5 Pro?

Pichai announced it at I/O in May for a June release, but it never shipped. The Decoder, citing the Financial Times, reported that the project missed three deadlines. Kavukcuoglu said Google took a step back to focus on faster Flash models, and he did not say whether the model is canceled.

What do we know about Gemini 4 specs and pricing?

Nothing confirmed. No named outlet has reported a model size, context window, benchmark score or price. Treat any figure you see before an official Google release as unverified.

Should I wait for Gemini 4 before choosing an AI assistant?

No. With no date and no confirmed specs, a purchase decision should rest on what ships today. If you build on an API, keep your model choice configurable so you can test a new checkpoint the day it appears.

How is Muse different from a model like Gemini 4?

Muse is Meta’s personal AI agent, a product that completes tasks on a user’s behalf, according to Meta’s newsroom posts. Gemini 4 is a model. The fairer comparison pairs Gemini 4 with Meta’s Muse models and Google’s agent surfaces with the Muse app.
