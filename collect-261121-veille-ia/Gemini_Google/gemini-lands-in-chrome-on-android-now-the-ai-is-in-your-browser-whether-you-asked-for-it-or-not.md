Google has finished rolling out Gemini in Chrome to all US Android users. The assistant reaches into bookmarks, calendar and email, while paying subscribers get agentic features too — use at your own risk.


Image by Qwen for abit.ee

Back in May, Google promised "some" Android users access to Gemini right inside Chrome. The promise held up — mostly. The feature has now rolled out to every Android user in the US, and dodging it has gotten noticeably harder.

The pitch sounds nice enough: ask the AI something about the page you're on, pull a recipe's ingredient list straight into Google Keep without leaving the tab. Found a concert? Gemini adds it to your calendar and emails the ticket to a friend on its own. All of it opens with one tap on the Gemini icon or the three-dot menu in the toolbar corner.

And that's where the real question sits: did anyone actually ask users whether they want their browser continuing to "understand" whatever is on their screen? Google evidently decided asking wasn't necessary — access to Gmail, Calendar and Keep gets switched on through the Personal Intelligence section, and how voluntary that "switching on" really feels is anyone's guess.

A separate story entirely is AI Pro and AI Ultra subscribers. They get the agentic auto browse feature: the browser clicks links, fills out forms, and completes multi-step tasks on its own, stopping just short of payment or posting on social media. As an example, Google showed the AI independently picking out a personalized dog bowl on Chewy.com — a rousing vision of the future, if ever there was one.

Technically, it all looks tidy enough: the current page is added to the prompt's context automatically, though that can be turned off in settings. Requirements are modest — Android 12 or higher, at least 4GB of RAM, English-language device settings. The feature only works in the US for now — the rest of the world waits its turn, as usual.

Chrome commands roughly 63% of the global browser market, and Android runs on about 70% of the planet's smartphones. In other words, Google is dropping its assistant into the pockets of hundreds of millions of people in one move, many of whom never asked for an AI assistant — it just showed up. Rivals like Microsoft with Copilot in Edge, or Apple's plans for Safari, clearly aren't planning to ask permission either.
