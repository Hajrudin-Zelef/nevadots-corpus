## Analysis

Google expanded Gemini in Chrome to all US Android users this week, adding a customizable Gemini shortcut to the browser toolbar that can summarize articles, answer questions about the current page, and connect to Calendar and Keep, Droid Life reported. Google separately introduced Admin Assist, two new Gemini-powered capabilities in the Google Admin Console aimed at simplifying enterprise IT troubleshooting.

The consumer-facing headline feature is Auto Browse, an agentic mode built on Gemini 3 that can handle multi-step browsing tasks -- researching prices across sites, filling out forms, managing subscriptions -- without a user clicking through each step manually. It's rolling out in preview, US-only, gated to Google AI Pro and AI Ultra subscribers; the free Chrome tier gets the always-available side panel and page summarization, not the autonomous agent.

## The browser-agent field just consolidated, hard

This expansion lands days after two competitors moved in the opposite direction. OpenAI is winding down its standalone Atlas browser following a July 9 desktop consolidation, and has replaced it with a ChatGPT extension for Chrome rather than continuing to compete as an independent browser. Microsoft, meanwhile, retired its "Copilot Mode" branding in Edge in favor of "Browse with Copilot," which lets the assistant act directly in-browser -- selecting, typing, navigating across open tabs -- but restricts that action-taking capability to Microsoft 365 Premium subscribers on desktop, US-only. Everyone else using Edge gets summarization and multi-tab reasoning without the hands-on-the-page agent.

“A company running Google Workspace gets Gemini in Chrome as part of an existing relationship; a company on Microsoft 365 gets Browse with Copilot the same way.”


The pattern across all three companies is identical: a free tier that summarizes and answers questions, and a paid tier that actually takes autonomous action in the browser. None of the three majors have made the fully agentic experience free, which suggests the compute cost of an agent that clicks, types and navigates on a user's behalf -- not just generates text -- is still expensive enough that none of them wants to give it away.

OpenAI's retreat from a standalone browser is the more interesting signal than Google's expansion. A dedicated AI-native browser was, a year ago, treated as a plausible new default-app category the way the mobile browser was in the smartphone era. OpenAI trying and abandoning that approach in favor of a Chrome extension is evidence that browser habit and default-search-engine economics are harder to disrupt than model quality alone can overcome -- Chrome's install base and Google's default-search deal revenue give Google a distribution moat no standalone competitor can match by shipping a better agent.

For enterprise IT buyers, the practical near-term decision isn't which agent is smartest -- it's which one is already licensed. A company running Google Workspace gets Gemini in Chrome as part of an existing relationship; a company on Microsoft 365 gets Browse with Copilot the same way. The agentic browser war is shaping up to be won on existing seat licenses, not on head-to-head model benchmarks.
