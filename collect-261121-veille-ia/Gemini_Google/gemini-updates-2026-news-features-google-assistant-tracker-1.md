LegalEU court adviser: Gemini's real-time reuse of press articles can infringe publishers' rights

The Court of Justice of the EU published Advocate General Maciej Szpunar's opinion in case C-250/25 on 3rd Nov. The press release calls it "the first generative AI case to reach the Court of Justice".

Like Company, a Hungarian web-portal operator, sued Google Ireland in Budapest. It says Google "repeatedly reproduced and made them available to the public through the Gemini chatbot between 13 June 2023 and 31 May 2024".

The adviser proposes two factors for the publishers' right. First, "whether that chatbot accessed the press publication at issue unlawfully, in particular by circumventing effective technical measures (such as a paywall)".

Second, whether the answer "reuses the form of expression of that publication in a recognisable manner, to such an extent that the user, by reading that answer, is entirely dispensed from accessing the original publication".

The provider is liable where such acts "are inherent to the business model of that provider or the latter has failed to put in place appropriate measures".

He advises the Court to declare the two training questions inadmissible, "since there is no evidence that Like Company's articles have been used to train the Gemini LLMs". Training is, "for the sake of completeness", called "as a rule, freely permitted under the text and data mining exception".

The right "does not protect the mere facts reported in press publications, but only the specific form of expression of those publications".

The full opinion names Google's search surfaces too. Paragraph 156 says the same technology "is also behind 'AI Overviews' and 'AI Mode', which are two separate AI services, integrated into Google Search and also powered by the Gemini LLMs".

"The Advocate General's Opinion is not binding on the Court of Justice." No judgment date is set, and the release carries no Google statement.

The opinion decides nothing about AI Overviews or AI Mode, which are not before the Court. It names no figure and no remedy.

ProductGoogle will read a shared robots.txt training token as a Google-Extended disallow from 2027

John Mueller's Search Central post of 30th Oct announces "plans to support a shared AI training control in robots.txt". It is "based on work from the AIPREF working group" at the IETF.

The control is a "policy token" in the User-agent line. The crawler documentation names it: __usage_ai-training.

"When a website uses this policy token to opt out of training in its robots.txt file (and Google-Extended is not explicitly specified), Google plans to interpret that as if the publisher had disallowed Google-Extended."

"Existing company-specific controls will continue to function alongside this new joint signal." A site can set the broad choice and still tailor Google's rule with Google-Extended.

The post restates the trade. "Websites that specifically allow Google-Extended are eligible to appear in responses and receive clicks via grounding in Gemini Apps."

Timing: "We aim to support this new control along with other AI providers in 2027." The common crawlers page, updated the same day, says "in early 2027".

The token comes from an individual Internet-Draft dated 30th Oct. The IETF page says it "has no formal standing in the IETF standards process".

Microsoft and OpenAI announced support the same day. Our crawler guide compares the announcements.

The scope is Google-Extended's: Gemini training and grounding. The post says nothing about Googlebot, AI Overviews, AI Mode or Search ranking, so our Google-Extended section stands.

DataA detector study estimates 8.9% of pages behind generative answers are optimized for them, and Gemini grounding sees more than Search

A team from CISPA Helmholtz Center for Information Security, HPE and the University of Waterloo posted GEO-Flag to arXiv. It appeared on 17th Aug.

It is the first attempt we have seen to measure a simple thing. How much of the web that generative answers draw on was written for them?

Its Gemini finding is why it belongs on this page.

The method has two halves. First, a detector.

The authors built a 3,200-page benchmark spanning 400 queries, four domains and eight families of GEO optimizer.

They found that the strongest existing detection method reaches an aggregate F1 of 0.880 while leaning on authorship cues.

They trained their own on ModernBERT to F1 0.944 with worst-group accuracy up from 0.725 to 0.883.

Then a live audit. They sampled 1,000 queries from a released ORCAS subset and took the URLs those queries returned in two channels.

They fetched every page between 28th and 31st Jul 2026.

The channels are the ones to hold onto. One is landing-page links from a conventional Google Search results page.

The other is the source links attached to answers from Gemini 2.5 Flash generated with Google Search grounding. That is the mechanism our Google-Extended section is about.

Across the 10,095 pages they could fetch, the detector flags 8.90% as GEO-optimized. The 95% confidence interval is 8.36% to 9.47%.

The channels differ: 8.14% of the Google Search pages against 9.09% of the Gemini-grounded ones. When every query is given equal weight the gap widens rather than closing, to 7.91% against 9.34%.

That is a difference of 1.43 percentage points, with a bootstrap interval of 0.46 to 2.41.

The trend over time is steeper than the gap. Among pages whose metadata carries a modification date, the estimated rate rises year on year.

It is 7.02% for 2024, 12.80% for 2025 and 16.36% for 2026. The 2026 figure splits as 13.52% for Search and 18.20% for Gemini.

The citation audit is the finding a publisher should sit with longest. On the pages flagged as GEO-optimized, the authors parsed 6,663 citation occurrences.

They graded each for whether the cited source is accountable and reachable.

Across both channels 69.34% land in the lowest verifiability band. By channel that is 45.88% for pages Search returned against 74.15% for pages Gemini grounding returned.

Flagged Gemini-channel pages also carry nearly twice as many citations each, 8.80 against 4.90.

In other words, the pages this pipeline flags do not merely read as optimized. They cite more and cite worse.

That is what an answer engine's own trust signals being gamed looks like from the outside.

Three limits decide how far this travels, and the paper states all three itself. The authors call these estimates rather than measurements.

Live pages carry no ground-truth labels, and both the detector and the citation audit can be wrong.

The queries come from ORCAS, which is clicked query and document pairs from real Bing users. So they are genuine historical queries rather than a snapshot of what people ask Google today.

And 3,890 of the 13,985 URLs never yielded a usable page, mostly persistent 403s and robots restrictions. So the 8.90% describes the retrievable web rather than all of it.

One thing this study does not measure, and it is easy to assume otherwise. It never touches AI Overviews, AI Mode or the Gemini app.

Its two channels are an ordinary results page and a grounded API answer.

It does say something to anyone weighing whether to let their content ground Gemini answers. The pool of pages doing that grounding is measurably more optimized than the pool Search returns.

And it is getting more so year by year.

LegalStarbuck v Google: a defamation claim over Gemini outputs survives dismissal

Judge Meghan A. Adams of the Delaware Superior Court ruled on 24th Jul 2026 in Starbuck v. Google LLC. She denied Google's motion to dismiss.

Robby Starbuck, a conservative commentator, sued in October 2025. He says Google's AI products stated false things about him, including invented crimes and accusations.

Law professor Eugene Volokh quotes the opinion at length. It refers to "the Gemini Outputs" and records that "Google does not contend the Outputs are true."

The ruling decides no facts. Delaware asks only whether the pleaded facts are conceivable, a lower bar than the federal plausibility test.

Three of Google's arguments failed at this stage:

Publication. The complaint says a Google AI product reported giving false statements about Starbuck to 2,843,917 users. Google called that figure unreliable, and the court declined to discard it before discovery.

Disclaimers. Google said accuracy warnings stop any reasonable reader relying on the outputs. The court said it had no record of which disclaimer was shown to whom.

Actual malice. Starbuck says he notified Google's legal department before the outputs appeared. His earlier complaints concerned Bard, which the court noted was later combined with Gemini.

Google also relied on Walters v. OpenAI, a Georgia case that OpenAI won. The court answered that Walters was decided on summary judgment, after discovery.

Bloomberg's report puts the decision at 36 pages. It quotes the judge calling the dispute "a new frontier for defamation law".

Bloomberg names the product as Bard. The passages of the opinion we read name both, with Gemini as the source of the outputs at issue.

This is one of two such rulings we track. On 14th Sep 2026 a federal judge let part of Keene v. Google proceed over AI Overviews.

We found this ruling on 27th Sep 2026 and publish it at its own date.

Gemini and your site: what Google-Extended actually controls

The robots.txt token that governs Gemini does something quite different from what most publishers assume. It is the opposite of what it does for AI Overviews.

10.94%Gemini's share of AI assistant referrals, Sep 2026

Start with Google's own words.

Per the crawler documentation, "Google-Extended is a standalone product token that web publishers can use to manage whether content Google crawls from their sites may be used for training future generations of Gemini models that power Gemini Apps and Vertex AI API for Gemini and for grounding (providing content from the Google Search index to the model at prompt time to improve factuality and relevancy) in Gemini Apps and Grounding with Google Search on Vertex AI."

Two things follow, and they are the practical answer for publishers.

First, Google-Extended really does control something here. It gates whether your crawled content trains future Gemini models.

It also gates whether that content can be used to ground a live answer in the Gemini app.

That is a genuine, documented lever.

Second, and this is where it differs from everything on our Search hubs. Google-Extended does not gate whether you appear in an AI Overview or an AI Mode answer.

Those run on ordinary Search indexing.

So a publisher can disallow Google-Extended and keep full Search and AI Overviews eligibility. They still stop their content training Gemini or grounding its chat answers.

One independent measurement complicates that second point without contradicting the documentation. A SIGIR 2026 study found sites disallowing Google-Extended were significantly less likely to be cited in AI Overviews.

That is despite Google's stated access. We cover the study and its limits on the AI Overviews hub.

A second measurement speaks to what the grounding pool looks like rather than to who is in it.

It does not change what the token controls. It is context for the decision.

The content competing for a grounded citation is increasingly written for that purpose.

Eligibility and citation are different things, and the paper measures the second. It rests on one December 2025 snapshot and reports an association rather than a demonstrated cause.

So treat it as a reason to check your own citation data after blocking.

A third measurement speaks to the cost of blocking rather than to citation, and it comes from outside Google entirely.

That is an aggregate effect across every major AI token rather than a Google-Extended finding. Its window stops before AI Overviews launched.

So it cannot tell you what this one token costs. It does say the blocking decision has shown up in traffic data before.

That is a reason to measure your own rather than assume the cost is zero.

Google is explicit that the token costs you nothing in Search: it "does not impact a site's inclusion in Google Search nor is it used as a ranking signal in Google Search".

It also runs no crawler of its own.

As the documentation puts it, "Google-Extended doesn't have a separate HTTP request user agent string. Crawling is done with existing Google user agent strings; the robots.txt user-agent token is used in a control capacity."

Update, 30th Oct 2026: Google will also read a shared robots.txt training token as a Google-Extended disallow, from 2027. The entry has the detail.

What it cannot do is remove you from a conversation entirely. It restricts Google's use of its own crawled copy.

So it does not govern what happens when a user pastes your URL into a chat directly.

No documentation describes a separate mechanism for that case.

Whether Gemini links out, and how much traffic it sends

Gemini does cite, but not always. Google's help page says "When sources are available, you can find the Sources button at the bottom of the response or in-line throughout the response", and equally that "Not all responses include related links or sources."

One case is guaranteed: "If Gemini Apps directly quotes a large amount of text from a webpage, you'll see a link to that webpage in the sources list."

One distinction in that documentation is easy to miss. Google also surfaces "related links", which it says are "not necessarily sources used to generate the response".

A link sitting next to an answer is not proof Gemini used that page for the claims it made.

It held 10.94% in Sep 2026, second behind ChatGPT. October is left out because ChatGPT links stopped carrying a referrer that month.

Note that this is share of a pool rather than absolute traffic. It measures referrals out to sites rather than usage of the assistant itself.
