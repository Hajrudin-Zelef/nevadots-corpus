# Google DeepMind has released 'AlphaGenome Atlas,' a database that predicts the effects of approximately 9 billion possible mutations in the human genome.


 Google DeepMind has released ' **AlphaGenome Atlas** ,' a database that uses AI to predict the effects of approximately 9 billion possible single **-nucleotide** mutations in the human genome on gene expression, RNA splicing, and other processes. It can be searched in a browser without the need for custom programs and can be used to narrow down mutations related to potential causes of rare diseases or traits.

 The human genome consists of approximately 3 billion base pairs of DNA, but only about 2% of it is the region that codes for protein design information. The remaining 98% is non-coding regions that play a role in regulating gene function, but the effects of mutations in these regions on biological function are not yet fully understood.

 There are approximately 9 billion single-nucleotide mutations in the entire human genome, where one base is replaced by another. Since it is not practical to experimentally verify each of these individually, Google DeepMind developed 'AlphaGenome,' an AI model that predicts the impact of gene mutations on biological processes at the molecular level.

 **Google announces 'AlphaGenome,' an AI capable of analyzing the effects of genetic mutations, which can input 1 million base pairs at once and may be useful in establishing treatments for genetic diseases - GIGAZINE**


 The AlphaGenome Atlas is a dataset of approximately 1 petabyte compiled from the pre-analysis of about 9 billion single-nucleotide mutations using AlphaGenome. Its scale is more than 30 times that of the AlphaFold Database, which contains predictions of protein three-dimensional structures, and it predicts the impact of each mutation on gene regulation across thousands of items in hundreds of types of human and mouse cells and tissues. 

 Furthermore, Google DeepMind has introduced the 'AlphaGenome Variant Impact (AVI) score,' which represents the importance of a mutation with a single numerical value. The AVI score integrates predictions from AlphaGenome, which primarily deals with non-coding regions, and predictions from AlphaMissense, which evaluates mutations that alter proteins, allowing researchers to rank coding and non-coding mutations on a common scale.

 The AVI score is associated with the characteristics that underpinned its value. This allows us to see which elements—such as chromatin accessibility, gene expression, RNA splicing, evolutionary conservation, and protein function—a particular mutation was predicted to affect. It also includes more than 2,500 short DNA sequence 'motifs' that repeatedly appear in the genome, along with their locations. 


 According to Google DeepMind, the AlphaGenome Atlas is already being used in research investigating unsolved rare diseases. A research team from **the Broad Institute** , a joint research institution of MIT and Harvard University, used the AVI score to narrow down mutations from a large number of candidates and discovered a mutation in the ' **DNM1** ' gene that is strongly associated with epileptic encephalopathy. AlphaGenome predicted that this mutation would create an incorrect splice site, resulting in an abnormally long protein, and this effect has been confirmed in experiments.

 Gareth Hawkes, a fellow at the Medical Research Council of the University of Exeter, applied the AlphaGenome Atlas to whole-genome data from more than 54,000 participants in **the UK Biobank** . By grouping rare mutations by their predicted molecular function, he detected 22% more associations between non-coding regions and traits that were previously buried in statistical noise, identifying mutations in **PLA2G7** , which has been linked to aging, and in regulatory regions that affect the amount of **EGLN1** , which acts as a cellular oxygen sensor.

 Hawkes further analyzed the relationship between BMI and hundreds of millions of non-coding region mutations in the UK Biobank. By focusing on the top 1% of mutations predicted by AlphaGenome Atlas to have the greatest impact, he reported being able to identify 19 genetic regions associated with BMI. 


 AlphaGenome Atlas allows you to search for mutations and view prediction results for free from your web browser, without requiring any specialized programming knowledge. For programmatic analysis of large amounts of data, the AlphaGenome API is available, and for research using AI agents, a skill for Google Antigravity is also available. As of the time of writing, it is provided primarily for non-commercial use, and commercial services are planned to be offered on Google Cloud in the future.

 However, Google DeepMind explains that the information included in AlphaGenome Atlas is based on AI predictions and is intended for theoretical modeling and research purposes. Furthermore, since AlphaGenome has not been validated or approved for clinical use, predictions from AlphaGenome Atlas cannot be used for clinical decisions such as diagnosis or treatment.
