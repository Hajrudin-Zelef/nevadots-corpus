# AI Gene-Discovery Claim Meets Real-World Science Test

Anthropic says its AI found a new molecular machine, but independent labs have yet to verify how it works.

## In a nutshell

Anthropic used its Claude artificial intelligence to identify an unusual molecular pattern inside bacterial viruses, calling it ART and comparing its structure to gene-editing tools like CRISPR. While independent coders have confirmed that the DNA pattern exists in public genetic databases, no outside laboratory has tested the physical enzymes, leaving it unproven whether the system can actually cut, copy, or edit genes.

## Highlights

- Anthropic announced that its AI software discovered a new molecular system called ART across 95 related enzyme clusters.
- Outside programmers successfully detected the five-copy repeating DNA pattern in public viral genomes without using Claude.
- Independent reanalysis found viral RNA levels of 3.8% and 4.5%, falling short of Anthropic's reported 8% maximum.
- The underlying viral enzyme was originally cataloged by Texas A&M researchers in 2021, years before Anthropic's study.
- No independent laboratory experiment has demonstrated that the ART system can edit or copy DNA.

## From the Editor’s Diary

Spotting a structural pattern with artificial intelligence is an engineering feat, but biology requires physical proof: until a machine performs its function under independent laboratory observation, a computational finding remains a hypothesis.

## In short

Artificial intelligence can now spot hidden patterns in genetic code, but computers alone cannot prove what those patterns actually do inside a living cell. This gap sits at the heart of an announcement by Anthropic, the California company that makes the Claude chatbot. Anthropic reported that its automated AI programs discovered a new molecular setup called ART inside viruses that infect bacteria. The layout looks like CRISPR, the natural defense mechanism that scientists turned into a revolutionary gene-editing tool. But looking like a molecular tool is not the same as working like one.

The most likely next step is a series of independent laboratory tests to see whether this viral machinery can actually cut, copy, or alter DNA.

Whether that will happen remains uncertain because Anthropic has not released its full computer records, and no outside team has made the system perform gene editing in a physical experiment. To date, independent coders have confirmed only that the repeating DNA pattern exists in public databases. Proving that it works as a controllable biological machine will require hands-on experiments by outside scientists.

## How it unfolded

## The enzyme was in public databases years before the AI search

The core building block of Anthropic's report was already in the scientific record. In July 2021, a research team at Texas A&M University published a study of giant viruses that kill Staphylococcus bacteria. One of these viruses, named MarsHill, carried an unusual enzyme called a reverse transcriptase, which builds DNA copies from RNA templates. The authors pointed out an unexplored stretch of genetic material right next to it, but they did not describe the repeating pattern Anthropic later highlighted.

## Public databases held the raw clues

Two academic studies provided the public foundation for the AI finding. The Texas team deposited the full MarsHill viral genome in a public genetic repository. A year later, a research group in China deposited measurements of RNA—the working copies cells make of their DNA—from a related virus called SA1. These records were created to study basic viral infections, giving future researchers open data to re-examine without having to collect new samples.

## Anthropic uses AI to link the patterns together

In September 2026, Anthropic announced that automated programs powered by its Claude model had spotted something new. Scanning massive protein catalogs, the AI noticed that the known viral enzyme sat beside a series of repeating DNA sequences and a helper gene. Anthropic named the cluster ART. It noted that Claude found ART's architecture, not a brand-new enzyme, turning previously cataloged parts into a newly proposed system.

## Outside scientists question the claims as coders test the code

The announcement drew quick pushback from independent biologists. A Danish researcher reported that his team had already studied similar systems and had previously asked Claude about them. Anthropic denied that its discovery drew on private user interactions. At the same time, genome engineers cautioned that ART resembles CRISPR in architecture, not yet in function, meaning its parts look familiar but may not cut or edit DNA. Outside programmers soon used open code to confirm the five-part repeating DNA pattern in public files, though their RNA measurements did not match Anthropic's numbers.

## A public scientific paper highlights the missing evidence

On October 11, Anthropic published its findings on bioRxiv, an online archive for early scientific papers that have not undergone independent peer review. The paper revealed that Anthropic has filed for a provisional patent on the system. Independent researchers noted that ART needs mechanism, programmability and cellular editing before becoming a platform for biotechnology. Anthropic also acknowledged that when it ran the same automated search ten more times, the AI failed to re-identify the array, showing how difficult it is to duplicate automated computer findings.

## Where things stand

As of late October 2026, the case for ART is partly verified on computers and unproven in living cells. The underlying viral sequences are genuine and publicly available, and outside programmers have verified that repeating DNA sequences sit next to the enzyme gene.

What remains missing is proof of biological function. Anthropic has not released its full software instructions or computer logs, and no outside laboratory has demonstrated that the ART enzyme actually copies RNA or alters DNA. Until independent scientists can test the system in physical experiments, ART remains a computer-detected genetic pattern rather than a working biotechnology tool.
