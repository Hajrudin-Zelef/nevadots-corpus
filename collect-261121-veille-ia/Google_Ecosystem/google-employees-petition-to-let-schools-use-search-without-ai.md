More than 500 Google workers have signed a petition asking company leadership to allow schools to access Google Search without having to deal with the company’s AI features.

Earlier this month, Google was subjected to intense scrutiny online after a New York Times report said that Google would restrict students from accessing Search if their schools impose an AI ban. Several school districtsâmost notably the nation’s largest, New York Cityâhave enacted bans or moratoriums on student-facing generative AI in schools following reports claiming that the tools impede learning.

Google Vice President for Education Chris Phillips told the NYT that because “Search and AI are deeply integrated,” if AI is banned in a school, then its students who try to access Search will instead “see a message from their school that indicates it’s not available.”

Following the report, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani accused Google of “trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools,”Â and a number of parents, students, and teachers protested the decision in front of Google’s office in Chicago.

In a petition titled “Let Students Search,” the Alphabet Workers Union is now asking Google executive Phillips, as well as CEO Sundar Pichai and head of Search Elizabeth Hamon Reid, to reconsider the decision.

“The decision of how and when to implement AI into a classroom belongs to the educators leading it, not the executives who stand to profit from its expanded use,” Parul Koul, the union president and a software engineer at Google, said in a press release.

The moratoriums and bans show that parents and educators are worried about the use of AI in classrooms, according to the employees.

“It is our job as Googlers to respect the user, and listen to them when they have concerns about our products,” the letter stated. “Any opportunity to drive positive change with AI in education will rely on educators having the freedom to choose how to implement it in their curriculum. Cutting off access to Search for schools that don’t wish to use AI will not help persuade our users, and will more likely result in them shutting out Google products altogether.”

The reputational harm that Google has already suffered and could continue to suffer due to the decision could jeopardize the company’s existing relationship with school districts, the union claims, and could result in a loss of business that might threaten the job stability of the workers. That might already be somewhat underway: three major school districts, New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago, are all considering switching to alternate search engines due to Google’s decision, the New York Times reported on Wednesday.

“Decisions like this erode the trust schools place in Google, and that lands on the workers who build and support these products,” Rob Rose, a union member and a software engineer on Google’s Education team, said in the press release.

But the union did say that the reporting around the decision has been “poor” and makes Google look like it is “punishing schools for not using AI.” Google also tried to make similar clarifications to Phillips’ quotes earlier this month in a post on X, saying that the new initiative expected to take effect early next year would allow schools to individually turn on or off Search, Gemini and Gemini Notebook.

So according to Google, if a school decides to ban the standalone Gemini AI chatbot product, they could still access Search. But that does not explain what will happen to the AI features inherently baked into the Search experience. The complete AI integration into Search has been a big selling point that Google has been capitalizing on for months, but now that selling point might be the reason why the tech giant finds itself in hot water with schools.

The AI Search functions were also the subject of a lawsuit filed against Google earlier this month. A North Carolina couple claimed that their 13-year-old daughter’s use of Gemini AI tools became a “life-threatening obsession.” The lawsuit claims that the child only had access to Gemini through her school-issued Chromebook laptop. The victim allegedly used both Gemini and Google’s AI features embedded in Search, AI Overviews and AI Mode, to plan her suicide attempt. The lawsuit claims that “every time a student used their Chromebook to run a Google search, Gemini-powered AI features could be triggered and draw them into unsafe conversations that endangered their safety and well-being,” and that Google failed to notify the public of this danger.

In the petition, Google workers urge the leadership team to “allow schools to turn off AI features in Search without disrupting their ability to use search as a whole.”
