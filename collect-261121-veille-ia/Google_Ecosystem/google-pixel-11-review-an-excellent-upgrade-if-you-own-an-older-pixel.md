# Google Pixel 11 review: An excellent upgrade… if you own an older Pixel

Google Pixel 11 review examines price jump, hardware updates, and AI features like Gemini Intelligence, impacting its market competitiveness.

When you buy through our links, Business Insider may earn an affiliate commission. Learn more

While smartphone price increases have become the norm in the RAM-shortage era, the Google Pixel 11 has the unwelcome task of justifying a third $100 jump in four years. Annual trade-in buyers and Gemini power users will choose the 11 Pro without complaint, but for longtime fans accustomed to buying "value flagships" with point-and-click cameras and bloat-free software, the $899 Pixel 11 may trigger sticker shock.

Besides the new 256GB base storage and the expected Tensor G6 boost, most Pixel 10-to-11 hardware changes are subtle, such as a new modem and a full-glass camera bar. For Pixel 9 owners like myself, though, the telephoto lens and Pixelsnap accessories from 2025 remain "new" and exciting, as does the 50% boost to RAM and display brightness for Pixel 8 owners. Online discourse aside, the Pixel 11 won't be a "boring" upgrade for everyone.

On the software side, your perspective on Gemini Intelligence will depend on how much of your life's data already lives on messaging or social media apps, or how useful you'll find its contextual AI automation. Pixel 11 exclusives like Magic Capture and Camera Looks should have universal appeal, while Creator Suite and Screen Reactions target the social media–savvy, AI-dependent demographic that may not apply to you.

As much as I wish the Pixel 11 had the 11 Pro's HiLight LEDs, full-resolution photos, and Instant Night Sight, the base model is easier to recommend. Matched up against even 2025's iPhone 17 or Galaxy S25, the Pixel 11 falls short in raw speed and gaming performance but remains competitive thanks to its cameras, long-term support, and cool Gemini vibes. The Pixel 11 Pro has a much harder case to make against its flagship rivals. This is arguably the best Google Pixel phone for most people, though.

## Design: Pixel tries yet another new minimal-edge look, with a Hibiscus glow-up

The Pixel camera bar has come full circle. The Pixel 6 paved the way with a mostly-glass rectangular bar, bordered by thin, colorful metal. The Pixel 7 switched to a metal frame, while the Pixel 9 sliced off the bar's corners for an oval cutout. Now, the Pixel 11 has returned to a mostly glass housing hugged by a sliver of metal, while retaining its rounded edges and slimmer profile.

In practice, this change helps the Pixel 11 cut about 6g from last year's model while lying flatter on a desk. My Pixel 9's metal housing was more likely to show oil and grime without frequent cleaning, while the Pixel 11 all-glass surface can get dirty but masks smudges better. Pocket lint accumulates along the bar edge, but not around the sensors themselves, as typically happens with iPhones or Galaxy S phones.

With this latest look, the Pixel 11 design aligns closer to other "edgeless" Pixel products, like five generations of Pixel Watches and recent A-series models with "teardrop" cameras like the Pixel 10a. In particular, the matte Obsidian Pixel 11 plays into this understated style.

By contrast, my Hibiscus review unit practically glows, creating a pop of color that still shines through with the case attached along the display and camera edges. Pink isn't my color, but my fiancée immediately and favorably compared it to her old Razr V3 flip phone, with a metallic sheen that's prettier than the Pixel 9's understated Peony. The Frost and Pistachio handsets align more with the muted 11 Pro lineup, without the standout vibrancy of the Indigo Pixel 10 or Berry Pixel 10a.

Basically identical to the Pixel 9 and 10 in height, width, and depth, the Pixel 11 feels as comfortable and secure to hold as always. For those brave souls who go caseless, you'll have an easy, secure one-handed grip; I had no trouble keeping hold even when my hand got sweaty during a six-mile run. With this review finished, I won't keep pushing my luck; even with Gorilla Glass Victus 2 protecting the front and rear glass, the Pixel 11 doesn't have the Pro's secondary anti-scratch layer or the ceramic anti-shattering tech of other brands.

Google strengthened the Pixelsnap magnets in the Pixel 11 for a more secure connection with Qi 2.2 wireless chargers. I lack a Pixel 10 for comparison, but my Pixel 11 attaches to my Ugreen Qi2 charger with an unmistakable thunk, with or without one of the best Pixel 11 cases. Once on, there's no budging the phone without a decent yank, so there's no chance of it getting nudged off by mistake. Google also increased the wireless charging speed to 25W, making it only slightly slower than a 30W wired charger for quick top-offs.

The only other notable design change applies to U.S.-based folks switching from the Pixel 9 or older: The physical SIM card slot has vanished, replaced with a larger microphone slot opposite the speaker. I much prefer the convenience of physical card swaps when testing multiple phones at a time, or if a problem arises while traveling abroad. In most scenarios, though, American Pixel buyers will prefer the convenience of an eSIM transfer.

## Display: Coasting while the competitors push the envelope

For years, Android brands could point to the base iPhone's 60Hz display as evidence of being ahead of the curve. Once Apple caught up, however, its rivals scrambled to add new features, leaving Google complacently behind the pack. The Pixel 11's FHD 120Hz OLED display is unchanged from the Pixel 10's, which itself only added a couple of hundred nits of max brightness over the Pixel 9. This isn't a problem in itself, as the display is as smooth-scrolling, vivid, and durable as Pixel old-timers would expect from an upgrade. When they see that the Pixel 10a pOLED gives you identical perks for $400 less, though, they'll be less impressed.

Both the iPhone 17 and Galaxy S26 have LTPO technology, meaning they can range from 120Hz down to 1Hz; this saves battery in scenarios like always-on display mode and tends to reduce flickering. Google also faces a tougher fight in Europe and Asia, where value flagships from Oppo or Xiaomi offer more pixels per inch (~460 vs. 422), higher refresh rates (up to 165Hz), or faster Pulse Width Modulation to reduce eye strain from high brightness.

Ignoring the industry context, only PWM-sensitive folks or gamers who want better pixel density should have any real complaints. I took the Pixel 11 jogging on a sweltering day and had no issue seeing colorful YouTube let's plays thanks to its 3,000-nit brightness. Just be warned that the phone's thermals don't do the best in direct sunlight, so it's best to avoid it.

HDR content on Disney+ and Apple TV maxes out at 2,000 nits and washes out under direct sunlight, but generally does quite well outdoors. Google's adaptive brightness sometimes leans too dim or adapts slowly to lighting changes, and it's annoying to have to swipe down twice to reveal the brightness slider. Since this probably helps the Pixel 11 battery last longer, I'll let it slide.

Seasoned Pixel owners may have experienced in-display fingerprint sensor issues with much older models. Thankfully, Google's ultrasonic sensor always recognizes my thumb immediately, even when sweaty or oily with sunblock. I prefer Fingerprint Unlock to Face Unlock, since Pixels don't use infrared for low-light facial recognition like iPhones do. Google recently claimed the feature is "more accurate in low light," but I still see a "Not enough light" pop-up when testing it in a dark room.

## Performance: Google Tensor still prioritizes AI over gamers and benchmark bros, but everyone will appreciate the new modem

Google says the Tensor G6 delivers 25% faster browsing and 50% more TPU compute than the Tensor G5, which itself offered 34% and 60% CPU and AI power boosts, respectively, over the G4. In practice, anyone jumping from a Pixel 9 or earlier will note significantly faster app loading and photo processing, with the largest speed boost for on-device AI processing. Where Tensor continues to draw criticism is in raw benchmarks, as Apple and Samsung/Qualcomm SoCs have evolved significantly faster in the last few years.

Rather than treat Geekbench as the end-all value judgment, let's speak in real-life terms. Our Pixel 10a reviewer noted that the phone ran smoothly but struggled with "multitasking between multiple resource-hungry apps" while outside. With the Pixel 11, its extra 4GB of RAM helped it bounce between Chrome, Wuthering Waves, and 10 various news and Google Workspace apps — while using 5G and running a YouTube video in the background — with nary a hiccup. Only when I tried adding a second game or using the Camera did the Pixel 11 start forcing some apps to reload to free up memory.

Tensor performs notoriously poorly on graphical benchmarks, with no on-device ray tracing support. This won't matter much for your casual Pixel Pass experience, except for slightly slower load times on the toilet. I tested some popular premium Android games instead and was surprised by how well the Pixel 11 performed: Diablo Immortal supported "High" resolution and max settings for shadows, fog, anti-aliasing, visual effects, and more, though only at 30 FPS. On COD Mobile, it handled "Max" resolution and frame rate with ease. It even ran the notoriously power-hungry Wuthering Waves, though it stuttered on "Balanced" and ran best on the low-res "Performance" mode. Overall, the Pixel 11 is a mid-tier "gaming phone" at best that runs warm in most use cases, but still does its job better than you'd expect.

Google claims the Pixel 11 hits 30+ hours per charge, a six-hour boost over the Pixel 10, despite adding only 15 mAh of typical capacity. I *have* easily hit that number on "normal" days when I only get a few hours of screen time over wi-fi, but your experience will vary based on your carrier, 5G vs. wi-fi usage, and other factors. Past Pixels worked fine in factory settings but had widespread issues with 5G connectivity, which then triggered major battery drain. Google gave the Pixel 11 a MediaTek M90 5G modem meant to be "faster and more power efficient," so I set out to test that.

The Google Fi (aka T-Mobile) signal in my area isn't the best, so my typical signal strength (around -94 to -106 dBm) isn't particularly impressive. Despite that, the Pixel 11 never struggled with connectivity or battery drain when using 5G around my area. So I stress-tested it by taking my fully charged Pixel 11 on public transit, then to a stadium with 50,000 people. It successfully streamed college football in and out of tunnels, with only a few brief "buffering" breaks. Once in the stadium, the Pixel 11 briefly registered as "offline" but cut through the traffic to browse and send photos to family after about 15 minutes. After eight hours, including nearly five hours of screen time with plenty of challenging 5G streaming and photography, I still had 52% battery left.

Other scenarios led to more significant battery usage. The Camera app guzzles plenty of energy with every shot or video, especially when multitasking. Video streaming over 5G is fairly efficient, but paired with peak brightness, a two-hour YouTube video took the Pixel 11 from 50% to 20%. And while gaming on WiFi barely moves the needle, any online component, paired with the memory usage, will quickly kill your capacity. But despite all that, my Pixel 11 regularly ends the day with about 30–40% battery remaining, and that's while using much more 5G data than I normally would. It's not a multi-day phone, but it has the endurance to last all day with 8–10 hours of screen time.

## Gemini Intelligence: Equal parts genuine innovation and Big Tech's cynical vision of an automated future

Google's "Gemini Intelligence" name invites an obvious Apple Intelligence comparison, though the latter was rebranded as "Siri AI" after Apple's AI underwhelmed iPhone 17 Pro buyers. In Google's case, it's a chance to rebrand its "proactive" AI tools after 2025 features like Magic Cue and Daily Hub failed to match Google's hype. This time, some features feel more tangible and integrated into Android OS, while others fall into the same pie-in-the-sky lifestyle promises that Silicon Valley loves to offer.

On the Pixel 10, the "Magic Cue" tool searched your Google apps and data to provide a contextual answer to messaged questions like "What time is your flight?" Now, Gemini Intelligence backs up its suggestions with visual evidence: a "Check Schedule" button will display a Google Calendar card summarizing your plans for that day, without leaving the Messenger app. You can also get a brief Maps summary if a friend suggests a restaurant, or a Gmail summary of an upcoming flight or event. You confirm information for yourself, rather than taking Gemini on faith, and it genuinely saves you time.

Where Gemini Intelligence loses me is with its promises of automation. Google wants you to have Gemini reserve a table on OpenTable or a parking spot on SpotHero, based on your Messages conversation or current Chrome webpage. I tested it by pretend-planning a lunch date, and it showed some good Mexican and dim sum options from Maps. When I tried to reserve a table, however, the first three options weren't on OpenTable; with the fourth, Google quickly found a noon slot, but for the *next* day, not the day mentioned in Messages.

Google will no doubt add more partners like Yelp and Resy, but the fundamental issue is that I'll always want to check Gemini's work. For example, will it pick the closest parking spot on SpotHero that's too expensive, or a cheap one that's far away? Google says you can auto-fill an Instacart order from a Google Keep list, but I'd want to check if it picked the best sale prices or quality brands.

More generally, Proactive Assistance feels very cynically centered on keeping your data and money within Google's ecosystem and its favored partners. I'm not surprised that it's currently restricted to North America and Asia, as I'm certain this would trigger more antitrust regulation in the E.U.

For now, I'd rather Google perfect the basics. I asked Gemini to create a Google Calendar entry based on my current Chrome webpage about "How to watch" an upcoming football game. It didn't work, first offering me data to paste into Calendar myself, then telling me to tap a nonexistent link to save the entry. If Google makes *this* kind of simple, contextual automation work, it'll make Pixel owners much happier than shopping AI.

Google did, thankfully, focus on *some* of the little things with Android 17. For instance, it improved one of my favorite Gemini tools, Circle to Search, by letting you use it in the Camera viewfinder. Point it at an unfamiliar plant or animal, tap and hold the navigation bar, and circle it to get an AI summary describing it. You could use Google Lens to examine photos before, but this helps prevent cluttering up your gallery.

Rambler, the new Gboard speech-to-text tool, removes filler words, adds punctuation and spacing, and makes your message more "clean and concise." Unlike similar tools on other phones, the transcript only appears once you tap an icon to signal that you're done. I find Rambler works fully as intended, though I sometimes lose track of what I'm saying without a real-time transcription.

I'm the furthest thing from an influencer, but I appreciate how the Pixel 11 makes it easier for anyone to get into creating video content. Creator Suite, located in the Video settings, offers a few useful options for enhancing audio or organizing and editing video clips, but my favorite is the on-screen teleprompter. You either paste text from your clipboard or use Rambler to spew out your thoughts, then have a more polished script to read without it being obvious to the viewer. I found that it worked seamlessly if I followed the teleprompter perfectly, but it freezes if I skip over some words. I hope Google allows for more improvisation in future updates.

The other Android 17 video tool I enjoyed using was Screen Reactions, another breezily simple tool that lets you use a webpage or app as a green screen. You simply tap the Record icon in the Quick Settings menu (next to Cast), then choose to record your mic, selfie camera, and the "entire screen." You can drag your head to appear anywhere on the screen, your background automatically cut out via AI, and then start recording your reaction video. It's not the highest quality, but it works well enough.

## Cameras: This is where the fun begins

Pixel 10 owners will yawn at the Pixel 11's unaltered telephoto lens with 5X optical zoom and side-eye the stagnant specs for the main and ultrawide sensors. For buyers two generations back or more, however, getting a telephoto lens on a non-Pro model is a serious game changer, and the main reason *not* to skimp on a Pixel A-series phone. I took the Pixel 11 to a college football game at my alma mater, and the phone helped memorialize a truly awful loss with some stylish, magnified shots.

Close-up photos of campus architecture looked truly fantastic, particularly this well-lit shot of Sather Tower. While digital zoom relies solely on post-processing, optical zoom provides greater accuracy and detail from a decent distance. Much of that detail is lost when capturing people's faces — particularly in low-light scenarios — devolving into semi-accurate blurs when you pinch to zoom in on specific people.

I'm satisfied that the 10.8MP sensor captured enough visual information to hit a Pro-lite quality level despite the challenging conditions. But I don't put much stock in the 30X "Super Zoom," or anything beyond 5X, really, unless the lighting is perfect. If you need that level of detail, shell out for the Pixel 11 Pro's 50MP telephoto lens.

In more pedestrian scenarios, I appreciate that I can capture lovely shots of animals without spooking them by getting close or sacrificing on quality.

Magic Capture mode has you record a video, then uses AI to pick and process the best photos from that video. The feature would work best in a scenario like a party, where the subjects stay in a certain range. Since Google said it would work for animals, sports, or "dynamic/candid scenes," I did my best to test it out, but got plenty of out-of-focus or overly bird's-eye shots because this mode doesn't work with 5X optical zoom.

When you *can* get right up to your subject, the Pixel 11's macro focus mode can create gorgeous nature shots with a telephoto effect from just a few centimeters away. If your subject is the wrong size or you shoot slightly too far away, Macro Focus can sometimes fail to trigger or blur parts of your subject. I wish Google made this mode a manual toggle, similar to Portrait mode on Galaxy phones and iPhones.

What I found especially exciting was Google's decision to let us change the Pixel 11's default "Look." Each of the photos below, taken from the same angle, has subtle-but-permanent differences, meaning you can't switch a Look *after* you take the photo. You can prioritize how you would normally edit your photos by default, or try some of the more gimmicky Looks like "Black Tie" or "Editorial." Simply put, they're a lot of fun to use and encourage you to be more purposeful about how you compose a shot before you snap it.

Instant Night Sight on the Pixel 11 Pro uses Tensor AI to capture a photo near-instantly that would normally take three seconds of open exposure. Unfortunately, the Pixel 11 lacks this feature, whether due to limited RAM, the smaller main image sensor, or a classic feature price-lock. Whatever the reason, night photography on the Pixel 11 isn't great, outside of scenarios where you have artificial lighting.

Are the Pixel 11's camera perks worth the upgrade? Not if you have a Pixel 10, but for everyone else, you'll wonder how you lived this long without optical zoom once you have it. Google still reserves the best manual photography tools for the 11 Pro, but with this release, it gave everyday Pixel users "casual" videography software, camera shortcuts, and gimmicky processing customization to make photography feel fun. And that will satisfy the Pixel vets who don't care as much about other Gemini AI perks.

## Should you buy the Google Pixel 11?

If you have a Pixel 10, the only reason to upgrade is if you live in a rural or poor-signal area and frequently experience serious battery drain. In that case, you can gamble on the MediaTek modem solving your woes; I can't recreate your specific environment or carrier issues, but I can at least promise that my Pixel 11 gets a stronger signal than past Pixels I've reviewed.

If you have an older Pixel, you could buy a Pixel 10 and still get six years of software support. We've seen it drop as low as $534 around the time the Pixel 11 was released, and it will likely dip further now. But trading in an old Pixel will get the Pixel 11 close to that price, and I'd rather have the Tensor G6 efficiency boost, new modem, improved Pixelsnap, and doubled storage. I'd also skip the Pixel 10a, just because I can't go back to a life without a telephoto lens.

The trickier question is whether you should *switch* from other brands to a Pixel 11. As a camera phone, its 5X zoom gives it a serious edge over the telephoto-less iPhone 17 and the 3X-zoom Galaxy S26. As a gaming device, it only functions while its rivals thrive. As an AI phone, Google reserves the best on-device tricks and camera processing for its Pixels, but Google still needs to prove that its Intelligence can make life more convenient. Fans of ChatGPT, Claude, and/or Perplexity are accustomed to using AI for more robust tasks like coding, research, or agents, which could make Gemini Nano tools feel insignificant by comparison.

Ultimately, this isn't an exciting release on paper, and I wouldn't blame anyone for holding out for the Pixel 12. But there's no reason to assume Google will deviate from its current Tensor strategy and suddenly deliver massive compute power next year with the Tensor G7. For now, whether you care about Gemini Intelligence or not, the Pixel 11 is still one of the best phones of the year, and is worth the upgrade for Pixel fans with aging Tensor hardware.
