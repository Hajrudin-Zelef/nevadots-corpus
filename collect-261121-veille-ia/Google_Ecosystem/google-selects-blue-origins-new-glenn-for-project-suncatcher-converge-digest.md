Google has selected Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket for the first dedicated heavy-lift launch of Project Suncatcher, its research program exploring whether solar-powered satellites can support AI computing in orbit. The companies announced a launch services agreement on November 19, with the mission targeted for the first quarter of 2029. The planned destination is sun-synchronous orbit.

Blue Origin says the mission is designed to deploy multiple satellites, each with computing capacity roughly equivalent to a data center rack. Google’s proposed architecture distributes Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) across spacecraft connected by free-space optical links. The 2029 mission would follow an initial satellite launched with Planet in October 2026 and a planned two-satellite experiment in 2027 to test optical inter-satellite connectivity. Google has not announced that the 2029 mission will deploy an operational orbital data center.

The agreement adds a larger launch step to a program that has already moved from a published system concept to an in-orbit TPU test. Google reported on October 30 that its prototype had powered up its TPU hardware and generated a response using an onboard Gemma 4 model. Its next tests address performance under orbital thermal and radiation conditions; the high-bandwidth optical connections needed for distributed workloads remain a separate milestone.

“Project Suncatcher is a long-term moonshot to scale AI infrastructure in space by tapping into the abundant solar power in orbit,” said Travis Beals, Senior Director of Project Suncatcher at Google.

🌐 Analysis

Project Suncatcher’s main networking question is whether multiple moving satellites can exchange enough data, reliably enough, to act as a distributed AI system. Google’s original architecture calls for high-bandwidth free-space optical links. The single prototype has demonstrated an AI workload in orbit, but it cannot validate a link between spacecraft; Google assigns that test to the planned 2027 mission.

Compute and power present different tests. Sun-synchronous orbit can provide extended access to sunlight, while TPU operation still produces heat that must be rejected through space-compatible cooling hardware. Google says it will continue testing its prototype under thermal stress and radiation exposure. The 2029 New Glenn mission would increase the scale of hardware placed in orbit, but its announcement establishes a launch plan rather than the performance or economics of a production system.
