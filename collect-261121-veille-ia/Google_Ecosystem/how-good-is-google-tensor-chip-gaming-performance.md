Google's Tensor processors are designed around more than raw speed. They also support Google's photography, artificial intelligence and software features, which helps explain why Pixel phones can feel responsive in everyday use without matching specialist gaming phones. To understand the difference, it helps to look at graphics benchmarks, actual game performance and how the newer Tensor generations compare with Snapdragon.

## How well do Google Tensor chips handle mobile gaming?

The short answer is that Tensor is good enough for casual and moderately demanding gaming, but less convincing for people who want maximum graphics settings, consistently high frame rates or the best performance for the money.

The distinction between CPU and GPU performance matters here. The CPU handles general processing, game logic and other background work, while the GPU renders the graphics. A processor can therefore feel quick in everyday use and still struggle to compete with another flagship chip in graphically intensive games.

Google's graphics performance has improved over the Tensor generations, but not at the same pace as its main competitors. A comparison of five generations of mobile graphics performance found roughly a 50% improvement between the Pixel 6 Pro and Pixel 10 Pro, while competing processors achieved approximately 300% gains over a similar period.<sup>[1]</sup>

The Pixel 11 brings some improvement. Testing found that the Tensor G6 scored around 100 points higher than the Tensor G5 in 3DMark Steel Nomad Light, a benchmark designed to simulate mobile gaming workloads.<sup>[2]</sup> However, the improvement is relatively modest compared with the graphics performance available from Samsung and Apple flagship phones.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What the gaming benchmarks reveal

Synthetic benchmarks help separate graphics performance from general processing speed. AnTuTu measures several parts of a chip, while 3DMark focuses more directly on graphics. Neither score translates directly into a game's frame rate, but both help explain why one phone handles demanding titles better than another.

The Tensor G6 provides a useful example of the difference. Tested in the Pixel 11 Pro XL, it recorded the following AnTuTu V11 results.<sup>[3]</sup>

| AnTuTu V11 test | Tensor G6 score | 
|---|---|
| Overall | 1,687,489 | 
| CPU | 739,209 | 
| GPU | 251,126 | 
| Memory | 340,262 | 
| UX | 356,892 | 

The overall result is approximately 31% higher than the Tensor G5's 1,291,252 points in the same publication's earlier test. However, the GPU result is 34% lower than the previous generation's GPU score. That is a significant distinction: the overall benchmark improved, but graphics performance did not follow the same direction.<sup>[3]</sup>

Geekbench 6 paints a more encouraging picture of the G6's CPU. It achieved 2,691 in single-core performance and 7,203 in multi-core performance, compared with the Tensor G5's 2,285 and 6,191 respectively.<sup>[3]</sup>

This improvement is useful for general processing, but it does not automatically make the G6 a better gaming processor. A game that depends heavily on graphics rendering can remain limited by the GPU even when the CPU becomes substantially faster.

The 3DMark Wild Life Extreme stress test gives a clearer indication of the G6's graphics limitations. The Pixel 11 Pro XL recorded a best loop score of 3,338, a lowest loop score of 2,236 and 67% stability.<sup>[3]</sup>

That stability figure deserves some attention. It suggests that the chip maintains a reasonably consistent level of graphics performance over repeated runs, even though its maximum graphics output is relatively modest. Consistency is useful during longer gaming sessions, but it cannot compensate entirely for a weaker GPU.

## Tensor G6 gaming performance and actual frame rates

Benchmark scores are useful, but actual games reveal limitations that synthetic tests cannot always capture. Testing on the Pixel 11 Pro XL shows that the Tensor G6 can deliver a good experience in some games while struggling with others.

The results differ considerably depending on the title.

| Game | Tensor G6 gaming result | 
|---|---|
| Call of Duty: Mobile | 120 FPS on Low and Medium | 
| BGMI | Capped at 60 FPS | 
| Genshin Impact | Did not run properly | 

These results come from gaming sessions on the Pixel 11 Pro XL, rather than theoretical estimates based on benchmark scores.<sup>[3]</sup>

Call of Duty: Mobile is the strongest result. The game ran at 120 FPS on Low and Medium graphics settings, with generally smooth gameplay and only minimal performance dips. The phone also remained relatively cool during the sessions.<sup>[3]</sup>

This is encouraging for competitive mobile games where responsiveness matters more than maximum visual detail. However, the 120 FPS result should not be interpreted as a guarantee that every Pixel game can reach that frame rate. Game support, graphics settings and software optimization all affect the result.

BGMI presented a different limitation. It remained capped at 60 FPS regardless of the graphics settings selected during testing. The testing report attributes this more to game optimization than to a fundamental hardware restriction.<sup>[3]</sup>

Genshin Impact was the least successful example. The game did not run properly on the tested Pixel 11 Pro XL, with the PowerVR GPU identified as a likely compatibility problem. This is particularly relevant because demanding open-world games place greater pressure on graphics hardware than many lighter mobile titles.<sup>[3]</sup>

There is also an important distinction between a game running at a high frame rate and a game running at high graphics quality. Call of Duty: Mobile reaching 120 FPS on lower settings does not mean the phone can deliver the same performance with every visual option enabled.

## How Tensor compares with Snapdragon

The comparison with Qualcomm Snapdragon is where Tensor's gaming weaknesses become more obvious. Snapdragon flagship processors have generally made much larger graphics improvements over recent generations, while Google's progress has been considerably slower.

A comparison of graphics performance over five generations found that the Pixel 6 Pro to Pixel 10 Pro improvement was roughly 50%. Competing processors achieved approximately 300% gains over a similar period.<sup>[1]</sup>

The difference is visible in demanding games. In testing reported for the Pixel 10 Pro, Genshin Impact barely sustained 40 FPS at maximum settings. Snapdragon competitors, meanwhile, reached 120 FPS with better graphics in Call of Duty: Mobile.<sup>[1]</sup>

The Tensor G6 comparison is even more revealing. Its PowerVR CXTP-48-1536 GPU scored less than half the performance of the Adreno 840 in the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 and the Arm G1-Ultra in the MediaTek Dimensity 9500 in the reported graphics comparison.<sup>[3]</sup>

The Pixel 11 benchmarks also show how far Google remains behind Samsung and Apple. The Tensor G6 improved its Geekbench 6 and Geekbench 7 graphics scores substantially over the Tensor G5, but the Pixel 11 still could not match the graphics performance of competing flagship phones.<sup>[2]</sup>

That does not mean every Snapdragon phone will outperform every Pixel in every game. Different devices have different cooling systems, software configurations and game optimizations. However, the available graphics comparisons show a clear advantage for competing flagship processors.

## Is Tensor G4 better than Snapdragon 7 Gen 4?

The Tensor G4 is a useful example of why processor comparisons need more than one benchmark. It powers the Pixel 9 series and uses an eight-core CPU alongside an ARM Mali-G715 MP7 GPU.<sup>[4]</sup>

Notebookcheck records a Geekbench 7 multi-core result of 4,268 for the Tensor G4.<sup>[4]</sup> This provides a useful indication of its CPU capabilities, but it does not establish that the chip is a better gaming processor than the Snapdragon 7 Gen 4.

Gaming performance depends heavily on GPU capabilities, sustained performance and game optimization, none of which can be established from a CPU comparison alone.

The G4's graphics results also show why it should not be confused with a top-tier gaming chipset. Its average 3DMark Sling Shot Extreme Unlimited Physics result is 5,674 points.<sup>[4]</sup>

The practical verdict is that the Tensor G4 can handle mobile gaming, but it is not a compelling choice over newer gaming-focused processors purely for graphics performance.

## What happened to Tensor's graphics performance over the generations?

Google's original Tensor processor illustrates how the company approached its custom silicon project. Announced in October 2021, the original chip used a 5 nm manufacturing process, an eight-core CPU and a Mali-G78 MP20 GPU.<sup>[5]</sup>

Its benchmark profile shows a substantial difference between general processing and graphics performance. NanoReview gives the original Tensor a Geekbench 6 single-core score of 1,477 and a multi-core score of 3,595, alongside a 3DMark Wild Life Extreme score of 1,899.<sup>[5]</sup>

The original chip's graphics test recorded 11 FPS, with 59% stability. In 3DMark Steel Nomad Light, it achieved 704 points and 63% stability.<sup>[5]</sup>

These figures are useful historical context, rather than direct predictions of performance on newer Pixel phones. They also come from different benchmark versions and testing conditions than the Tensor G4 and G6 results, so comparing their raw numbers as if they were measured in one controlled session would be misleading.

The Tensor G6 also demonstrates that newer hardware does not automatically mean better sustained gaming. In the 3DMark Steel Nomad Light stress test, the Pixel 11 scored 1,117, with a lowest loop score of 805 and stability of 69.2%.<sup>[2]</sup>

The Pixel 11 Pro Fold achieved 1,122 in the same benchmark, with a lowest loop score of 678 and stability of 88.4%. By comparison, the Pixel 10 Pro's Tensor G5 scored 1,007, with 95% stability.<sup>[2]</sup>

These results highlight an interesting trade-off. The Tensor G6 can deliver better peak graphics performance than its predecessor, but its lower stability in the standard Pixel 11 suggests that users may not maintain that advantage during longer, demanding gaming sessions.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Why Google Tensor gaming performance has limitations

There are several reasons Tensor can feel less capable in games than its general smartphone performance suggests.

**GPU priorities:** Google has invested heavily in AI and photography capabilities. Gaming graphics have received less attention than they have from some competing chip manufacturers, according to the assessment of Pixel's graphics development across five generations.<sup>[1]</sup>

**Graphics hardware:** The Tensor G6 uses a PowerVR CXTP-48-1536 GPU. Despite the newer processor generation, its graphics performance remains substantially behind leading Snapdragon and Dimensity alternatives.<sup>[3]</sup>

**Game compatibility:** Not every Android game is equally optimized for every GPU architecture. The G6's difficulty running Genshin Impact demonstrates how graphics compatibility can matter just as much as theoretical processing power.<sup>[3]</sup>

**Power management:** Google's performance tuning also affects sustained gaming. The Pixel 11's Tensor G6 achieved 69.2% stability in the 3DMark Steel Nomad Light stress test, while the Pixel 10 Pro recorded 95%.<sup>[2]</sup>

These limitations explain why a Pixel can feel fast when opening applications, switching between tasks or using Google's software features but still fall behind in demanding games.

The distinction matters when evaluating a phone. A strong everyday experience is not proof of strong gaming performance, and a high overall AnTuTu score does not guarantee competitive GPU performance.

## Is a Google Pixel good enough for mobile gaming?

That depends on the games being played and the performance expectations.

For casual games, lighter Android titles and well-optimized competitive games, Tensor-powered Pixels can provide a perfectly usable experience. The Tensor G6's 120 FPS result in Call of Duty: Mobile is a good example of what the hardware can achieve when a game is well supported.<sup>[3]</sup>

However, people who regularly play demanding open-world games or want high graphics settings should pay closer attention to GPU benchmarks than general processor scores.

Here is how the different gaming requirements compare:

| Gaming requirement | Tensor suitability | 
|---|---|
| Casual mobile games | Good | 
| Call of Duty: Mobile | Good | 
| High-refresh competitive gaming | Game-dependent | 
| BGMI at 120 FPS | Not demonstrated | 
| Genshin Impact | Compatibility concerns | 
| Maximum graphics settings | Limited | 
| Dedicated gaming performance | Below flagship rivals | 

These are practical assessments based on the reported G6 and Pixel 10 gaming results, not guarantees for every Pixel model or game version.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>

The Pixel 10 series also illustrates the price-to-performance problem. Android Authority's testing found that the latest Pixel could still run modern games, but competing phones offered substantially better frame rates and graphics capabilities.<sup>[1]</sup>

For someone who mainly wants a reliable Android smartphone with good everyday performance, gaming may not be a deal-breaker. For someone choosing a phone primarily for gaming, Snapdragon flagship devices and competing Dimensity-powered phones offer a stronger graphics proposition.
