Takeaways Up Front

1. The European Commission confirmed an AI-generated cyber campaign disrupted its internal staff systems for more than 10 days.

2. Payroll, leave, and medical-reimbursement platforms went offline, though the Commission reports no evidence of a staff-wide data breach.

3. ARTEX is the AI framework an FBI advisory this month flagged as hitting government and defense targets worldwide.

The European Commission confirmed Thursday that an artificial intelligence-driven cyber campaign disrupted its internal staff systems for more than 10 days, knocking payroll, leave and medical-reimbursement platforms offline and prompting an emergency response from senior officials.

Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier said the campaign was detected on October 22, that investigators had found no evidence of a data breach affecting staff as a whole, and that no ransom had been demanded. He declined to attribute the attack.

CERT-EU, the cybersecurity service for European Union institutions, identified the attackers’ use of ARTEX, a publicly available AI hacking framework, according to Politico, which first reported the incident.

What the Attack Disrupted

Sysper, the Commission’s human resources platform for payslips, leave requests and timesheets, and MyPMO, its medical reimbursement system, were offline for much of the disruption before returning earlier this week. Sysper briefly crashed again Wednesday, and translation services went down Tuesday.

The Commission’s IT department, DG DIGIT, is assessing the damage, and two EU officials told Euronews that both CERT-EU and the European Data Protection Supervisor were notified. The affected platforms hold officials’ names, pay grades, home addresses and contact details, records that could support targeted operations against individual staff.

The Commission convened its highest internal crisis body, the Corporate Management Board, for an urgent meeting over the incidents earlier this month, according to a leaked internal email reported by Politico. Technology Commissioner Henna Virkkunen briefed fellow commissioners on the response Wednesday.

An AI Tool Washington and Brussels Had Flagged

ARTEX is an open-source framework that uses large language models to automate cyber intrusions from reconnaissance through data theft.

In a November 13 advisory, the U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation said criminal and state-aligned actors had used it since at least August against targets in the financial, government, technology, defense, energy, healthcare, and telecommunications sectors, with some intrusions reaching privileged access in under three hours.

The developer released ARTEX on the code-sharing site GitHub in July and pulled it in October after reports of misuse, though community copies and a commercial edition called ARTEX PRO keep it available.

The FBI cited research counting more than 1,600 ARTEX consoles exposed on the open internet by early November. The Commission detected the campaign on October 22 and disclosed it November 19, six days after the FBI advisory.

Shaun Williams, a senior director at the security firm SentinelOne, said the concern was less the tool itself than how widely available AI systems now let attackers find and exploit flaws “faster, more effectively, cheaper, and with less skill than ever before,” in remarks to The Record.

The Commission has been breached before. In March, attackers stole roughly 91.7 gigabytes of data, including personal information and email content, from cloud infrastructure hosting its Europa.eu websites, and a January intrusion into its mobile-device systems may have exposed staff names and phone numbers, Euronews reported.

Regnier said the investigation remains open and that the Commission would act on findings as they emerge. Whether the attackers accessed staff data remains unclear.
