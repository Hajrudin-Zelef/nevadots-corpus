Relief for GPU buyers now hinges on memory supply, and the supplier and analyst dates for that supply, not for any GPU launch, cluster around 2028. TrendForce and IDC warn that meaningful new capacity is unlikely before late 2027 or 2028, according to TechTimes’ May 2026 report, and SK Hynix’s chairman said production will lag demand by 20% until at least 2030, as Engadget reports. The squeeze began with a first quarter in which conventional DRAM contract prices rose 93-98%, per TrendForce’s June 2026 release, more than the 90-95% to which TrendForce’s February 2026 release had raised its estimate from 55-60%. TrendForce projected Q2 would add a further 58-63%, so the question for buyers is no longer whether prices rose but how long the new level lasts.

## Q1 landed at 93-98%, and Q2 was projected at a further 58-63%

Three Q1 figures circulate, each for a different quantity. TrendForce’s February release raised its estimate of the quarter-on-quarter rise in conventional DRAM contract prices from 55-60% to 90-95%, which makes 90-95% a revised forecast. The June release puts the outcome at about 93-98%, with DRAM industry revenue up 81% quarter on quarter to $97 billion; Rillor’s Q1 compute market review reports the same figures. Server DRAM, a narrower category, rose roughly 90%, the largest quarterly increase on record, according to the Ldstn newsletter. A page that quotes 90-95% as the Q1 result is quoting the February estimate, and one that quotes about 90% is describing server DRAM.

TrendForce’s 31 March 2026 release projected Q2 conventional DRAM contract prices at +58-63%. TrendForce’s 3 July 2026 release forecasts a Q3 moderation to +13-18% for conventional DRAM and attributes it to buyers: consumer-market customers “are reaching their affordability limit”. The slowdown reflects that resistance, not an improvement in supply. Voxbooster’s DRAM statistics page, which lists the same 13-18% as server DRAM, puts it bluntly: “A slower rate of increase on a tripled base is still an increase.”

NAND ran alongside DRAM; the NAND column of the next table carries the quarterly contract price rises that Hiper Global’s mid-2026 update, citing TrendForce, reports. Tech Insider’s reports on the NAND shortage and SSD prices and on DRAM and NAND contract prices cover that side.

| Quarter | DRAM contract prices, quarter on quarter | NAND contract prices, quarter on quarter | 
|---|---|---|
| Q1 2026 | +93-98% (conventional) | +55-60% | 
| Q2 2026 | +58-63% (conventional, projected) | +70-75% (projected) | 
| Q3 2026 | +13-18% (conventional, forecast) | +10-15% (projected) | 

Chaining the midpoints of the Q1 and Q2 ranges (95.5% and 60.5%) puts contract prices at about 3.1 times their Q4 2025 level by the end of Q2, even though the quarterly rate fell by more than a third between the two quarters.

That is why “relief” needs sorting into two kinds. A slower rate of price rises is already visible in the Q3 forecast. Lower prices need new supply or a ceiling on what buyers can pay, and the supplier dates below put new supply no earlier than late 2027. Only the first has happened, and it is not a fall in the price level. The knock-on reaches finished products too: Gartner projects 2026 PC shipments down 10.4% and smartphone shipments down 8.4% because of memory costs.

## Who says 2028, and how far each statement reaches

Every row below concerns memory supply or memory prices, and the scope column shows where each statement stops.

| Speaker | Statement | Scope | Where reported | 
|---|---|---|---|
| TrendForce and IDC | Meaningful new capacity unlikely before late 2027 or 2028 | Memory supply | TechTimes, May 2026 | 
| Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra | Ldstn reports he has said meaningful large-scale capacity is unlikely before 2028 | Memory supply | Ldstn newsletter | 
| SK Hynix chairman | At least four to five years to build adequate wafer supply; production 20% behind demand until at least 2030 | DRAM wafer supply | Engadget | 
| SK hynix | Long-term agreements cover more than 50% of total production capacity for 2027 and 2028; demand expected to exceed supply through 2030 | Memory supply and demand | SK hynix Q3 2026 results release, 28 October 2026 | 
| TechInsights | Biggest memory shortage in history; DRAM and NAND prices elevated through the rest of the decade | Prices | Hiper Global mid-2026 update | 

None of these is a GPU launch date, so the 2028 figures here are supplier and analyst statements about memory. The three dates in the table answer different questions. Late 2027 to 2028 is when supply starts to expand in meaningful volume. About 2030 is when, on the SK Hynix chairman’s estimate and SK hynix’s own release, production stops lagging demand. Taken together, the dates put 2028 as the start of easing, not the end of the shortage, and that distinction is the one a buyer waiting for cheaper cards needs.

The fab calendar shows why. Micron’s press release says it still expects initial wafer output at its first Idaho fab in mid-calendar 2027 and that ground preparation has begun for its second Idaho fab, and a separate Micron release expects first wafer output in late calendar 2028 for that second fab. A fab that starts producing wafers in mid-2027 still has to ramp, so first output is not the moment memory gets cheaper. Meanwhile, Engadget reports that many producers have already sold much of their 2027 supply and are focusing sales on 2028 and beyond, and that Micron has reportedly sold 75% of its 2027 HBM output. TrendForce’s July 2026 release anticipates a 2027 server DRAM shortage, with RDIMM bit supply growing only 15-20% year over year.

Micron’s fiscal Q4 2026 results (the quarter ended 3 September 2026; call held 30 September 2026) show a GAAP gross margin of 86.8% (87.0% non-GAAP) and GAAP net income of $37.70 billion on revenue of $54.23 billion. Those are figures for the single quarter, not the fiscal year, for which GAAP net income was $84.97 billion. Returns like that give suppliers little financial reason to hurry capacity. Source: Micron’s results release, checked on 12 November 2026, and the same release as filed with the SEC.

The obvious counterargument is Chinese capacity. The Ldstn newsletter notes that CXMT is adding more DRAM wafer capacity this year than Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron combined, but concludes that it only eases the shortage. Chip equipment is a second constraint, covered in Tech Insider’s report on AI demand outrunning chip-tool supply through 2028.

## Why the gaming card loses the wafer: a gigabyte of HBM uses the area of three to four gigabytes of DRAM, TechTimes reports

HBM and ordinary DRAM draw on the same wafers, per the Ldstn newsletter. TechTimes’ August 2026 report says one gigabyte of HBM consumes roughly the wafer area that three to four gigabytes of standard DRAM would need, so every gigabyte shipped to an AI server displaces that much ordinary DRAM. IDC forecasts supply growth of 16% for DRAM and 17% for NAND in 2026, “well below historical norms”; the Ldstn newsletter gives the post-2018 norm for ordinary DRAM as 20-30% a year. Tech Insider’s report on Micron and the memory crunch covers the supply-growth side in more detail.

Nvidia’s own comments show which side wins. Its CFO said, “We are experiencing extreme pricing conditions in memory,” according to TechTimes, and that customer forecasts point to doubling next year while Nvidia’s guidance holds at 70% because the supply chain cannot keep up. The Neysa blog, a vendor source rather than an Nvidia figure and one no other source here corroborates, says Nvidia cut RTX 50-series consumer production by 30-40% in the first half of 2026, redirecting wafer capacity to data-center Blackwell parts. Gartner’s 24 August 2026 forecast puts memory revenue at $837 billion in 2026 and above $1 trillion in 2027; TechTimes adds Gartner’s projection that DRAM revenue rises 246.6% in 2026.

## What a GPU costs now: the RTX 5090 at $4,920, H100s renewed at old rates

Engadget reports the RTX 5090 retailing at $4,920 against its $1,999 launch price, roughly 2.5 times the list price.

On the data-center side, the contract record suggests demand is sticky. SemiAnalysis, via Rillor, reports H100s being renewed “at the exact same rate they were signed at 2-3 years ago,” some for four years through 2028.

## What could ease prices before new fabs open: hyperscaler capex now runs on debt

The Ldstn newsletter makes the one demand-side case for earlier relief: hyperscaler free cash flow no longer covers capex, so debt and equity increasingly fund it. In its words, “Scarcity still sets the price, but the cost of capital sets the ceiling on how far scarcity can push it.” If funding tightens, hyperscalers could pay less for memory and the contract price slope could flatten sooner.

In our assessment the current evidence runs the other way. Nvidia’s CFO says demand points to doubling next year while supply allows 70%, which describes demand limited by supply and not by money. SemiAnalysis found its one-year H100 contract index rising from $1.70 per GPU-hour in October 2025 to $2.35 by March 2026, almost 40%, with on-demand capacity sold out. A funding squeeze would more likely flatten the rate of rises, the Q3 pattern TrendForce already attributes to buyers’ affordability limits, well before it lowered the level. Lowering the level takes new supply: Micron’s Idaho fabs and CXMT’s expansion, which the dates above place from 2027 to 2028. Even then, a card is unlikely to return to its launch MSRP while HBM keeps competing for the same wafers.

## What a 2028 horizon means for a card, for memory and for rented GPU-hours

### A graphics card

If the current card works, waiting is a bet on 2028 or later: the supplier and analyst dates above start at late 2027, and producers have already sold much of 2027 supply. If a card is needed now, the Engadget figure above shows what the top of the range costs, and a card bought this year should be budgeted as one that has to last through 2028.

### Memory and prebuilts

Memory is the cost driver, and its relief is dated to the same window as the cards: Tech Insider’s report on DDR5 chip prices and relief slipping to 2028 covers kit prices. For server and industrial builds, Hiper Global says module lead times have stretched past 30 weeks in some cases, with allocation controls meaning early orders effectively set deployment schedules. Order dates, not list prices, decide when that hardware arrives.

### Rented GPU-hours and servers

The one-year H100 index rose almost 40% in five months, while existing holders renewed at old rates, some for four years through 2028. A term that spans the shortage fixes today’s rate while the short-term market climbs; on that record, a multi-year commitment is the lower-cost route for capacity needed through 2028, with spot kept for bursts.
