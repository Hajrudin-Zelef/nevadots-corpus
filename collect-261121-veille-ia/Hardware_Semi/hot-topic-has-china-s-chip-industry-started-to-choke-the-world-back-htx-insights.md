November 9, 2026, at the regular press conference of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. An AFP reporter asked: Reuters reports that about 600,000 advanced NVIDIA AI chips are stored in Hong Kong, waiting to be shipped to mainland China; although tech companies have demand, the Chinese side has refused to approve large-scale imports. Spokesperson Guo Jiakun answered with just one sentence: "China's position on the issue of U.S. chip exports to China is consistent. For specific details, I suggest you ask the competent Chinese authorities."

On the same day, a Bloomberg reporter followed up on "how China would respond if the U.S. adopts stricter chip restrictions," and received an identical answer word for word. No confirmation of quantity, no explanation of standards, no response on "where it is stuck."

But this ambiguity is more informative than an explicit statement. Over the past three years, the keyword for China's AI industry has been "can't buy chips," with the constraint in Washington; now about 600,000 H200s that have already obtained U.S. export licenses are sitting in Hong Kong warehouses, and the line awaiting signature is in Beijing. The U.S. has already let them through; Beijing has not signed. U.S. Commerce Secretary Lutnick said bluntly at a Senate Appropriations Committee hearing on April 22, 2026: "I can tell you clearly that, to date, they have not bought a single chip."

This picture is easy to read as a clean narrative reversal. But as a judgment, "reverse choking" needs to be precisely unpacked: China has indeed gained the ability to say "no" at several asymmetric nodes in the chip supply chain, but this capability is still a long way from equivalent countermeasures in the sense of technological sovereignty.

First, see where this deal sits on NVIDIA's books: small enough to "not approve"

600,000 H200s stranded in Hong Kong should intuitively make NVIDIA anxious. But when the financial data are broken down, this intuition needs correction.

In the second quarter of fiscal 2027 (ended July 26, 2026), NVIDIA's revenue was $96.221 billion, of which data center revenue was $89.023 billion, accounting for 92.5%, up 117% year on year. Shipments of Hopper products to China were less than 1% of data center revenue, estimated at less than $890 million at the literal upper limit; mainland China and Hong Kong combined were $7.88 billion, 8.2% of total revenue. Jensen Huang disclosed at the annual shareholders meeting that China's revenue share had fallen to about 9% in fiscal 2026, compared with 26.4% in fiscal 2022 before export controls.

Even harder than these numbers are two sentences in the annual report. First, over the past six months, the company recorded about $400 million in charges for excess H200 inventory and purchase obligations; second, the company itself assessed that without approval from both governments, it "has in effect been foreclosed from competing in China's data center computing market," and this guidance no longer assumes any data center revenue from China. The appearance of the word "foreclosed" in a company's annual report says more than any analyst's judgment.

Jensen Huang was even more direct in a CNBC interview on May 21, 2026: "Huawei is very, very strong... we have in effect given that market to them."

There is another layer that is easily overlooked: H200 was approved for small-batch exports to China in February 2026, but the annual report states that "the program generated no revenue," and H200s must pay a 25% tariff when shipped back to the United States. The production and sales chain of this product line itself has to cross a U.S. tariff, and its cost structure is no longer just the price of a chip.

This means that the marginal damage of "not approving" to NVIDIA's current revenue is controllable. With 600,000 chips stuck in Hong Kong, its global AI computing business is still expanding at more than double the rate. But the symbolic significance of this business far exceeds its financial significance: if even chips approved for export cannot get in, NVIDIA's presence in China's data center market will accelerate toward zero, and Huawei's ecosystem will continue to consolidate in an environment of "no competition." One precondition for Beijing to dare to slow the approval pace so much is that NVIDIA's commercial weight in the Chinese market has already become too small to constitute reverse pressure.

What domestic substitution has caught, and what it has not

The reason H200s can be stranded in Hong Kong is another precondition: domestic AI chips can already hold up in a considerable portion of scenarios.

IDC data for the first half of 2026 provide a clear刻度: total shipments in China's data center AI accelerator market were 2.008 million units, with NVIDIA at 678,000 units, a 34% share, down 44% year on year; Huawei Ascend at 572,000 units, a 28% share, compared with more than 300,000 units in the same period last year, narrowing the gap between the two from 31 percentage points a year earlier to 6 percentage points. Alibaba Pingtouge 7%, Cambricon 5%, Kunlunxin 4%, Hygon 3%. More symbolic is the structural change: non-GPU accelerator shipments were 1.016 million units, surpassing GPU shipments of 991,000 units for the first time, up nearly 80% year on year, and these four domestic vendors together accounted for 95% of the non-GPU market. Of the 11 Chinese chip vendors tracked by IDC, 9 saw shipment growth in the first half. Morgan Stanley expects China's AI chip self-sufficiency rate to rise from 35% in 2025 to 45% in 2026 and jump to 68% in 2027.

However, "share" depends on who is counting and what is counted. Huawei Rotating Chairman Xu Zhijun said in September 2026 that, based on data that can be counted, Ascend's shipments in the Chinese market have already surpassed NVIDIA's; IDC's figure is 34% versus 28%. The former refers to all accelerator cards, while the latter is limited to data center accelerators. Both numbers are true, but the denominators differ—this itself is a feature of a market in restructuring: even the most basic statistical boundaries are still moving.

Beijing's approval standard for H200 confirms a layered strategy: prioritize domestic chips for inference, and mainly reserve imported chips for training. This strategy has already landed at the software layer. DeepSeek's V4 runs entirely on Ascend; on September 30, 2026, it open-sourced a full set of infrastructure components for Ascend—the TileLang compilation tool, the DeepGEMM operator library, and the DeepEP communication library—existing in parallel with corresponding components for NVIDIA's platform, with the same DeepInfer code supporting both Ascend 950 and NVIDIA SM100, and switching hardware by changing one environment variable. This is far more important than "catching up in performance": it turns "not using CUDA" from a slogan into a deployable, measurable, and comparable engineering option. In other words, the part of "reverse choking" that holds on AI chips is "for inference, I can choose not to buy yours."

But the gap on the training side cannot be ignored, and the bottleneck is not only in chip design. Ascend 910C uses dual-die packaging, with FP16 peak performance of about 800 TFLOPS and 96GB HBM integrated; its overall performance is about 60% to 70% of H100, and the DeepSeek team measured inference performance at about 60% of H100. The per-card gap is made up by the system: the 384 supernode uses self-developed HCCS to connect 384 cards into one computing pool, with more than 500 sets deployed, and the CloudMatrix 384 cluster has computing power of about 300 PFlops, at the cost of about four times the power consumption.

The real bottlenecks are in two places. First, production capacity: the 2026 production target for Ascend 910C is about 600,000 units, and the target for all-series wafers is 1.6 million, nearly double 2025, but the wafer demand corresponding to 600,000 units is about 35,000 wafers per month, already exceeding the current total capacity of SMIC's N+2 line; either capacity must be expanded before year-end or output diverted to other nodes. Second, yield: estimates for SMIC N+2 yield range from 20% to 70% across institutions—about 20% in early 2024, about 40% in early 2025, and 65% to 70% in Bloomberg's industry citation in 2026—all below TSMC's roughly 90% at the same process. The divergence in estimates itself shows one thing: the real data for this line are not transparent, and cost happens to hide in that opacity. As for HBM, global HBM revenue share in the second quarter of 2026 was SK Hynix 50%, Samsung 33%, and Micron 18%, while Chinese vendors are still at the sample-delivery stage with no share.

Therefore, "reverse choking" does not hold on the training side. China still needs a limited import window to fill the high-end computing gap, but this window has been deliberately narrowed.

What comes closest to "reverse choking" is precisely not chips themselves

If "reverse choking" means having veto power at a link the other side cannot replace, then China's closest claim to this definition in chip-related fields is not any chip, but the raw materials on which chip manufacturing depends.

First, state the status of this card accurately. On December 3, 2024, Ministry of Commerce Announcement No. 46 of 2024: paragraph 1 prohibits the export of dual-use items to U.S. military users or for military use; paragraph 2 states that "in principle, no license will be granted for the export of gallium, germanium, antimony, superhard materials and related dual-use items to the United States," and imposes stricter end-user and end-use review on graphite. On November 9, 2025, Ministry of Commerce Announcement No. 72 of 2025: paragraph 2 is suspended, until November 27, 2026.

That is to say, only the civilian half is suspended; the ban on military users and military use has never loosened; and the civilian half is currently in a suspension period, with seventeen days left before expiration. The card is on the table, with a hand pressing it but not playing it. Whether it resumes or is extended after seventeen days is a variable more worth watching than "600,000 units" right now.

The numbers are ready-made. Consulting firm Project Blue estimates that in 2025 China will account for 98.9% of global primary gallium supply and 68.6% of germanium supply. S&P Global expects that by the end of 2026, gallium supply capacity outside China will be about 20 tons, with a gap of about 678 tons; germanium metal output will be about 31 tons, 177 tons short of demand. Reuters reported in September that since controls began in 2023, prices of these two metals in markets outside China have risen to 9 to 10 times their levels at that time.

The most intuitive comparison is not in the gap, but in the physical form of capacity. Gallium is not mined; it is a by-product of alumina production. Industry research gives a ratio of about 200,000 tons of alumina capacity to support every 1 ton of gallium; China has more than 55% of global alumina capacity. Sanmenxia Aluminum alone has metal gallium capacity of 290 tons per year—fourteen times the expected supply capacity of all regions outside China by the end of 2026. The same applies to germanium: it is a by-product of zinc smelting, and to extract germanium, one must first have zinc smelters. Even under the most optimistic path: if the eight announced projects proceed smoothly and non-China gallium supply rises from about 5 tons currently to 386 tons by 2030, Reuters calculations based on S&P data show that the non-China market will still need about 65% of its gallium from China by then; germanium refining capacity rising to 126 tons would cover only about 48% of local demand.

The common feature of these materials is that they are used in extremely small amounts, but without them the entire production line stops. Gallium is the basis for gallium nitride RF devices and advanced radar, and germanium is the core of infrared optics and optical fiber. Switching suppliers takes years and huge certification investment, and China's concentration of capacity means there is no second option in the short term. It restricts not design capability or manufacturing process, but those elements in the periodic table for which China happens to have reserve advantages and refining capabilities. This is currently the most substantial reverse card, but its nature is veto power in the physical supply chain, not dominance brought by technological leadership.

Memory is another asymmetric node taking shape. In the second quarter of 2026, ChangXin Memory's DRAM revenue share rose from 4% in the same period last year to 10%, ranking fourth globally, with revenue up 716% year on year; Yangtze Memory's NAND shipment share reached 17%, rising to third globally, with a bit-based share of 14%, and its revenue share also rose from 9% to 14% within four quarters. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron together still account for about 90% of the DRAM market and more than 60% of the NAND market.

It does not "choke" anyone, but it weakens the ability of a few giants to price at will. When Samsung and SK Hynix shift capacity to HBM and choose to control conventional memory supply to maintain prices, incremental supply from ChangXin and Yangtze Memory is the marginal force pushing prices down. This is a kind of "unavoidable in the price mechanism," not the same as gallium and germanium's "physically unavoidable," but it is likewise asymmetric.

Hong Kong's 600,000 chips: the physical sediment of regulatory friction

The 600,000 H200s will not rot in warehouses, but their circulation path itself is a specimen for understanding this game.

According to a Reuters report on November 5, about 600,000 chips are stored in Hong Kong awaiting shipment to the mainland; in recent months some have been released, with several large Chinese internet companies each obtaining about 10,000 chips, and a small number entering through middlemen and indirect channels. Price signals explain the state better than quantities: by the end of October, the quote for an eight-card H200 server in the mainland had exceeded 6 million yuan, about three times that of a year earlier, and the quote for a Blackwell server exceeded 20 million yuan. Hong Kong is not a low-price dumping ground; it is a dammed lake of high prices and shortage.

The huge profits of gray channels are documented. On August 24, the Keelung District Prosecutors Office in Taiwan indicted nine people: a company used a Japanese company as an intermediary break point to obtain whitelist status, forged on-site data center inspections, obtained 130 servers equipped with B300 from Supermicro, of which 74 were transshipped to the mainland via Indonesia, Japan, and Hong Kong, making a profit of $21.2 million after deducting purchase costs, equivalent to about NT$640 million, with a per-unit cut of nearly $290,000. The "toll fee" for one server explains better than the markup on the chip itself why this gray chain cannot be welded shut.

Both ends of regulation are escalating. On January 13, 2026, the U.S. side changed H200 exports to China from "presumption of denial" to "case-by-case review," while adding four hard conditions: a third-party laboratory must verify that its AI capability is "below H200"; the number of chips obtained by Chinese customers must not exceed 50% of the total purchased by U.S. customers; it must be proven that U.S. market supply is sufficient and does not crowd out U.S. customer capacity; and the recipient must have adequate security procedures and must not be used for military purposes. Another 25% revenue share is added. On May 31, BIS issued further enforcement guidance: as long as the counterparty's headquarters or its ultimate parent company's headquarters is located in Country Group D:5 (including mainland China and Hong Kong) or Macau, even if registered in a third country, exports of 3A090 and 4A090 advanced computing items to it still require prior authorization, with the review standard maintained at "presumption of denial." This explains why Hong Kong can only be a warehouse and not a passage—intermediary entities registered in third countries are pierced all the way to headquarters.

China's concerns are not without basis. In July 2025, the Cyberspace Administration of China summoned NVIDIA over the "backdoor risk" of H20 and required explanatory materials; Reuters reported in January this year that NVIDIA requires Chinese customers to pay in full in advance, with orders non-cancelable and non-refundable, and configuration cannot be changed after ordering. Under the expectation that supply may be cut off at any time, full prepayment means leaving all supply-cut risk to the buyer. Negotiations are therefore stuck on terms, not just on approval.

Hong Kong has thus become an institutional buffer zone: two major powers impose opposite but extremely complex regulatory requirements on the same batch of goods, and inventory settles there.

The time account of the generation gap: H200 is a depreciating ticket

There is another easily overlooked rational element: Beijing's caution on large-scale imports is not only industrial protection, but also an asset depreciation calculation.

H200 belongs to the Hopper architecture, with 141GB HBM3e and 4.8TB/s bandwidth. B200 has 192GB HBM3e and 8TB/s. These two numbers cannot be directly compared on FP4—H200 does not support FP4 at all; the comparable metric is FP8, where B200 is about twice H200 and uniquely supports FP4. By 2026, NVIDIA's main push is already Vera Rubin (288GB HBM4), which had already entered full mass production by the second quarter of fiscal 2027. H200 was the main chip of 2024 to 2025; the generation gap had already opened by 2026 and will continue to widen.

The underlying logic of U.S. approval of H200 exports to China is "second-best export": what is given to you is the previous generation, allowing you to run but not run fast. Senator Coons of Delaware said at the hearing that H200's performance is six times that of the previously approved H20—that sentence was meant for U.S. lawmakers, but it also explains another thing: what Washington is willing to sell to China is always one or even two generations behind the most advanced.

If H200s are purchased in large quantities, when Vera Rubin spreads two years later, this batch of cards will depreciate faster because of the lack of FP4 support and lagging memory bandwidth. Buying a rapidly depreciating admission ticket is worse than leaving capital expenditure for domestic capacity that is still ramping up. Whether this tradeoff is wise depends on whether domestic 7nm capacity and the HBM supply chain can be released as scheduled in 2027 to 2028; but the logic of the decision itself is far more complex than "Beijing just won't approve."

The precise meaning of "reverse choking"

Putting the four lines together, a more accurate picture emerges.

China has not achieved "reverse choking" of the United States in the core links of chip manufacturing—advanced processes, EUV lithography machines, high-end HBM. These fields remain one-way dependence, and dependence that cannot be made symmetric in the short term. China's reverse capability is concentrated in three asymmetric nodes: absolute control at the raw material end (gallium 98.9%, germanium 68.6%), access rights at the demand end (whether H200s enter and how many enter is decided by Beijing, and is subject to the U.S. side's hard 50% ratio constraint), and the structural impact of mature capacity on global prices (10% of DRAM, 17% of NAND).

All three share the same logic: they do not require you to lead in technology, only to occupy a position in the other side's supply chain that is "unavoidable and has no short-term substitute." Gallium and germanium are physically unavoidable; H200 access is unavoidable because of market size; memory capacity is unavoidable through the price mechanism. Conversely, U.S. advantages in advanced processes, high-end equipment, and HBM are likewise unavoidable for China.

The real change is not that the direction of "choking" has reversed, but that the structure of the game has changed from "one side chokes, one side suffers" to "each side has nodes the other cannot avoid." Being able to say no on raw materials and being able to say yes on lithography machines are two completely different kinds of power. China is learning to use the former, while the answer to the latter is still written in the processes that have not yet been broken through.

The reason the Foreign Ministry's sentence "the position is consistent" needs no explanation is that behind it stands not a single statement, but this entire slowly restructuring structure of dependence—it is not yet symmetric, but it is no longer one-way. In seventeen days, the gallium and germanium card expires; whether it resumes or is extended will be the next moment when this structure develops.

What truly changes the China-U.S. chip game has never been the 600,000 H200s stranded in Hong Kong, but the gallium and germanium control window expiring on November 27. All our current "reverse gaming capability" is built on an asymmetric structure in which technology still lags but raw materials have absolute voice.

The technical barriers of lithography machines and high-end HBM cannot be broken through in the short term; but the export authority over strategic raw materials such as gallium and germanium is in our hands. In seventeen days, whether this key policy is extended, whether the original controls are restored, and whether it is tightened again will directly rewrite the cost and supply landscape of the global AI, RF, defense, and optical industry chains. This is the biggest hidden variable in the semiconductor track at the end of 2026.
