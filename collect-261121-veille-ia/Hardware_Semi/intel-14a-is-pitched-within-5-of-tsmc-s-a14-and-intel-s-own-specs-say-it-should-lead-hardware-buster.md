**Intel Foundry’s top technologist sets a modest bar for the 1.4nm-class race, even though the published specs point to a lead.**


Intel is telling investors that its Intel 14A process will land “within 5%” of TSMC’s A14 on performance. That is a surprisingly cautious framing for a node the company has spent two years pitching as its route back to process leadership. The remark came from Naga Chandrasekaran, Intel’s CTO and head of Intel Foundry, in a conversation with KeyBanc, as reported by Tom’s Hardware.

“Within 5%” cuts both ways. It could mean slightly ahead, slightly behind, or dead level, and Intel didn’t say which. What makes it odd is that the company’s own published targets suggest the gap should favour 14A.

## What the spec sheets say

Intel rates 14A at 15-20% higher performance than 18A at the same power, or 25-35% lower power at the same clocks. TSMC quotes A14 at 10-15% faster than N2 at the same power, or 25-30% lower power at the same speed, alongside roughly 20% more logic density.

The missing piece is the baseline: how 18A stacks up against N2. Shipping silicon gives a rough read. Intel’s Core Ultra X9 388H, built on 18A, turbos to 5.1 GHz. On N2, AMD’s EPYC 9586F tops out at 5.0 GHz and Apple’s M6 at 4.78 GHz. Peak clocks are a crude proxy for a process, since core design, voltage targets and thermal limits all move them. Still, stacking each vendor’s claimed gains on top of those figures puts 14A somewhere between 2% and 16% ahead of A14, depending on which chips you compare.

So why undersell? The likeliest reading is that a foundry courting outside customers would rather under-promise. Intel has missed process targets before, and a company designing a chip three years out cares more about predictable results than a headline percentage. A “within 5%” claim that turns into a small lead in 2028 is a far better outcome for Intel than a bold promise that slips.

## TSMC isn’t standing still

The comment lands the same week as reports that TSMC has begun A14 pilot production in Taiwan, ahead of risk production next year and volume output in 2028. According to TrendForce, citing Taiwan’s Commercial Times, formal pilot runs at Fab 20 in Hsinchu could be pulled forward to the first quarter of 2027, with the new Fab 25 in Taichung following later that year. A14 test vehicles are said to be reaching around 90% of target device performance, with SRAM yields approaching 90%.

Intel’s own schedule is close behind, or level. CEO Lip-Bu Tan has pointed to 14A risk production in early 2027, internal products in the second half of that year, and a high-volume ramp in 2028. For the first time in most of a decade, Intel and TSMC’s leading nodes could arrive within months of each other.

## What actually decides it

Performance parity is only one line on a foundry customer’s checklist. Yield and cost matter at least as much, and TrendForce puts Intel 14A’s defect density at around 0.5 in June, with a target of 0.1-0.2 by early 2027. That is the number to watch over the next two quarters.

For PC buyers, none of this reaches a desktop before 2028 at the earliest. But whether Intel’s own CPU teams, and paying outside customers, commit to 14A instead of TSMC will decide whether Intel Foundry becomes a genuine second source. “Within 5%” is now the figure they will hold it to.
