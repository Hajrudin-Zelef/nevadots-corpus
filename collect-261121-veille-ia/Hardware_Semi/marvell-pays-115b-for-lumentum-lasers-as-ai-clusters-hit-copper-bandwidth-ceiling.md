Marvell Technology paid a $115 billion enterprise-value price on Monday to own the lasers that copper wires cannot replace inside next-generation AI data centers — and the market's response was to sell both companies' shares, with Marvell closing down approximately 13% and Lumentum down nearly 19%, an unusual double decline that signals deep skepticism about whether the deal will close, the timeline to do so, and the dilutive all-stock deal structure that ties Lumentum shareholders' returns directly to Marvell's volatile price. The gap between Lumentum's closing price of roughly $684 per share and the deal's implied $1,132 per share — approximately $448, or 40% — is the market's visible estimate that this transaction is not certain.

### Why Copper Can No Longer Keep Up

The engineering case for this deal rests on a physical limit that chipmakers have been approaching for years and that frontier AI workloads have now reached. Copper interconnects transmit data as electrical signals through metal traces. As data rates rise, electrons encounter resistive loss, crosstalk, and electromagnetic interference along those traces, requiring active amplification that consumes increasingly impractical amounts of power.

At the scale of today's AI training clusters — thousands of accelerators exchanging hundreds of terabits of data per second across racks and pods — copper's energy cost and bandwidth ceiling are no longer theoretical constraints. They are architectural obstacles. An AI data center operator cannot simply add more copper traces and more amplifiers: the power draw and heat generated by the correction circuitry would overwhelm the facility's cooling capacity before the bandwidth target was reached.

The solution is light. Photons do not experience resistive losses, travel roughly three times faster than electrons through a medium, and can carry multiple independent data streams simultaneously on a single fiber at different wavelengths — a technique called dense wavelength-division multiplexing (DWDM). Optical interconnects cut energy use 40 to 80% compared with copper equivalents at rack-to-rack distances; co-packaged optics (CPO) — the next-generation architecture in which the optical engine is integrated directly into the same package as the AI chip — can reduce power per link by up to 90%.

### What Makes Indium Phosphide the Strategic Material of the Deal

Silicon dominates semiconductor manufacturing for good reason: decades of process refinement, enormous wafer sizes, and a global supply chain optimized for it. But silicon has a critical limitation for optical systems: silicon has an indirect bandgap that limits light emission, preventing it from efficiently emitting light at the wavelengths — roughly 1,310 to 1,550 nanometers (nm) — required for data-center optical networks. Those wavelengths represent the range where optical fiber achieves its lowest attenuation (approximately 0.2 dB per kilometer), making them the only practical choice for high-speed links.

Indium phosphide (InP), a compound semiconductor made from indium and phosphorus, has a direct bandgap and natively emits light precisely in that 1,310–1,550 nm range. It is the material of choice for the laser diodes, optical transceivers, optical circuit switches, and photonic integrated circuits that move light through data-center fiber networks.

Lumentum operates its own indium phosphide fabrication facilities — fabs that silicon foundries cannot simply replicate, because InP requires entirely different manufacturing processes, crystal growth techniques, and equipment than silicon. InP production is also severely capacity-constrained: roughly 60% of the world's InP wafers come from a single Japanese supplier, Sumitomo Electric, and the industry has largely remained on legacy 3- to 4-inch wafer formats rather than the 12-inch standard silicon uses today. Lumentum's chief executive, Michael Hurlston, said the company could not meet roughly 70% of anticipated demand for certain products through 2027, and its optical capacity sold out through 2029.

That supply bottleneck is precisely what makes the $115 billion price tag legible. Marvell is not simply buying a customer relationship or a product line — it is buying a constrained, specialized manufacturing capability that cannot be replicated on short notice and that the hyperscale data center operators who are Marvell's primary customers need urgently.

### How Marvell and Lumentum Fit Together

Until this deal, Marvell and Lumentum occupied adjacent but distinct positions in the AI connectivity supply chain. Marvell designed the electrical side: custom AI accelerators (called XPUs in the company's nomenclature), networking switch chips, and optical digital signal processors (DSPs) — the semiconductor circuits that process, encode, and prepare signals for transmission. Lumentum supplied the optical side: the laser sources, optical transceivers, optical circuit switches, and the underlying InP material platform that converts those electrical signals into light and routes them through fiber. This is laid out in the joint SEC press release filed at the time of the deal announcement.

Today, integrating a Marvell networking chip with a Lumentum optical transceiver requires each company to design to published interface specifications, leaving performance tradeoffs on the table at every interface boundary. After the merger, Marvell would be able to co-design both the chip and the laser source as a unified system — optimizing power consumption, bandwidth allocation, signal encoding, and physical packaging in ways that separate vendors cannot coordinate efficiently. This architectural principle — that co-packaged optics require co-designed electrical and optical layers from the outset, not bolted together at the end — is what the companies described in their SEC joint customer letter as "extreme co-design" and "end-to-end connectivity from custom silicon to optical components."

The SEC press release from the companies stated the combined company's total addressable market at $550 billion, encompassing the full scope of AI infrastructure connectivity from current scale-across and scale-out architectures to future scale-up and scale-in designs. That figure is the companies' own projection, not an independently audited market size.

### Marvell's Optical Buildout Before This Deal

This acquisition is not Marvell's first move into optical connectivity. On December 4, 2025, the company announced its acquisition of Celestial AI, a startup whose "Photonic Fabric" technology connects components within AI infrastructure using light. The Celestial AI deal closed February 2, 2026, at an upfront price of $3.25 billion — with potential additional consideration of up to $2.25 billion tied to cumulative revenue milestones, for a maximum deal value of $5.5 billion. Celestial AI's Optical Multi-Chip Interconnect Bridge (OMIB) technology gave Marvell an early position in within-package optical interconnect — the architectural layer closest to the chip.

Lumentum dramatically extends that position: where Celestial AI addressed intra-package optical links, Lumentum's portfolio covers the lasers and transceivers for rack-to-rack, pod-to-pod, and eventually building-to-building AI cluster connectivity. Together, they represent the beginnings of an integrated optical stack from the package interior to the data center exterior. This strategic logic is Marvell's SEC investor presentation filed alongside the deal announcement.

Marvell had already established a supplier relationship with Lumentum before announcing the acquisition, and the two companies demonstrated joint optical circuit switching for AI scale-up infrastructure at OFC 2026 in Los Angeles in March of this year.

### Marvell's Position Against Broadcom

The competitive context for this deal is Marvell's intensifying rivalry with Broadcom, the semiconductor company that has dominated custom AI chip design for hyperscalers — most notably as Google's primary partner for its Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) through successive generations.

Marvell moved directly at that relationship in August 2026, announcing an expanded partnership with Google in which the search company received a warrant to acquire up to 58.97 million Marvell shares — valued at roughly $12.2 billion if fully exercised — at a price of $206.58 per share. The warrant's vesting structure is tied to Marvell's custom-product revenue from Google: one tranche of approximately 240,000 shares vests for every $500 million in qualifying revenue, out of 240 tranches. If all 240 tranches were to vest, that would imply $120 billion in cumulative custom-chip purchases by Google through Marvell's fiscal year 2033. That $120 billion figure is the warrant's vesting ceiling, not a guaranteed purchase commitment — but the structure aligns Google's equity upside with Marvell's revenue growth in a way that makes the two companies' interests substantially convergent. Nvidia, for its own strategic reasons, invested $2 billion in each company — $2 billion in Marvell and $2 billion in Lumentum — in March 2026 as part of multiyear agreements to develop advanced optical technology.

By folding Lumentum's optical manufacturing into its own operations, Marvell is pursuing the same vertical integration thesis that has made Broadcom difficult to displace: breadth of portfolio, depth of customer co-design, and the ability to offer hyperscalers a wider range of the components their data centers need from a single vendor.

### Deal Structure and Financial Commitments

Both Marvell's and Lumentum's boards approved the transaction unanimously on November 1, 2026. Lumentum stockholders will receive 4.5 shares of Marvell common stock for each share they hold, implying approximately $1,132 per Lumentum share based on each company's unaffected closing prices on October 28 — the last trading day before Bloomberg News reported that advanced talks were underway. That implied price represents a premium of approximately 23.9% over Lumentum's pre-announcement closing price.

Post-closing, current Marvell stockholders will own approximately 67% of the combined company; Lumentum stockholders will own approximately 33%. The transaction's enterprise value is $115 billion, which includes Lumentum's debt; the equity value of the consideration is approximately $102.54 billion. Marvell's chairman and CEO, Matt Murphy, will continue in that role at the combined company, and three Lumentum directors — including CEO Michael Hurlston — will join the Marvell board.

The companies project more than $1 billion in annual run-rate cost synergies following closure. Marvell said the transaction is expected to be accretive to its non-GAAP earnings per share in the first full year after closing. Lumentum announced its fiscal first quarter 2027 results on the same day as the deal, posting $1.26 billion in revenue against analyst consensus of $1.25 billion. Marvell separately reported preliminary fiscal third-quarter 2027 revenue in line with its prior guidance, narrowing the midpoint to $3.15 billion at a ±2% range from the earlier ±5% estimate.

The merger is structured in two steps, with a Marvell subsidiary first merging into Lumentum (which survives as a Marvell subsidiary) and then merging into a second subsidiary. The transaction requires shareholder approval from both companies and standard regulatory clearances across the United States, European Union, and key Asian markets. Completion is expected in the first half of calendar year 2028 — approximately 18 months from the announcement date.

### What Does Marvell Pay $115B to Own? (And What Does It Not Yet Buy?)

The merger gives Marvell control of Lumentum's compound semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure, the strategic bottleneck that the broader AI optics industry is scrambling to expand. Lumentum has announced plans to invest $350 million in Japan to increase its indium phosphide production capacity — a plant investment that signals how structurally important InP supply is expected to become and how difficult it is to scale quickly.

What the deal does not immediately solve is the supply constraint itself. Lumentum's capacity is reportedly sold out through 2029, meaning that owning Lumentum does not instantly give Marvell's hyperscaler customers more optical components than they could procure from Lumentum as a standalone company. The integration thesis is longer-term: once Marvell's chip designers and Lumentum's optical engineers work as a single team, the resulting co-designed systems should be more efficient, faster to qualify, and more tightly integrated into hyperscaler infrastructure than either company could achieve selling separately.

There is also the matter of Lumentum's pluggable module business — a transceiver product line that competes directly with other optical module suppliers. RBC Capital Markets analysts noted before the deal was announced that the combination's strategic case would be strongest if Marvell subsequently divested that pluggable module segment and focused on higher-value co-packaged and near-packaged optics.

### How Coherent Fits Into the Picture That Marvell and Lumentum Together Change

When a combined Marvell-Lumentum controls one of the two primary independent sources of indium phosphide lasers for AI data centers, the other primary independent source — Coherent Corp. — becomes more strategically important to every hyperscaler that wants a second supply option. Coherent stock rose approximately 2% on Monday as investors repriced the optical supply landscape. Applied Optoelectronics, a smaller optical component company, fell alongside both deal parties.

This dynamic — a newly integrated vertical supplier creating scarcity premium for the remaining independent optical vendors — is the deal's largest architectural consequence beyond its direct participants. A hyperscaler that sources all of its AI optical connectivity from a company primarily in the business of selling competing custom silicon and networking chips faces a structural vendor-concentration risk that did not exist when Marvell and Lumentum were separate.

### Antitrust and Regulatory Risk

At $115 billion enterprise value, the Marvell-Lumentum merger is the largest announced tech deal of 2026 by equity value, according to Bloomberg. The closest recent comparison in semiconductor history is Broadcom's $130 billion unsolicited bid for Qualcomm in 2017–2018, which the U.S. government blocked on national security grounds in March 2018 — a deal that was horizontal (chip designer acquiring chip designer) and involved foreign ownership concerns that do not apply here. The Marvell-Lumentum combination is primarily vertical (chip designer acquiring component manufacturer), which antitrust regulators typically treat with less concern than horizontal mergers between direct competitors.

That said, the deal's scale is unprecedented in the AI infrastructure supply chain, and regulatory review timelines in the United States, European Union, and China are difficult to predict. The companies' expected close date of H1 2028 implies they are building in at least 12 to 18 months of regulatory process. If regulators in any major jurisdiction require remedies — such as divestitures of specific product lines — the strategic logic of the deal could be meaningfully altered.

### Where Will the Combined Company Fit in Five Years?

Marvell has projected revenue of $70–90 billion by 2031, with $20 billion targeted for fiscal 2028 and approximately $37.5 billion of the 2031 figure projected from interconnect alone. Those are aspirational targets in a market that has repeatedly forced revisions to semiconductor revenue forecasts as hyperscaler spending priorities shift.

What is not aspirational is the physical fact driving the deal: copper's bandwidth ceiling is real, AI clusters' bandwidth demand is growing, and indium phosphide is the material at the other end of the transition. The question the market raised on Monday — with Marvell shares closing down approximately 13% and Lumentum shares closing approximately 19% below their pre-announcement levels — is not whether that transition will happen, but whether a two-year regulatory journey, a dilutive all-stock deal, and the integration of a manufacturing-intensive compound semiconductor company into a fabless chip designer will proceed without significant cost overruns, delays, or competitive setbacks.

Matt Murphy said on CNBC's Squawk on the Street that creating a company that controls both the semiconductor and the photonics "comes along once in a generation." Whether the combined company captures that opportunity — or whether the window for integration has been priced too optimistically into the deal terms — will depend on whether copper's limitations prove as absolute as the physics suggests, and whether Marvell can execute an acquisition whose manufacturing demands are fundamentally unlike anything the company has attempted before.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is Marvell paying $115 billion to acquire Lumentum?

Copper interconnects — the wiring that connects chips, racks, and pods inside AI data centers — are hitting their physical bandwidth and power limits as AI training clusters scale to tens of thousands of accelerators. Optical interconnects, which transmit data as light through fiber rather than electricity through metal, can carry far more data at far lower power consumption. Lumentum makes the indium phosphide lasers and optical components at the heart of that transition, and standard silicon fabs cannot produce InP. Marvell is buying the manufacturing capability and the material moat that it believes will be required for the next generation of AI infrastructure.

### What does co-packaged optics mean, and why does it matter?

Co-packaged optics (CPO) is an architecture in which the optical engine — the laser, modulator, and photodetector — is integrated directly into the same package as the AI accelerator chip, eliminating the copper trace that currently runs between the chip and the optical transceiver. That trace, though short, dissipates signal and consumes power at the bandwidths modern AI workloads demand. CPO removes it entirely, placing light emission and detection millimeters from the silicon. Industry research suggests CPO can reduce power per optical link by up to 90% compared with pluggable module architectures. Achieving CPO at commercial scale requires that the chip designer (Marvell) and the laser manufacturer (Lumentum) co-design both elements as a unified system — which is the core rationale for owning both rather than sourcing one from a separate company.

### Why did both Marvell and Lumentum stocks fall after the deal was announced?

Marvell fell approximately 13% because the deal is structured entirely in stock — meaning existing Marvell shareholders will be diluted, and the company carries no cash backstop if the deal falls through or Marvell's own stock declines over the 18-month regulatory review period. Lumentum fell approximately 19% because even after the deal was announced at a premium, Lumentum shares traded roughly 40% below the implied deal value of $1,132 per share. That gap is what deal arbitrage traders call a "risk spread" — it reflects market estimates of the probability that the deal closes, weighted against the two-year timeline, regulatory uncertainty, and the fact that Marvell's stock (which is the deal currency) may itself decline before closing. A large risk spread on an announced deal typically signals that investors doubt either the completion probability or the timeline.

### Does this deal mean the AI optical interconnect supply chain will become more concentrated?

Yes — at least in the near term. Before this deal, Lumentum and Coherent Corp. were the two primary independent sources of indium phosphide-based optical components for AI data center networks. If the Marvell-Lumentum merger closes, Lumentum will no longer be an independent supplier; its products will be part of a company that primarily sells competing custom silicon and networking chips. Hyperscalers that want an independent, non-captive optical supply chain will need to rely more heavily on Coherent — which is likely why Coherent's stock rose approximately 2% on the day of the announcement. The concentration risk for hyperscalers in their optical supply chain will be a material consideration in how cloud companies structure their procurement agreements over the next two years.
