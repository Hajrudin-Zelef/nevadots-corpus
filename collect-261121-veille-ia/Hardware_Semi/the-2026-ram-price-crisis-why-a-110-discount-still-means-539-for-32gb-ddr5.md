# The 2026 RAM Price Crisis: Why a $110 'Discount' Still Means $539 for 32GB DDR5

32GB DDR5 kits cost 4–5x their 2025 price. Inside the numbers, the suppliers' warnings for 2027, and the only buying strategies that still math out.

By Bongyeon Kim · Researched from primary sources, verified by hand, and produced with AI assistance on a production stack this site actually operates (Vercel · Neon · Cloudflare R2).

A memory vendor discounted its most popular 32GB DDR5 kit by $110 this week.
The new price: **$539** — for a kit that sold in some configurations for
**one-fifth of that** about a year ago. The reactions on the Korean hardware
forum where the news landed were four short variations of the same sentence:
"still expensive."

This is the RAM price crisis, quantified: what actually happened to the numbers, what the suppliers are warning about for 2027, where the first cracks are showing, and the buying strategies that still make sense when the baseline itself has moved 5×.

## TL;DR

- The US cheapest-price index for 32GB DDR5-6000 went **~$100–$200 (Oct
2025) → $454 (Jan 2026) → $400 (Aug) → $449.97 (Oct 14)** . In Europe, a
4-kit average went from**~€113 to ~€544 — about 4.9×** in 13 months.
- G.Skill's "Black Friday preview" discounts delivered cuts of 2–17% on kits
whose reference prices already sit at 4–5× the 2025 baseline:
**$649 → $539 (32GB)** and**$699 → $679** . Its DDR4 kits landed
at $209–$224.
- TeamGroup's CEO warns the **worst shortage runs Q4 2026 into mid-2027** [unverified — vendor projection, not an observed event] and that soon
customers "will pay more and get nothing."
- First cracks are real, though tiny: Corsair 32GB Vengeance fell to
**$369.99–$379.99** from ~$490; a G.Skill 32GB kit sold for**$156 inside
a CPU+motherboard bundle** .
- No upgrade stops working. The buyer's job in a 5× market is different:
*don't buy* , size honestly, and treat bundles as the real discount channel.

translate that through any shopping region and the story is identical, with
local accents: the European 4-kit sample (Corsair, G.Skill, Kingston,
Patriot) averaged **~€113 in September 2025 and ~€544 by late September
2026**, with G.Skill's prices up **+138%** in the first two months of the
crisis alone — the steepest of the four brands, because its CL30-class kits
are the enthusiasts' default. The boring upgrade line item became a
negotiation budget.

Why the climb? The cause chain is public and boring in the way that real
costs are: AI accelerator demand pulled the big three's (Samsung, SK Hynix,
Micron) wafer allocation toward HBM and enterprise DRAM, and consumer DIMM
supply became the residual — Samsung's quarterly DRAM revenue share reached
38% on that same demand wave, per the Nov 5 regional relay. Suppliers'
pricing reflects their *procurement* cost, not your upgrade budget, as
G.Skill's own explanation puts it: procurement and sourcing costs "have
substantially increased," subject to change without notice.

## What the suppliers are saying now

TeamGroup — a Taiwan-based maker of consumer and industrial memory — put
quarterly numbers next to a warning worth your attention. Its CEO (Cheon
Ching-yuan, per the coverage) expects prices to keep rising "for several
months," and frames the peak as the **worst semiconductor shortage, running
from Q4 2026 into the middle of the following year** — with his bluntest
warning being that soon customers will pay the premium and still find
nothing to buy. Two more lines from that statement travel well beyond the
forum:

1. **Supply is being negotiated at spot premiums.** Even with contracts
signed, allocation came up short: the company said it's buying part of
its own supply back at spot-market prices.
2. **The customer mix has flipped.** TeamGroup's B2B revenue share jumped
from ~24% (first three quarters of 2025) to**64%** in the same window of
2026, while B2C share collapsed from 75% to 35%. Why would a memory
vendor pass on consumers? Because*lots* of AI-adjacent business want
every module it can source, at retail-plus pricing, in volume.

And the financials confirm the incentive: Q3 revenue of NT$10.139B (about
US $311.5M), +33% quarter over quarter, with operating profit up **46.8%**
and net profit around +50% — reported at exactly the moment its consumer
business can't get stock. The price curve isn't malice; it's margin.

## Where the cracks are showing

Three signals that the plateau has edges, all from the last weeks:

- **Corsair's standalone prices slipped before the official discount
season:** 32GB Vengeance DDR5-6000/6400 fell to**$369.99–$379.99** ,
down from ~$490 recent highs — reportedly the first meaningful step-down
in months.
- **Bundles are the deep discount channel:** a G.Skill 32GB DDR5 kit landed
at**$156** when purchased with a Ryzen chip and motherboard. The standalone
price did not drop $293; the*allocation logic* of a bundle lets the
platform vendor get paid once for the whole ticket.
- **DDR4 isn't the escape hatch.** With DDR4 module production winding down
over 2026, its prices are up 86%+ vs 2024 — G.Skill's own DDR4 kit
discounts land at $209–$224 for 32GB, versus roughly a quarter of that in
2024 [unverified as a UK/global retail average — varies by market].

None of that promises the crisis is ending. Suppliers — including the big
three and the Taiwanese ecosystem companies — have collectively said 2027 as
the earliest stabilization. Tom's Hardware's European price tracking is
already reporting *month-over-month* stabilization, roughly ±0.1% in a recent
month [at publication — paragraph from the site's tracker; check its current
month before treating it as a trend statement].

## The buying math that survives a 5× market

Everything below assumes you are a builder in a market where the "usual" price of 32GB has moved from ~$100 to ~$400+. The correct decisions change:

1. **Size honestly, not aspirationally.** 16GB is functionally minimum; 32GB
is the sweet spot. Over-provisioning at 64GB/96GB means paying the same
scarcity multiplier on a much bigger ticket — and forum-level trackers
put 64GB kits in the €2,500 range in late 2026 [unverified — a single
comment-section figure, check local listings]. A decade of "just buy the
bigger kit" advice does not survive a 5×-priced baseline.
2. **Top-bin speed grades are the wrong luxury.** DDR5-6600-class kits
tracked at**$655+** in the January index — a premium above the 6000's
~$454 at the time for single-digit real-world gains in games. In a
scarcity market,*latency-tiered pricing* expands to capture the budget
you'd otherwise spend on capacity.
3. **If you need CPU + motherboard anyway, bundles are the arithmetic choice.** The $156 G.Skill kit inside a chip/board bundle isn't a memory deal —
it's a*platform* deal with memory attached. The alternative — waiting
for standalone "$400" kits to drift back — requires assuming the peak
was passed; suppliers aren't promising that.
4. **Don't buy inventory "in case."** When suppliers tell you the supply
squeeze peaks*ahead of you* , a third kit you only sort-of need is a
leveraged position against your own usage forecast. The first Corsair
slip is not (yet) a landslide — and AI demand, the cause of this all,
is not forecast to decline. If anything, the floor under RAM prices for
the next several quarters is the suppliers' word, which they also
profit from telling you.
5. **Check the used market before overpaying for new.** In a 5× market,
second-hand listings reprice the fastest — and a matched 32GB kit from
a reliable seller routinely undercuts new retail by a wide margin. The
risk profile changes too: used DIMMs with zero warranty count against a
purchase that already carries import and return friction.

## What the community said

Two forum threads ran the same day (November 5). On the G.Skill discount
post, four comments in the visible review window were all the same
sentiment shape: *"still expensive even with the discount."* No one debated
whether the kits were worth it — the *principle of the discount* was
evaluated against a remembered baseline, not the listing.

The TeamGroup warning article drew 13 reactions within hours, and the distribution was different — analysis instead of annoyance:

- The dominant thread: **capacity is the resolution, not discounts** ("prices will stabilize only when expanded fabs output consumer
allocations"), paired with skepticism that the capacity growth will reach
consumers while AI absorbs it ("even if fabs expand, consumer availability
is a separate question").
- A resignation pattern: **"prices will stabilize, but never return to
their old levels"** — the same acceptance you'd expect in any market,
and notably the same conclusion of the vendor's own quarterly framing.
- One commenter jokingly translated the CEO's tone rather than the words: it was not a reassurance about stabilization, it was a "premium ahead" notice — and the safest reading is to take it exactly that way.

As paraphrased stats, neither thread activity indicates doom or optimism —
they indicate that readers track the *baseline* question, not the discount.
That's a sensible evolution: discounts in a 5× market are relative events.

## Wrap-up

- Numbers: **~$100–$200 → ~$450–$539** for the standard 32GB DDR5 kit in one
year, with a 4.9× European average as the international cross-check.
- Suppliers' own timelines put the worst stretch ending **around mid-2027
[unverified — vendor and index-of-truth projections]** , with AI demand
continuing to hoover up capacity cuts to consumer supply.
- Your job if you're buying: 32GB (honestly sized), mid-tier speeds,
bundles over standalone, and *strict* refusal to buy speculative inventory.
If your machine already has 32GB — congratulations, your upgrade budget
belongs to literally anything else this quarter.
