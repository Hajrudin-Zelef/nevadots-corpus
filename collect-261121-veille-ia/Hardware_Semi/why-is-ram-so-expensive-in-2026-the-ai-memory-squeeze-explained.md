## Why Is RAM So Expensive in 2026? The AI Memory Squeeze Explained

  
 
 
  
   Nov
   10
   2026
  
 

 
 

If you've priced a new PC lately, you've felt it: RAM that cost under a hundred dollars two years ago now costs several hundred, and the reason has almost nothing to do with your computer. Here's why RAM is so expensive in 2026 — and what to do about it.

# Why Is RAM So Expensive in 2026? The AI Memory Squeeze Explained

If you have priced a new computer lately, you already know something strange is going on. A 32GB kit of DDR5 memory that sold for roughly $72 to $86 back in 2024 was going for around $375 by June of this year, and by early November some of the cheapest kits on the market had climbed past $550. That is not a typo, and it is not a sale that ended. Memory got expensive, stayed expensive, and every serious forecaster expects it to stay that way for a while. So why is RAM so expensive in 2026? The short answer is that the memory inside your computer and the memory inside an AI data center are cut from the same silicon wafers, and the data centers are buying first.

## The sticker shock, in real numbers

It helps to see the climb laid out plainly, because the scale of it still surprises people who have not shopped for parts in a couple of years. In October 2024, a decent 32GB DDR5 kit could be had for about $86. By June 2026, Tom's Hardware reported that the cheapest 32GB DDR5 kit available cost $375. In late October, price trackers put that same class of kit around $459. Then, in the first three days of November, one manufacturer's own store raised a 32GB kit from $459 to $569.97, a jump of nearly 25 percent in a single long weekend. A 64GB kit from the same maker climbed from $989 to $1,099.97 over the same stretch.

Those are retail prices, the ones you and I actually pay. Behind them, the wholesale market tells the same story in even starker terms. Market research firm TrendForce, which tracks the contract prices big buyers pay for memory chips, recorded conventional DRAM contract prices rising 93 to 98 percent in the first quarter of 2026 alone. The second quarter added another 58 to 63 percent. The pace has slowed since then, to an estimated 13 to 18 percent in the third quarter and a forecast of 10 to 15 percent in the fourth, but notice what slowing means here: prices are still going up, just less violently. Add the quarters together and conventional DRAM contract prices end 2026 at roughly four times where they started the year. J.P. Morgan has estimated that DRAM prices will have risen more than 400 percent from the start of 2024 to the end of 2026.

And it is not just RAM. The same forces are pushing up SSDs, because the NAND flash chips inside them come from the same handful of factories. TrendForce expects NAND flash contract prices to rise 15 to 20 percent in the final quarter of 2026, and retail SSD prices have already jumped roughly 300 percent in a year by some measures. Even USB flash drives and memory cards got caught up in it: Tom's Hardware found in April that the median price of USB drives and memory cards on Amazon was about 124 percent higher than in 2025, with one popular 256GB microSD card up 261 percent.

## Three companies make almost all of it

To understand why this happened, start with who makes the stuff. Just three companies, Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron, sell close to nine out of every ten dollars of DRAM in the world. That is an extraordinarily concentrated market, and it means there is no scrappy fourth competitor waiting in the wings to undercut everyone. When those three decide where their factory capacity goes, that is where the world's memory goes.

For decades, that was fine for consumers. Memory prices moved in cycles: factories overbuilt, prices crashed, demand caught up, prices recovered. The cycle still exists, but the AI boom bent it out of shape, because AI servers need far more memory per machine than a PC or a phone ever will, and they need a special, expensive kind of it.

## AI memory eats three times the silicon

Here is the part that matters most, and it is almost mechanical. Every AI accelerator, the chips from Nvidia and AMD that train and run AI models, ships with stacks of high-bandwidth memory, or HBM, sitting right next to it. HBM is made from DRAM wafers, the same wafers your desktop memory comes from. But HBM uses about three times as much silicon to store the same amount of data, according to Micron. So every wafer a factory devotes to HBM produces far fewer gigabytes than a wafer devoted to ordinary PC memory, and the AI companies are happy to pay top dollar for it.

The numbers show how fast the balance has tilted. TrendForce estimates that HBM takes about 22 percent of the world's DRAM wafer capacity by the end of 2026 while producing only about 9 percent of the bits. A Samsung executive expects HBM to take close to 30 percent of DRAM wafer capacity in 2027. Every one of those wafers is a wafer that is not making the DDR5 that goes into gaming PCs, laptops, and workstations. On top of that, cloud providers are buying up huge quantities of ordinary server DDR5 for their AI infrastructure too, and SK hynix says more than half of its total production capacity for 2027 and 2028 is already locked up under long-term deals.

Put it together and the picture is simple: the factories are not broken, and nobody is hoarding chips in a warehouse somewhere. The factories are running flat out. It is just that the richest customer in line, AI infrastructure, is buying memory faster than the factories can make it, and everyone else gets what is left.

## The squeeze is showing up everywhere

RAM is only the most visible symptom. Because memory is soldered into most modern laptops, you cannot just add more later, so the shortage lands directly on the price tag of the finished machine. Dell raised some laptop prices by up to 30 percent earlier in the year, according to the Wall Street Journal. Gartner has forecast PC prices up 17 percent and smartphone prices up 13 percent in 2026 compared with 2025. IDC reported that PC shipments fell 20.1 percent and smartphone shipments fell 22 percent in the third quarter of 2026, and SK hynix's finance chief expects fewer PCs and phones to be sold as a direct result of the shortage. Some phone makers have started shipping devices with less storage or simpler cameras, and some have even resorted to recycled memory chips, according to Counterpoint Research.

One moment that captured the mood came in early December 2025, when Micron announced it would exit the Crucial consumer business, one of the best-known budget RAM and SSD brands, so it could redirect that capacity to AI-driven data-center demand. When a company walks away from a consumer brand shoppers actually love, that tells you how lopsided the economics have become. And in an interview reported on October 30, OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman said running AI models on home computers is a poor use of the world's limited DRAM, because a home PC's memory sits idle most of the time while a data center keeps it busy around the clock. You can disagree with him, but his comment shows how the people at the center of the AI boom think about your RAM: as capacity that could be working harder somewhere else.

## When will RAM prices come down?

This is the question everyone actually wants answered, and the honest answer is that nobody who would know is forecasting a fall anytime soon. TrendForce's fourth-quarter forecast is still an increase, 10 to 15 percent for conventional DRAM and 15 to 20 percent for NAND flash, just a slower one. SK hynix said on October 28 that it expects demand to exceed supply through 2030. Samsung says the market stays tight through 2028. Micron has said supply will be much tighter in calendar 2027 and 2028.

The reason the timeline is so long is that building a memory factory takes years, not months. Most of the new fabs that could genuinely loosen supply do not open until 2028, with Micron's first new fab expected around mid-2027. Until then, the industry is essentially running on the factories it already has, and those factories are already spoken for. The one piece of good news is that the rate of increase is slowing, which suggests the market is finding a painful new equilibrium rather than spiraling without end. But slowing is not falling, and no major forecaster is calling for prices to drop before the second half of 2027 at the earliest.

There is a historical caveat worth remembering. Memory is famously cyclical, and the last two shortages ended suddenly when new capacity arrived and demand cooled at the same time. SK hynix's operating margin was negative 67 percent in the first quarter of 2023, which is what the bottom of the last cycle looked like. Nobody can promise this cycle ends the same way, but the history is a reminder that today's prices are not a law of nature. They are a market, and markets turn.

## Should you buy RAM now or wait?

So what do you actually do about it? It depends on what you need and when you need it.

If you are building or upgrading a PC and you need the memory within the next year or so, the evidence points toward buying now rather than waiting for a drop that no forecaster expects. Prices are still rising, just more slowly, and waiting a few months is more likely to cost you money than save it. Buy the capacity you need, and if you are choosing a laptop, choose the memory size carefully at purchase, because in most modern laptops it is soldered in and cannot be upgraded later.

If your current machine is doing fine, there is no reason to panic-buy. Your old RAM is not going to wear out, and an upgrade you do not need is the worst kind of purchase in an expensive market. The one exception is if you were already planning a build for the holidays: Black Friday deals on memory this year are likely to be modest, because retailers have no margin to give away, so do not count on a big sale to rescue the budget.

There are a couple of cheaper paths worth knowing about. DDR4 memory has plateaued while DDR5 keeps climbing, so if your motherboard takes DDR4, that older standard is relatively better value right now. Used memory is another option, though prices there have risen too: one reseller's 32GB DDR4 server module costs 60 percent more than it did in January. And if you only need heavy computing power for a few hours a week, renting a GPU in the cloud is the one price in this whole story that has actually fallen.

For most people, though, the practical move is simple: figure out how much memory you genuinely need, buy it once, and stop watching the price trackers. They will only make you feel worse.

## The bigger picture

There is something a little strange about this whole episode. For thirty years, the story of personal computing was that the hardware got cheaper and better every year. The RAM shortage of 2026 broke that pattern in the most ordinary way possible: the same factories, the same wafers, a new customer with deeper pockets. Your next computer is more expensive because the world's most valuable companies are all trying to buy the same silicon you are, and they can afford to outbid you.

That will not last forever. New factories are coming, the cycle will turn eventually, and memory prices will fall again someday, the way they always have. But if you need a computer in the meantime, it helps to know what you are paying for. It is not a tariff, it is not a scalper, and it is not a conspiracy. It is a wafer, and there are only so many of them to go around.
