Kimi K3.1 Pro is Moonshot AI’s October 2026 flagship open-weight model and the first member of the K3.1 family. It is a 2.8-trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts model with 104 billion parameters activated per token, 93 decoder layers, 896 routed experts with 16 selected per token, two shared experts, a 160K-token vocabulary, native image input through a 401M-parameter MoonViT-V2 encoder, and a nominal 1,048,576-token context window. Its most consequential architectural change from Kimi K3 is the replacement of the 24 dense Gated-MLA attention layers with Gated DeepSeek Sparse Attention, while retaining 69 Kimi Delta Attention layers. The DSA indexer uses 32 heads of dimension 128 and retrieves the top 2,048 historical keys for each query. The released checkpoint uses quantization-aware-trained MXFP4 routed-expert weights and MXFP8 activations, with higher-precision weights elsewhere.

The model is unusually transparent architecturally while remaining substantially opaque about its training budget. Moonshot has disclosed the broad K3 corpus taxonomy, training schedule, optimizer family, long-context curriculum, post-training architecture and K3.1 Pro’s large-scale joint reinforcement-learning strategy, yet it has not published K3.1 Pro’s total training-token count, exact source-corpus composition, complete dataset list, absolute pre-training FLOPs, accelerator count, wall-clock training time, or numerical post-training compute budget. K3’s technical report likewise omits an absolute token count and FLOP figure, so any numerical estimate of K3.1 Pro training compute would be speculative.

The independent evidence is more mixed than Moonshot’s launch charts suggest. Artificial Analysis currently scores K3.1 Pro at 46 on its Intelligence Index, versus 55 for GPT-6.1 Sol at maximum effort, while K3.1 Pro performs exceptionally well on its agentic subtests: 73.3% on AutomationBench-AA and roughly 67% on Terminal-Bench 4.0. Artificial Analysis measures K3.1 Pro at about 33 output tokens/s and roughly 36.7 minutes per Intelligence Index task, making it substantially slower per completed evaluation task than GPT-6.1 Sol. LiveBench 2.0 similarly places K3.1 Pro below the current proprietary leaders overall: K3.1 Pro scores 71.3, GPT-6.1 Sol 84.1, Claude Opus 5.5 85.1, GPT-6 Astra 85.6, and Claude Fable 5.1 85.7 in the retrieved leaderboard snapshots. GLM-5.3 scores 60.7 in one LiveBench 2.0 snapshot, although its browser-use score is zero in that configuration, materially depressing the aggregate and illustrating why leaderboard totals cannot be treated as intrinsic model IQ.

K3.1 Pro’s strongest case is therefore narrower and more interesting than a claim of general frontier supremacy. It is one of the strongest open-weight systems currently available for long-horizon coding, document-heavy agent work, finance, tool use and very long contexts, and its open checkpoint gives researchers access to a class of architecture and capability that remains proprietary at OpenAI and Anthropic. Its weaknesses are equally material: raw closed-book reasoning remains behind the strongest proprietary models on several independent suites; the model is expensive and exceptionally demanding to self-host; first-party API latency can be high; its benchmark profile depends heavily on agent harnesses, reasoning effort and tool availability; and several safety controls applied to Moonshot’s hosted product cannot be assumed to accompany a self-hosted checkpoint.

The standard legacy metrics requested in this report require a qualification. I found no credible, contemporaneous, apples-to-apples independent evaluation that runs **all six requested models** under the same harness on MMLU, GSM8K and HumanEval. K3.1 Pro itself has a reproducible GSM8K result of 98.18% in AMD’s vLLM validation on MI355X, but MMLU and HumanEval are absent from Moonshot’s principal K3.1 Pro evaluation table, and comparable current results for GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 are not consistently published under a common protocol. Filling those cells with scores scraped from unrelated harnesses would create false precision. Modern suites such as LiveBench 2.0, HLE, GPQA-Diamond, Terminal-Bench, FrontierCode, AA-Omniscience and GDPval are considerably more discriminative for this model generation.

## Architecture, training and post-training

K3.1 Pro inherits the fundamental scale and MoE topology of Kimi K3. The decoder contains 93 layers and activates approximately 104B of its 2.8T total parameters for each token. Its routed feed-forward component contains 896 experts, of which 16 are selected per token, accompanied by two shared experts. Attention hidden width is 7,168, the model uses 96 attention heads, and the MoE hidden dimension per expert is 3,072. The vision path is MoonViT-V2 with about 401M parameters, making K3.1 Pro natively multimodal rather than a purely text model wrapped with an external visual adapter at serving time.

The hybrid attention arrangement is central to the design. Sixty-nine layers use Kimi Delta Attention, a recurrent/linear-attention mechanism intended to avoid the full KV-cache growth of conventional attention. Twenty-four layers use Gated DSA, where an auxiliary indexer scans historical index keys and keeps at most 2,048 candidates for exact MLA attention. vLLM describes the per-token index key as 132 bytes in FP8, compared with 576 bytes for the FP8 MLA latent that dense MLA would otherwise read for every historical token; sparse selection therefore attacks the memory-bandwidth bottleneck that dominates very-long-context decoding.

This structure should be understood as a bandwidth-management architecture as much as a parameter-scaling architecture. At 2.8T total weights, dense activation of the full model would be computationally impractical. Sparse MoE routing reduces active parameter count to 104B, while KDA and DSA reduce long-context memory movement. The result remains extremely large in storage terms because inference hardware must generally retain the complete expert bank even though only a fraction is used for an individual token.

The released checkpoint applies MXFP4 to routed-expert weights while keeping other components in BF16; Moonshot describes the broader training recipe as quantization-aware training with MXFP4 weights and MXFP8 activations. This is important because the low precision is incorporated into training rather than imposed solely as a post-hoc consumer quantization. The practical checkpoint is roughly 1.4–1.6 TiB depending on how metadata and higher-precision tensors are counted; an independent deployment recipe measures approximately 1.42 TiB for the mixed MXFP4/BF16 checkpoint.

K3.1 Pro is explicitly described as a post-trained Kimi K3 rather than a fresh pre-training run. Moonshot says it spent substantially more post-training compute and performed joint reinforcement learning across hundreds of downstream domains, supported by changes to its RL framework, sandbox infrastructure, rollout scheduling, long-context inference stack and environment-quality pipeline. The company has not disclosed the absolute RL FLOP budget, number of trajectories, number of environment episodes, optimizer step count, or wall-clock duration.

The base K3 training recipe gives the best available picture of what lies underneath K3.1 Pro. K3 was jointly multimodally pre-trained from the beginning on a corpus divided into web text, code, mathematics, knowledge and a large vision collection. The visual corpus included captions, interleaved image-text material, OCR, perception, video and programmatic visual data connecting code with rendered SVG, webpages, games, CAD schematics and 3D assets. Filtering combined heuristics, learned quality classifiers and deduplication, while knowledge and mathematics data were partly rephrased using diverse prompting and fidelity checks. Moonshot does not identify a complete list of source datasets or publish a total token count.

K3’s optimization used Per-Head Muon, weight clipping, Quantile Balancing for MoE routing, cosine learning-rate decay, a 1% linear warm-up and weight decay of 0.1. Context length began at 8K and moved to 64K during pre-training; a subsequent cooldown extended the window through 256K to 1M. Long-context synthetic examples were deliberately constructed so that information required for solving tasks was distributed across the full sequence rather than concentrated locally.

The K3 post-training framework also explains K3.1 Pro’s selectable reasoning efforts. Moonshot trains different reasoning budgets using a per-problem token threshold during reinforcement learning; trajectories that exceed a scaled budget can receive a negative reward. A high-budget model is trained first, followed by progressively smaller budget multipliers to create high- and low-effort experts, after which trajectories from multiple effort levels are collected for supervised fine-tuning and multi-teacher on-policy distillation. The underlying agent environments include software engineering, kernel optimization, multimodal tool use, browsing, professional knowledge work and persistent workflows extending over hundreds or thousands of tool interactions.

The distinction between what is known and unknown is significant. Moonshot has disclosed unusually detailed model internals, yet the following quantitative properties remain undisclosed as of November 8, 2026: total K3.1 Pro training tokens, total K3 base tokens, exact language distribution, named web/code corpora, knowledge cutoff for the open checkpoint, absolute training FLOPs, total accelerator-hours, hardware mix used for training, post-training token count, reward-model architecture, human-preference dataset size, and whether conventional pairwise RLHF forms a material fraction of the final recipe. The public description emphasizes verifiable-reward and environment-based RL rather than a classical preference-model-centric RLHF pipeline.

## Independent benchmarks and comparative performance

The most useful independent comparison is currently obtained by combining Artificial Analysis and LiveBench because both evaluate multiple current frontier models with their own harnesses. Artificial Analysis’s current K3.1 Pro page reports an Intelligence Index of 46, approximately 33 output tokens/s, a 192.77-second time to first **answer** token in its reasoning workload, and approximately $3.68 per Intelligence Index task. The same evaluator reports GPT-6.1 Sol at 55 on the Intelligence Index, around 55 output tokens/s and about $1.27 per task under its evaluation methodology.

LiveBench 2.0 produces a similar ordering at the top end while showing that K3.1 Pro remains very capable. In the retrieved current leaderboard, Claude Fable 5.1 scores 85.7 overall, GPT-6 Astra 85.6, Claude Opus 5.5 85.1, GPT-6.1 Sol 84.1 and Kimi K3.1 Pro 71.3. K3.1 Pro’s category profile in that snapshot is 39.1 agentic, 77.8 design, 81.8 coding, 75.9 browser use, 79.1 data analysis, 77.9 research and 91.1 reasoning. GLM-5.3 scores 60.7 in a retrieved LiveBench 2.0 configuration with 35.9 agentic, 85.8 reasoning, 82.8 coding, 70.2 data analysis, 73.2 research and 77.3 design, but its browser-use result is zero in that run, which substantially distorts the aggregate comparison against models evaluated with working browser support.

The clearest independent pattern is that K3.1 Pro is disproportionately strong on agentic work relative to its general reasoning score. Artificial Analysis gives it 73.3% on AutomationBench-AA and roughly 67% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, both ahead of GPT-6.1 Sol in the direct comparison, while Sol wins strongly on FrontierCode 2.0, HLE-Diamond, CritPt and Terminal-Bench-Science. That profile is consistent with Moonshot’s training emphasis on environment interaction, tool use, sandbox execution and long-horizon reinforcement learning.

Moonshot’s own comparison paints K3.1 Pro in an especially favorable agentic light. It reports 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 against 66.4% for Opus 5.5 and 58.2% for GPT-6 Astra, 51.7% on SWE-Marathon against 46.3% for Opus and 42.5% for Astra, 70.0% on HLE with tools, 92.1% on BrowseComp, 64.4 on OfficeQA Pro v2 and 79.6% on OSWorld V2.1. Those figures should be treated as vendor-reported compilation results rather than a single controlled bake-off because Moonshot explicitly notes different harnesses for different models: Kimi often uses Kimi Code or Claude Code, GPT-6 Astra may use Codex, Claude values are sometimes imported from Anthropic, and some proprietary runs can invoke fallback behavior.

The same vendor table also reveals where K3.1 Pro remains weaker. It scores 31.5 on ProgramBench versus 65.0 for Opus 5.5 and 50.0 for Astra; 38.1 on Terminal-Bench-Science versus 63.3 and 68.1 respectively; and 39.4 on HLE-Diamond versus 60.9 for Opus and 65.8 for Astra. Those gaps correspond closely to the independent Artificial Analysis result in which K3.1 Pro’s HLE-Diamond score is 37.3% and GPT-6.1 Sol’s is 58.8%.

Classic MMLU, GSM8K and HumanEval are now particularly poor instruments for discriminating these systems. GSM8K is nearly saturated by K3.1 Pro at roughly 98%, while modern coding agents can differ radically on long-horizon repository work despite all scoring near the ceiling on short standalone programming problems. The absence of clean six-way legacy scores is therefore an evidential limitation, although it does not materially prevent ranking the models on the harder contemporary workloads that actually separate them.

## Quantization, deployment, latency and memory

K3.1 Pro’s “open weights” status should not be confused with ordinary local deployability. The released weights are so large that a conventional workstation is excluded by simple capacity constraints. The validated vLLM baseline is TP8 across eight B300-class accelerators or equivalent topologies; AMD has validated the released MXFP4 checkpoint on eight MI355X GPUs, while NVIDIA NIM lists an eight-B300 TP8 configuration and 16- or 32-H20 configurations using pipeline parallelism.

AMD reports that an eight-MI355X node, with 288 GB on each accelerator, can load K3.1 Pro with its DSpark speculative-decoding model and peaks at roughly 199 GB of device memory per GPU during startup; a single one-million-token request fits within the node. This corresponds to roughly 1.6 TB of model-plus-runtime device use across the node before exploiting the remaining memory for KV/state caches and batching. The practical checkpoint itself is approximately 1.42 TiB in one independently documented deployment.

The context cache is considerably more sophisticated than a conventional transformer’s KV cache because KDA maintains recurrent state while each DSA layer keeps both an MLA latent and an index key. vLLM measures 16,992 bytes per cached token per GPU for FP8 latent plus FP8 index keys under its replicated TP8 setup; using NVFP4 latents and MXFP4 index keys reduces that to 10,080 bytes. On eight GB300 GPUs, vLLM reports that TP8 with FP8 structures holds about 2.9 full 1M-token contexts, while the lower-precision cache formats raise that to roughly five; decode context parallelism can shard the token cache and increase capacity much further.

Long-prompt prefill is one of the architecture’s strongest systems results. On eight B300s, vLLM reports approximately 9.4 seconds to prefill a 340K-token prompt with token-sharded sparse attention, versus about 15.9 seconds with the head-sharded path. For a 2K-token append against a cached 150K-token prefix, its optimized sparse-indexer and MLA path takes roughly 15 ms per GPU instead of 56 ms in the comparison path. These are kernel-level measurements rather than complete API response times and should not be conflated with user-visible reasoning latency.

Speculative decoding matters considerably. vLLM’s preview DSpark draft model reaches a mean acceptance length of 4.597 with seven draft tokens on GSM8K at temperature 1.0/top-p 0.95 and yields roughly a 3× decode speedup at batch size one on eight GB300 GPUs while preserving the benchmark’s measured accuracy.

SGLang reports a comparable high-performance deployment path and scales from roughly 449 user-visible tokens/s at one session to about 69.8 tokens/s per user at 300 concurrent sessions while aggregate throughput reaches 33,688 tokens/s/GPU in its largest AgentX configuration. Those numbers are infrastructure benchmarks on optimized high-end hardware and are therefore best read as upper-end serving-system results rather than expectations for commodity hosts.

NVIDIA Dynamo’s more conservative production-style measurements illustrate the range. On an eight-GPU GB300 vLLM worker, it reports about 39.9 system output tokens/s/GPU and roughly 76.7 output tokens/s per user at concurrency 32, with median TTFT around 428 ms in its AgentX workload. A disaggregated 24-GPU configuration raises per-user output speed to about 87 tokens/s but changes system throughput and latency trade-offs.

Hosted API behavior is different again because K3.1 Pro can spend a long time generating hidden reasoning before emitting an answer. Artificial Analysis measures roughly 33 output tokens/s and around 192.77 seconds to the first **answer** token on its Intelligence Index workload, with 36.7 minutes per evaluation task. GPT-6.1 Sol reaches around 55 tokens/s and 20.4 minutes per task in the same evaluator, despite having a similarly large first-answer delay in that specific reasoning workload. The distinction between raw first-token latency, first visible answer-token latency, output generation speed and full task completion time is essential for reasoning models.

K3.1 Pro’s officially documented framework support is unusually broad for a model this new. The Hugging Face release exposes Transformers integration and explicitly documents vLLM and SGLang. vLLM supports NVIDIA B300/GB300-class hardware, AMD MI355X and experimental TPU deployment; the TPU path is currently text-only in the documented recipe. NVIDIA NeMo AutoModel also implements the K3.1 architecture, NVIDIA NIM provides packaged deployment recipes, AMD ATOM supports MI350-series hardware, and vLLM provides both ordinary tensor parallelism and more specialized expert-parallel, decode-context-parallel, speculative-decoding and prefill/decode-disaggregated modes.

## Safety, factuality and known failure modes

Moonshot took an unusual training-time approach to cyber safety in the open checkpoint. During K3.1 Pro development, it found that vulnerability-research capability rose as post-training scaled and traced some of the increase to cyber material in both its mid-training corpus and RL environment mixture. For the open-weight checkpoint it removed identified cyber-related content from both stages while retaining general coding data. In Moonshot’s internal xdev-bench-hard, an otherwise related checkpoint without those controls scored 90.5% pass@3, while the released K3.1 Pro scored 81.0%. Reuters independently reported the capability-reduction policy at launch.

This is a capability-shaping measure embedded in training rather than merely a front-end refusal filter. It matters because open weights can be run outside Moonshot’s hosted moderation stack. Moonshot’s public material does not document a universal external safety classifier that necessarily accompanies the raw checkpoint under self-hosting, so the API’s moderation behavior should not be assumed to be inherent to every offline deployment. The open model’s cyber restraint therefore depends partly on removed training data and reduced learned capability rather than solely on server-side policy enforcement.

Factuality is one of K3.1 Pro’s stronger improvements over K3, although the strongest published hallucination numbers are vendor-owned. Moonshot’s internal Kimi Hallu Bench reports a 5.4% claim-level hallucination rate without tools, compared with 4.2% for GPT-6 Astra, 6.8% for Opus 5.5, 11.4% for Fable 5.1 and 14.8% for GLM-5.3. In its harder tool-enabled variant, K3.1 Pro records 8.3%, versus 5.5% for Astra, 15.0% for Opus and 23.3% for Fable. Moonshot explicitly cautions that this benchmark is adversarially constructed and does not represent ordinary daily error rates.

Artificial Analysis offers a more independent factuality signal through AA-Omniscience. Its direct K3.1 Pro/GPT-6.1 Sol comparison gives K3.1 Pro an AA-Omniscience v1.1 score of 30 and GPT-6.1 Sol 42, while the launch-era Artificial Analysis write-up reported that K3.1 Pro improved substantially over K3 but still produced a high error rate on its adversarial omniscience questions. This suggests that Moonshot’s factuality improvement is real relative to K3 while leaving meaningful headroom against the strongest closed models.

Several failure modes recur across benchmark evidence and user reports. K3.1 Pro can over-invest in reasoning and generate very large token volumes, which raises latency and cost even when token prices appear competitive. Its scientific terminal performance is materially weaker than Astra and Opus in Moonshot’s own table, and its ProgramBench score indicates difficulty reconstructing or reproducing programs from behavioral specifications relative to those models. Its HLE-Diamond performance shows a pronounced gap on very difficult closed-book reasoning.

The million-token context also needs a precise interpretation. The checkpoint advertises 1,048,576 tokens and production inference frameworks demonstrably run very long contexts, but NVIDIA’s NeMo documentation explicitly notes that the advertised maximum context has not independently been validated as the model’s full training length. The K3 technical report does describe progressive training to 1M context and synthetic tasks designed to force full-context retrieval, so there is evidence that the base architecture was trained at that scale. Still, “supports one million tokens” does not imply uniform reasoning quality across every position, perfect recall, or constant latency.

The DSA mechanism itself creates another subtle limitation. It scans inexpensive index keys over the available history and performs exact MLA attention only over the selected top 2,048 locations. This substantially reduces bandwidth, though it means attention quality at long context depends on the learned indexer identifying the right positions. A retrieval miss at the indexing stage cannot be recovered by the exact sparse-attention stage because the omitted keys never reach it. That is an architectural inference from the published mechanism rather than a Moonshot-reported benchmark failure.

Operationally, K3.1 Pro’s scale creates a deployment failure mode that smaller open models do not have: the model may be “open” while remaining inaccessible for local experimentation. Eight 288-GB-class accelerators are a validated minimum-class configuration for full high-performance serving, and lower-memory hardware requires substantially wider parallelism, offload or nonstandard community implementations. That makes inference topology, networking and memory bandwidth first-order determinants of real-world quality-of-service.

## Community sentiment and benchmark validity

Public sentiment in the first two weeks after release is enthusiastic about coding and agent autonomy while noticeably more skeptical about cost, rate limits and vendor benchmark claims. A high-engagement thread in r/kimi contains users describing K3.1 Pro as competitive with Opus 5.5 for coding, alongside repeated complaints that Kimi subscription quotas can be exhausted rapidly and explicit requests for independent testing before accepting Moonshot’s launch charts. Another early user compared K3.1 Pro with GPT-6 Astra on a Three.js agent task and reported that Kimi took longer wall-clock time but fewer agent steps and an estimated lower cost, although that test used a single handcrafted task and estimated Kimi pricing rather than a controlled benchmark, so its evidential weight is modest.

Negative anecdotes exist as well. One r/kimi user testing Lua/Luau development described the model as overcomplicating simple tasks and failing their elementary test, while another community report complained that K3.1 Pro spawned multiple subagents and rapidly consumed a usage allowance. These accounts cannot establish population-level failure rates, but they align with the measurable tendency toward long reasoning traces and expensive agentic execution.

A community-created StealthMark evaluation reports K3.1 Pro at 56.7 versus 50.7 for K3, with particularly large gains in multi-step coordination and planning; the author disclosed involvement in building the benchmark, which is useful transparency but also means the result should not be treated as a fully independent blinded audit. Reddit discussion around the launch repeatedly distinguishes between impressive benchmark screenshots and confidence that only comes from independent runs, a healthy reaction given how strongly current agent benchmarks depend on scaffolding.

I found no reliably indexed X.com posts through the available search infrastructure that added sufficiently verifiable quantitative evidence beyond the sources above. It would be inappropriate to manufacture an “X sentiment” section from reposts, screenshots or inaccessible threads. Reddit is therefore better represented in this synthesis than X, while the quantitative conclusions rely primarily on official documentation, Artificial Analysis, LiveBench, vLLM, AMD and NVIDIA.

Benchmark methodology deserves particular caution for K3.1 Pro because modern agent tests evaluate a system rather than an isolated neural network. Moonshot’s own footnotes state that K3.1 Pro may run inside Claude Code or Kimi Code, GPT-6 Astra inside Codex, and other models under their vendor-recommended harnesses; browsing, Python, context compaction, fallback models and tool policies can differ. Terminal-Bench scores therefore capture model capability plus shell-agent scaffolding, prompt engineering, retry policy, tool schema, environment setup, reasoning effort and sometimes inference provider behavior.

Reasoning-effort mismatches are another major confound. Moonshot’s principal K3.1 Pro results use max effort, temperature 1.0 and top-p 0.95 unless stated otherwise. Artificial Analysis sometimes tests Claude models under “High, Default Fallback,” while OpenAI and Kimi expose several effort levels. Comparing a max-effort Kimi run with a default-effort Claude run is partly a compute comparison, not purely a parameter-quality comparison.

Cost results are similarly sensitive to reasoning verbosity. On Artificial Analysis, K3.1 Pro and GPT-6.1 Sol have the same standard $2/$10 nominal input/output tariff, yet K3.1 Pro’s evaluation costs approximately $3.68 per task and Sol’s about $1.27 because their token use and completion speed differ. LiveBench reaches the same conceptual conclusion from another direction: K3.1 Pro costs roughly $1.04 per successful task, compared with about $0.76 for GPT-6.1 Sol and several dollars for the top Claude/Astra systems. Cost-per-token is therefore a poor proxy for cost-per-solved-task.

Contamination is also increasingly difficult to rule out on MMLU, GSM8K and HumanEval because they have been public for years and have near-ceiling performance among frontier systems. LiveBench explicitly positions itself as a dynamic benchmark intended to reduce contamination and increase difficulty. Even dynamic benchmarks are not immune to harness effects, judge-model bias, task-selection bias or subsequent exposure once released.

The most defensible overall ranking from the evidence available on November 8, 2026 is consequently domain-conditional. GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6.1 Sol retain a substantial advantage over K3.1 Pro on aggregated independent frontier evaluations, with the largest Kimi gaps appearing on difficult closed-book reasoning, scientific agent work and some program-reconstruction tasks. K3.1 Pro closes much of that gap, and sometimes reverses it, on long-horizon coding, office/document agents, finance and heavily tooled workflows. GLM-5.3 remains a strong open competitor, particularly for raw reasoning and coding, although K3.1 Pro presently has the stronger overall agentic story in the independent and vendor evidence examined here.

The larger technical significance of K3.1 Pro is its combination of frontier-scale sparse MoE, hybrid recurrent and sparse attention, million-token serving, quantization-aware low-precision weights and publicly downloadable parameters. The model does not establish a general performance lead over the best proprietary systems. It does establish that much of the frontier agentic capability previously confined to closed APIs can now be inspected, modified and self-hosted by organizations capable of operating roughly terabyte-scale model infrastructure.
