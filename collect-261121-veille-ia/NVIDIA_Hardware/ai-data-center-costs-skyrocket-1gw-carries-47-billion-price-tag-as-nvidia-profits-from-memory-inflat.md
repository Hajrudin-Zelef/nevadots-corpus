# AI Data Center Costs Skyrocket: 1GW Carries $47 Billion Price Tag as Nvidia Profits from Memory Inflation

The global AI race is evolving from a battle over chips into an ultimate showdown of capital strength. A new report from Bernstein reveals that building a 1GW (gigawatt) AI computing data center using Nvidia's (NVDA) next-generation Vera Rubin architecture—expected to deploy at scale by 2027—will require total capital expenditure of $47 billion (approximately NT$1.5 trillion). This astronomical figure not only exposes the staggering scale of the AI arms race but also highlights how memory, power, and cooling systems are replacing GPUs themselves as the most capital-intensive components of data centers.

Bernstein's report indicates that the average cost of a Vera Rubin NVL72 rack is estimated at approximately $9.1 million (roughly NT$300 million), significantly higher than the $8 million figure widely cited in the market. The primary driver of rising costs is the continued surge in High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) prices, coupled with simultaneous increases in storage, networking equipment, and power supply system costs. Building a 1GW data center would require approximately 3,557 racks, with rack-related equipment costs alone reaching roughly $32 billion (about NT$1 trillion). Adding land, buildings, substations, cooling facilities, and other infrastructure expenditures of approximately $15 billion (around NT$470 billion), the total investment approaches $47 billion.

This figure means that future hyperscale AI data center investments are approaching the scale of large nuclear power plants, high-speed rail systems, or national infrastructure projects. The market broadly believes that the AI data center arms race currently being driven by players including Microsoft (MSFT), Amazon (AMZN), Alphabet (GOOGL), Meta Platforms (META), and OpenAI could require hundreds of billions of dollars in capital investment over the coming years. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang recently estimated that the global AI data center and accelerated computing market could ultimately reach $3 trillion to $4 trillion (approximately NT$94.8 trillion to NT$126.4 trillion).

Despite rising costs, Bernstein believes Vera Rubin remains highly attractive as an investment because the improvement in computing performance is even more dramatic. According to the report, each Vera Rubin NVL72 rack can deliver 2,520 FP8 Petaflops of computing power, far exceeding the 720 FP8 Petaflops of the current Blackwell architecture. Analysts note that while per-rack costs have increased by about 14%, computing power has improved more than threefold, meaning the computing power obtained per dollar invested has still improved significantly.

**Cost Structure Reversal: Depreciation Overwhelms Electricity**

Another key finding of the report is that the economic model of AI data centers is undergoing a fundamental transformation. The market previously viewed electricity costs as the primary expense for data centers, but as AI hardware prices rapidly escalate, depreciation costs have gradually surpassed electricity bills. Bernstein estimates that at $0.15 per kilowatt-hour, the annual electricity cost for a 1GW AI data center is approximately $1.3 billion (about NT$41 billion). However, based on a six-year depreciation cycle, the annual depreciation expense for related hardware equipment reaches $7.9 billion (approximately NT$250 billion)—more than six times the electricity expenditure. Analysts believe future data center operational efficiency will depend more on equipment utilization rates and model training efficiency rather than simply energy prices.

**Memory Inflation Becomes Nvidia's Revenue Engine**

Facing enormous cost pressures, the market once worried that hardware manufacturers would face margin compression crises. But Bernstein bluntly states that this wave of "memory inflation" not only won't hurt Nvidia but will instead become a significant engine for its revenue growth. The key lies in Nvidia holding a rare "pricing privilege" in the industry and likely having already activated dynamic pricing mechanisms to directly pass costs onto customers. The report notes that by the time Vera Rubin data centers are fully operational, HBM prices are expected to surge more than threefold. At that point, memory and storage costs alone for each rack will reach $3.2 million (approximately NT$100 million), accounting for over one-third of total costs.

Bernstein wrote in the report: "Nvidia has likely already implemented some form of dynamic pricing mechanism, and we believe it will pass these increased memory costs entirely onto customers rather than absorbing them and hurting margins." This means the more memory prices rise, the higher the handling fees and product quotes Nvidia can charge, turning enormous costs into a significant growth driver.

**Supply Chain Beneficiaries Emerge**

Bernstein points out that as AI systems become larger, more power-hungry, and require more memory, related supply chains will continue to benefit. Among these, High Bandwidth Memory is viewed as the segment with the greatest growth potential in the coming years, with major suppliers currently including SK Hynix, Samsung Electronics, and Micron Technology (MU). Additionally, demand for power management, cooling systems, advanced substrates, and high-speed networking equipment will rise simultaneously. On the other hand, Bernstein maintains an Underperform rating on Quanta (2382.TW) and CoreWeave (CRWV), noting that while CoreWeave is currently one of the most aggressive expanders of AI data centers, capital expenditure pressures and financing needs could still compress future shareholder returns.

**SpaceX Joins the Fray: Seizing Computing Territory from Space**

Just as terrestrial AI infrastructure costs are soaring, SpaceX is entering this computing war from another dimension. The company went public on June 12 with a valuation of $1.75 trillion to $1.77 trillion (approximately NT$55.9 trillion), raising as much as $75 billion (about NT$2.4 trillion) in what became the largest IPO in history. SpaceX plans to begin space-based AI computing tests by the end of 2027 and envisions future deployment of orbital data center satellite constellations, even filing long-term blueprints for up to 1 million data center satellites. This represents a shift in AI infrastructure from "terrestrial power and land constraints" to "space energy and cooling advantages," creating an entirely new computing supply curve.

SpaceX has explicitly stated that initial orbital AI systems will use Nvidia GPUs, with a single AI satellite providing computing power equivalent to one GB300 AI rack. If space-based data centers materialize, Nvidia will expand from terrestrial cloud to become an "orbital computing supplier," with demand potentially seeing exponential growth. Furthermore, SpaceX emphasizes that space data centers can be continuously powered by solar energy, avoiding terrestrial grid bottlenecks—directly addressing the biggest challenge facing the AI industry in 2026: power shortages.

**The AI Industry's Circular Funding Network**

However, behind this AI race lies an intricate circular funding network. According to Gizmodo, the current AI industry has formed a peculiar dynamic of "mutual funding": AI startups buy chips from Nvidia, Nvidia in turn invests in these startups, driving their data center expansion plans and further stimulating demand for Nvidia chips. For example, Anthropic announced last November it would pay $30 billion (approximately NT$950 billion) to expand its use of Microsoft's Azure cloud services, while Nvidia and Microsoft respectively committed to investing up to $10 billion (about NT$320 billion) and $5 billion (roughly NT$160 billion) in Anthropic. In March this year, Amazon committed to investing $50 billion (approximately NT$1.6 trillion) in OpenAI, while OpenAI stated it would invest an additional $100 billion (about NT$3.2 trillion) into its existing partnership with Amazon Web Services.

This circular transaction network has raised questions about the stability of the AI industry. Companies driving the AI boom, such as OpenAI and Anthropic, have yet to achieve profitability. The investment wave that has swept through the industry in recent years is essentially a massive bet that AI technology will eventually deliver the financial growth enterprises have promised. If AI demand fails to take off as expected, those already-built mega data centers will instantly become useless, AI labs will be unable to pay chip and computing suppliers' bills, and since these suppliers are simultaneously the industry's primary financiers, the entire edifice could collapse like a castle built on sand.

SpaceX is also deeply entangled in this circular network. On one hand, the company sells computing power through its Colossus 1 data center in Memphis, Tennessee (billed as "the world's largest AI supercomputer") to Anthropic and Google, signing contracts worth $1.25 billion per month (approximately NT$40 billion) and $920 million per month (about NT$29 billion) respectively. On the other hand, Google announced in April it would invest up to $40 billion in Anthropic. These intricate relationships make all participants more vulnerable than they appear on the surface.

Nevertheless, from an investment perspective, this race centered on computing power, electricity, and capital has only just begun. Driven by continuously expanding AI models, the rise of agentic AI, and rapidly growing enterprise AI demand, global AI infrastructure investment is projected to surpass trillions of dollars before 2030. If Bernstein's estimates hold true, building just 10GW of Vera Rubin-class AI data centers could require investments as high as $470 billion (approximately NT$14.9 trillion)—equivalent to several years of combined capital expenditure for a major tech giant. And SpaceX's IPO adds an entirely new dimension to this race: competing for computing dominance from low Earth orbit.
