TL;DR

- Nvidia's Q3 revenue hit a record $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier and about 6% above forecasts.
- It guided to $130 billion for Q4, plus or minus 3%, versus about $125 billion expected by analysts.
- Interim CEO Colette Kress said Nvidia will stop selling H200 chips in China, a deal Huang helped broker.
- The SEC has reportedly opened a civil probe into whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors.
- Board chair Harvey Jones said Nvidia does not intend to reinstate Huang; shares closed at $155.46 on Nov. 17.

On **November 17, 2026**, Nvidia reported the biggest quarter in its history, **$115.8 billion** in revenue, five days after co-founder **Jensen Huang** resigned as CEO. Within the same few hours, the company’s board ruled out bringing him back; its interim CEO said Nvidia will stop selling its **H200** chips in China, and reports surfaced that the US **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**, the regulator that polices what public companies tell investors, had opened an investigation.

If you don’t follow Nvidia closely, here’s why this matters: Nvidia makes most of the chips that power today’s AI, and its stock sits in a huge share of index funds and retirement accounts. So this first report without Huang is a test of two things at once: whether the business still runs without him, and how much his exit changes the company’s risks. Below, we split the night’s news into what moved the numbers and what only moved the risk.

## **The Numbers: Another Record, Bigger Than Expected**

| **Measure** | **Q3 fiscal 2027 (ended Oct. 25)** | **Change from a year ago** | **What analysts expected** | 
| Revenue | **$115.8 billion** | +103% | About $109 billion | 
| Data Center revenue | **$107.6 billion** | +110% | — | 
| Edge Computing revenue | $8.2 billion | +42% | — | 
| GAAP net income | **$65.3 billion** | +105% | — | 
| Adjusted earnings per share | **$2.68** | +111% | About $2.47 to $2.48 | 
| Gross margin (GAAP) | 74.8% | +1.4 points | — | 
| Q4 revenue outlook | **$130 billion ± 3%** | — | About $125 billion | 

By our math, revenue came in about **6%** above the roughly $109 billion analysts expected and about **7%** above the $108 billion Nvidia itself had forecast in August. The Data Center business, which sells AI chips and systems to cloud companies and AI labs, made up about **93%** of all sales. Nvidia kept about **56 cents** of every dollar of revenue as profit.

Nvidia also said it has begun production shipments of **Vera Rubin NVL72**, its newest rack-scale AI system, which it called the fastest product ramp in its history. Interim CEO Colette Kress summed up the quarter in Nvidia’s official results release: *“Our momentum is real.”*

Whatever happened at the top, customers didn’t stop buying. The quarter ended on October 25, before Huang’s resignation, so these numbers mostly reflect orders placed months earlier. The forecast is where the post-Huang story starts.

## **The $130 Billion Forecast Has Two Footnotes**

Nvidia expects about **$130 billion** in revenue for its fourth quarter, plus or minus 3%. That’s a range of roughly **$126 billion to $134 billion**, and its midpoint sits about **4%** above the roughly $125 billion analysts expected, by our math.

Two details make the forecast look a little less dramatic than the headline:

1. **An extra week.** Nvidia’s fourth quarter has 14 weeks instead of 13. Spread evenly, $130 billion works out to about**$9.3 billion a week** , versus about**$8.9 billion** in the third quarter. That’s roughly**4%** weekly growth, not the 12% the raw totals suggest.
2. **Thinner margins.** Nvidia expects its gross margin, the share of revenue left after the cost of making its products, to fall to**71% to 72%** , from 74.8%. New products often cost more to make at first, and Vera Rubin is in its early ramp.

The forecast also assumes **no Data Center compute revenue from China**, which brings us to the night’s biggest strategic change.

## **No More H200 Sales to China**

On the earnings call, Kress said Nvidia will **stop selling H200 chips in China**. The **H200** is an older, still-capable AI chip that the US government allowed Nvidia to sell to approved Chinese buyers under a licensing deal brokered by Huang and President Donald Trump. Chips had to pass through the US, which let the government collect a 25% tariff.

Here’s how that deal played out, based on Nvidia’s own filings and reports:

| **When** | **What happened** | 
| Early 2026 | The US granted licenses to ship small amounts of H200s to specific Chinese customers | 
| Mid-2026 | Nvidia’s quarterly filing said it hadn’t earned any H200 revenue yet and didn’t know if China would allow imports | 
| Q3 fiscal 2027 | Nvidia earned about $2.4 billion from licensed chip sales to China, Kress said, according to one report | 
| November 16 | The Commerce Department issued a rule blocking advanced US chips from foreign data centers that serve Chinese customers remotely | 
| November 17 | Kress said Nvidia will stop H200 sales in China and created a board committee to oversee export compliance | 

**Why it matters less to revenue than it sounds:** if that $2.4 billion figure is right, it’s only about **2%** of the quarter’s sales. Kress also argued that demand for newer chips like Vera Rubin is strong enough that the export changes won’t hurt total revenue.

**Why it matters more to risk:** the bigger threat isn’t China itself, but the new Commerce rule. It requires licenses to ship advanced AI chips to **Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil**, and data centers may have to certify they don’t rent computing power to Chinese companies. Investment bank Jefferies estimates the rules could cut **$44 billion to $62 billion** from Nvidia’s data center revenue next year. By our math, that’s about **10% to 14%** of a year at this quarter’s data center pace.

Nvidia also formed a **National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee** of three independent directors, chaired by Suzanne Nora Johnson, which will meet at least quarterly. Its trade compliance teams now report to that committee.

## **The SEC Probe: What It Is, and What It Isn’t**

The SEC is opening a **civil** investigation into whether Nvidia’s disclosures misled investors, according to The Wall Street Journal.

A few things to understand:

- **Civil, not criminal.** A civil SEC probe can end with no action, a settlement and fine, or a lawsuit. It doesn’t mean anyone has been charged with a crime.
- **The question is disclosure.** The SEC looks at whether a company told investors everything important they needed to know. Here, the likely issue is whether Nvidia said enough about risks tied to its CEO and to chips reaching China.
- **It’s one of several.** Nvidia’s board is running its own independent investigation, Congress has asked questions, and the Commerce Department has been examining whether a Singapore company diverted Nvidia chips to China. Huang has agreed to a transcribed interview with three House committees on**December 1** , according to reports.

SEC probes like this often take a year or more, and they usually have little direct effect on a company’s sales. What they do is keep uncertainty around the stock, and that uncertainty is already visible in the price.

## **The Board Closes the Door on Huang**

Hours before the results, Huang said in a statement that he would be *“honored”* to return as CEO if the board decided that was in the best interest of shareholders, employees and the country. Some employees, investors and tech leaders had urged the board to bring him back.

Board chair **Harvey Jones** opened the earnings call by shutting that down. He said the board *“unanimously accepted”* Huang’s resignation and *“does not intend to reinstate”* him, declined questions about the departure, and said the board has *“every confidence”* in Kress. The search for a permanent successor is still open, with internal and external candidates and no public timeline.

## **FAQs**

### **What is an earnings call?**

It’s a conference call, usually held right after a public company releases quarterly results, where executives explain the numbers and take questions from analysts. Investors watch the forecast and tone closely, since those often move the stock more than the results themselves.

### **What is the difference between GAAP and adjusted earnings?**

GAAP figures follow standard US accounting rules. Adjusted, or non-GAAP, figures leave out some items, such as stock-based pay or one-time costs, that companies say distort the underlying picture. Both are useful, but adjusted numbers are the company’s own choice of what to exclude.

### **How big is Nvidia’s stake in Anthropic?**

Nvidia has invested in Anthropic and is expected to put about $10 billion into its planned stock listing, according to earlier reports. These details on the Anthropic IPO price range explain how the listing could value the company.

### **Does Nvidia invest in its own customers?**

Yes. Nvidia has invested in AI labs and cloud providers that also buy its chips, which critics say can inflate demand.

Many brokers offer pre-market and after-hours trading, when prices can swing more because fewer people are trading. Some platforms also offer tokenized versions of stocks.
