China's AI developers lose their last licensed route to Nvidia's data center chips just as a US rule targets the overseas data centers they rent compute from.

Nvidia will stop selling H200 chips in China, interim chief executive Colette Kress said on Tuesday, November 17, on the company’s first earnings call since Jensen Huang resigned as CEO the previous week. TechCrunch reported the decision, and Nvidia’s fourth-quarter outlook, published the same day, assumes no data center compute revenue from China at all. The company also set up a board committee to oversee national security and export controls.

The halt ends Nvidia’s part in an arrangement that Huang and President Donald Trump brokered: H200 chips bound for China were routed through the United States so that Washington could collect a 25% tariff on them, according to TechCrunch. Uptake was thin. TechCrunch reports that Beijing had largely refused to accept H200 deliveries, and quotes Jacob Feldgoise of Georgetown University’s Center for Security and Emerging Technology as saying China prefers to build demand for its own chips.

What remains unclear is how wide the stop goes. In an interview with CNBC’s Jim Cramer after the call, Kress said, according to the interview transcript: "And we are no longer shipping H200 at all, let alone not shipping it to China."

## A record quarter carries a China forecast of zero

Nvidia’s results release puts revenue for the third quarter of fiscal 2027 at US$115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier and 20% from the previous quarter. Data center revenue reached US$107.6 billion, up 110% year on year. The company guided to US$130 billion of revenue for the fourth quarter, plus or minus 3%, in a quarter that includes an extra week, and stated: "NVIDIA is not assuming any Data Center compute revenue from China in its outlook."

China did contribute last quarter. Nvidia sold about US$2.4 billion of approved data center products to China in the third quarter, and Kress said it has stopped selling licensed Hopper chips there. CNBC’s Kif Leswing reported the figure and noted that China came up only briefly on the call. In the Cramer interview, Kress put China at roughly 10% of shipments and said that business is gaming, automotive and workstation products.

Investors focused elsewhere. According to CNBC’s earnings report, revenue beat the LSEG consensus of US$109.1 billion, the guide topped the US$125.1 billion analysts expected, and Nvidia raised its growth forecast for fiscal 2028 to 75% from 70%. Shares rose more than 3% in extended trading after falling 24% in November; CNBC’s separate analysis put the after-hours gain at almost 5%.

## The bigger exposure is Chinese demand served from abroad

The H200 decision lands one day after the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim rule requiring data center operators in countries including Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand to obtain a US license before exporting advanced AI chips, CNBC reported. The rule is aimed at Chinese companies that rent Nvidia computing power located outside China, and China’s Ministry of Commerce has opposed it and threatened countermeasures. EastFrontier covered the rule when the US began requiring licenses for overseas data centers that rent chips to Chinese firms.

Jefferies estimates the rules could take US$44 billion to US$62 billion out of Nvidia’s data center revenue next year, according to TechCrunch. Export control attorney Dan Fisher-Owens told the outlet that large data centers buying Nvidia chips will have to certify that they serve no Chinese customers, and that those unable to do so may turn to other suppliers. Kress said the backlog of orders for advanced chips such as Vera Rubin means the rules will not affect Nvidia’s top line.

That backlog argument rests on demand from outside China absorbing whatever capacity the Southeast Asian data centers no longer take. TechCrunch notes that Nvidia’s Singapore subsidiary reported US$104 billion of revenue in 2025 before the company stopped disclosing the figure, and that customers in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia could be lost over time.

## A new board committee will watch export compliance

Nvidia’s board has created a committee to oversee national security policy, export control compliance and global trade risks, which will meet at least quarterly, according to TechCrunch and CNBC. Board chair Harvey Jones said, according to CNBC: "NVIDIA has both a responsibility and an opportunity to lead the industry in addressing these challenges."

The committee arrives as the company’s leadership changes under scrutiny. Jones said Huang resigned after "disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company’s code of conduct, standards and cultural norms," CNBC reported, and Kress told Cramer that the board has decided not to reinstate him. Neither Kress nor Jones addressed on the call the US investigations into allegations that Nvidia sold chips to pass-through companies serving buyers in China, according to CNBC.

The first measure of the new approach will come in the fourth-quarter report, which should show how much China-linked demand Nvidia gives up once both licensed Hopper sales and Southeast Asian rental capacity are gone. Before then, the number of overseas data center operators that apply for US licenses, or certify that they have no Chinese customers, will show how much of the Jefferies range is at stake.
