# Nvidia Halts H200 Shipments to China; U.S. Crackdown Leaves $2.4 Billion China Revenue Gap

Nvidia (NVDA) has stopped supplying its H200 artificial intelligence (AI) accelerators, which it had been selling to China under U.S. government export licenses. Colette Kress, Nvidia's interim chief executive and chief financial officer, said on the company's fiscal 2027 third-quarter (August–October) earnings call on the 17th (U.S. time) that "we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper data-center products to China." The company also excluded that revenue from its forward outlook.

The scale is what matters. In the third quarter alone, Nvidia shipped $2.4 billion (~3.2 trillion won) worth of Hopper architecture-based data-center products to China, equal to roughly 2% of total quarterly revenue. The company's fourth-quarter (November–January) revenue guidance of $130 billion (~173.1 trillion won) excludes China Hopper sales entirely.

Kress did not explain the specific reasons for halting shipments. The decision, however, coincides with Washington ratcheting up restrictions aimed at China's access to advanced AI semiconductors. On the 16th, the Trump administration tightened semiconductor export reviews for some countries and imposed measures limiting sales when overseas data centers provide Chinese companies with remote access to high-performance AI chips and servers. The move targets not only direct chip exports to China but also workarounds through third-country data centers.

The H200 is two generations behind Nvidia's current flagship, Vera Rubin, and even older than the latest Blackwell. Even so, it is highly symbolic as an export channel to China that former CEO Jensen Huang opened by leveraging his personal relationship with President Donald Trump. Washington never allowed Blackwell sales to China but had approved the H200. Shipments were routed through the United States, with the U.S. government collecting a 25% tariff.

That sales channel began to wobble with Huang's resignation. He left the company last week amid multiple allegations of extramarital affairs and security controversies tied to China. Reports emerged that a woman linked to him is under U.S. investigation over possible ties to Chinese intelligence support and semiconductor smuggling. Beijing has denied espionage allegations, and no related charges have been publicly substantiated.

Despite the leadership vacuum, the results themselves were solid. Third-quarter revenue rose 103% from a year earlier to $115.8 billion (~154.2 trillion won). Still, the market's attention is fixed less on the numbers than on the direction of the China business and the succession picture. Board Chairman Harvey Jones drew a line on the call, saying "the board has no intention of bringing Jensen back as CEO." Nvidia also created a new board committee dedicated to national security and export-control compliance. Jones stressed that changes in the global security environment are having a major impact on the semiconductor market and that the next CEO must respond carefully to related regulations.

The market is weighing the fallout from the new export restrictions. Investment bank Jefferies estimates the measures could cut Nvidia's data-center revenue next year by $44 billion to $62 billion (~58.6 trillion to 82.6 trillion won). Interim CEO Kress maintained that the backlog for cutting-edge chips such as Vera Rubin is so deep that overall revenue will not be affected — a point on which views inside and outside the company diverge.

Another variable is that China was already lukewarm about accepting the H200. Jacob Feldgoise, a researcher at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET), noted that Beijing has been reluctant to bring in American equipment in order to channel demand to its domestic chip industry. Conversely, major Chinese AI labs have been found training models on export-banned Nvidia chips through offshore data centers and other means. The new rules target this kind of circumvention-driven training demand as well.

In South Korean markets, the view is emerging that the issue should be split into a short-term negative and a medium-term opportunity. Kim Doo-eon, an analyst at Hana Securities, said the leadership vacuum and uncertainty over the funding pipeline for AI investment are an immediate burden, but if U.S. technology controls on China tighten, China's catch-up pace could slow and give South Korean companies breathing room. Still, the fact that China remains the largest customer for South Korean semiconductors is a burden. According to provisional data from South Korea's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy, semiconductor exports to China in October totaled $14.8 billion (~19.7 trillion won), accounting for 70.1% of all exports to China.

In the end, what investors will be watching is the order book and cash flow that follow the shocking headlines. If AI investment is not canceled but shifts to other chips, demand for memory and power infrastructure has room to continue. On the other hand, some observers say that if U.S. restrictions dampen data-center investment across Asia, the fallout could spread to potential customer losses in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia.
