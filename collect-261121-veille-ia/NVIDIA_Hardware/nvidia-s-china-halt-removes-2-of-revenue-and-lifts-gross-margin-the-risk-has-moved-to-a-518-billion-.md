Colette Kress, Nvidia's interim chief executive, ended the export question on the November 17 earnings call with one sentence: the company's guidance "does not have any licensed Hopper data center products included, and we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China."

The same quarter, NvidiaNVDA-- had sold $2.4 billion of those licensed Hopper parts to China. Against $115.8 billion of total revenue, that is 2.1%.

Kress did not say why the shipments are ending. Bloomberg reported that she did not elaborate, and TechCrunch, citing a Georgetown researcher, reported that China's government had largely refused to accept delivery even before Nvidia stopped, preferring to steer demand toward domestic chips.

The rest of the quarter is where the economics sit. Revenue rose 103% from a year earlier, gross margin held at 74.8%, and Nvidia guided the January quarter to $130 billion with no China data-center compute in the number at all. Split the headline apart and the China halt is the smallest and most margin-friendly of the quarter's moving parts. What actually changed is the cost of memory, and the scale of the promises Nvidia now makes on its customers' behalf.

## What the $2.4 billion measures - and what it doesn't

The figure measures China-directed sales of one licensed product family, not Nvidia's total China revenue geography. Kress described "licensed Hopper data center products," meaning the H200 chips the U.S. government permitted Nvidia to sell to approved Chinese customers, with a 25% cut of the sales routed to Washington.

That is a different number from Nvidia's China revenue line. On the basis Nvidia now reports - the headquarters of the customer - China including Hong Kong was $7.9 billion, or 8.2% of revenue, in the July quarter, according to the company's 10-Q as compiled by MLQ.ai. The licensed Hopper line was under 1% of data-center revenue in that same quarter, and by simple division it reached 2.2% of data-center revenue in the October quarter. In other words, the legal H200 channel ramped hard right before it closed.

So the China exposure was never $2.4 billion, and the guidance exclusion is broader than the product line the headline names: Nvidia says the $130 billion January-quarter outlook - a 14-week quarter against 13 in the one just reported - assumes no data-center compute revenue from China whatsoever. That exclusion is not new, either. Kress told analysts in August that the forward outlook already contained no China data-center compute revenue, which makes the $2.4 billion an upside that guidance never counted. The halt mostly removes a line the model had already written to zero.

Even so, $130 billion is 12% above the October quarter, or about 4% per week once the extra week is stripped out.

There is one unresolved measurement problem. Nvidia said it will file its formal 10-Q "in the coming weeks" rather than on earnings day, so the October-quarter geographic split has not yet been published. Until it is, anyone quoting a China percentage for the quarter is quoting a product-line figure and calling it a geography.

## The margin math runs the opposite way from the headline

Nvidia has described the licensed Hopper shipments to China as "dilutive to corporate gross margins." Taken at face value, exiting that business should remove a drag on the blended margin.

Nvidia reported $86.68 billion of gross profit on $115.84 billion of revenue, a 74.8% margin. Strip out $2.4 billion of China Hopper revenue and the matching cost of goods, and the blended margin rises. If those chips carried a 30% gross margin, the ex-China margin is about 75.8%, roughly a point higher. Even at a 70% gross margin - high for a part sold under a 25% government revenue share - the blended figure rises to about 74.9%. The direction is certain; the precise size depends on a cost Nvidia has not disclosed.

That is worth holding next to the guidance. Nvidia expects gross margin of 71.0% to 72.0% in the January quarter, 2.8 to 3.8 points below 74.8%. Losing the dilutive China line pushes against that decline. It cannot explain it.

The explanation is memory. Supply commitments - mainly multiyear memory purchases - rose to $390 billion from $279 billion in a single quarter, an increase of $111 billion, or 40%. Total future commitments reached $518 billion, of which $222 billion falls due in the coming fiscal year, according to the CFO commentary; $203 billion of component spending is planned for fiscal 2028, Nvidia said, as reported by Bloomberg. Nvidia is not being squeezed by a customer walking away. It is paying more for the parts that go into the product, and betting it can pass that through with higher prices.

## Nvidia now manufactures part of its own demand

The commitment table is where the risk migrated. Nvidia disclosed up to $118 billion of guarantees it may owe third parties if partners default, almost all of it - $105 billion - tied to SB Energy's Ohio campus leased to OpenAI for 20 years. Add $32 billion of neocloud agreements under which Nvidia takes compute capacity if those operators secure financing, and the company is no longer just selling into demand; it is underwriting the buildings that will contain its racks.

The CFO commentary says why, in unusually plain language: neoclouds and AI labs "are seeing extraordinary demand for AI infrastructure, yet many are growing faster than their balance sheets and long-term credit profiles can support."

The balance sheet records the same shift from the other side. Accounts receivable reached $81.0 billion on October 25, up from $38.5 billion at the end of January - a 111% increase, faster than the 99% rise in nine-month revenue over the same stretch. Days sales outstanding lengthened to 64 from 60, which Nvidia attributed partly to extended payment terms on large multi-quarter deals. Over nine months the company spent $58.1 billion buying equity securities, against $8.9 billion of capital spending, and issued $24.9 billion of debt.

Against all that, cash plus marketable debt securities totaled $53.5 billion at quarter end - less than half the $118 billion guarantee ceiling. Most guarantees are conditional and phase in as buildings complete, with the first Ohio building expected in fiscal 2029, and Nvidia says it has not paid on any of them. That is a genuine mitigant. It is also a promise about projects that do not yet exist.

## What the price is actually discounting

Nvidia closed at $155.46 in the regular session before the results, down 0.44% on the day and about 36% over the past month - a slide Fortune reported had shed more than a trillion dollars of market value. It rose roughly 5% after hours on the results, to about $163. Using the quarter's 24.19 billion weighted-average diluted share count, the close valued the company near $3.76 trillion.

Run the company's own guidance through a simple model and the multiple compresses sharply. October-quarter non-GAAP earnings were $2.68 a share; the first nine months totaled $6.77. At the $130 billion revenue guide, a 71.5% gross margin and $10.7 billion of non-GAAP operating expense, the January quarter produces roughly $2.8 a share, putting fiscal 2027 near $9.60 - a calculation that assumes a 17% tax rate and negligible other income, so treat it as an illustration rather than a forecast. At $155.46 that is about 16 times. Apply the same $9.60 to the record high of about $240 the shares reached before the slide and the multiple is about 25 times. Part of the compression is therefore arithmetic - a lower price meeting a freshly raised earnings base - rather than a change in what investors think the business is worth.

Three cautions before anyone treats 16 times as cheap. First, that estimate is arithmetic from management's guidance, not a consensus figure, and it depends on a gross margin Nvidia has guided to a range. Second, GAAP flatters the comparison: nine-month GAAP earnings of $7.55 a share exceed non-GAAP because they include $25.5 billion of gains on equity securities - marks on stakes in the same AI companies Nvidia is financing - partly offset in the quarter by a $1.8 billion impairment on non-marketable holdings. Third, the multiple only looks low if fiscal 2028 revenue grows 75%, which is what Kress now projects, up from 70% three months ago. At 75%, fiscal 2028 revenue would be roughly $740 billion.

## The number to watch is not China

The China question is close to settled mechanically: the direct revenue was small, it was margin-dilutive, and guidance already excludes it. The open question is whether the demand Nvidia has contracted to serve can be paid for by someone other than Nvidia.

The test appears in the next two disclosures. Watch whether accounts receivable and guarantees grow faster than revenue again; that ratio, not the China line, tells you whether the $518 billion commitment book is being met by end customers or carried by the supplier. And watch the geographic footnote in the delayed 10-Q, which will show what China actually contributed in the quarter the H200 channel closed.

One gap remains genuinely unresolved. New rules issued the day before earnings bar advanced U.S. chips from foreign data centers that serve Chinese customers remotely. Jefferies, as reported by TechCrunch, estimated the change could trim $44 billion to $62 billion from Nvidia's data-center revenue next year. Kress said the backlog for Vera Rubin means the rules would have no impact on the top line. The two claims are hard to reconcile, and neither can be tested yet. That gap matters more than the Hopper line because it targets demand routed through third countries - the exposure that never appeared in the $2.4 billion, and the part of the China question the 2% figure cannot answer.
