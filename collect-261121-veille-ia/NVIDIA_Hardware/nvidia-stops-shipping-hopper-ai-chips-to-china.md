## Nvidia’s China Halt

Last quarter, Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data center products to China. Next quarter, it expects none. Interim chief executive Colette Kress told analysts the company has stopped those shipments, and Nvidia’s outlook assumes no data center compute revenue from China.

### **Key Takeaways**

- Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper data center products to China in the quarter that ended October 25.
- Interim CEO Colette Kress said shipments have stopped and gave no reason or timeline.
- The halt casts doubt on a special US licensing arrangement for H200 sales to Chinese buyers.

## What Kress Said

Kress spoke on Tuesday’s earnings call, her first since Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive last week. “Our guidance does not have any licensed Hopper data center products included, and we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China,” she said.

She did not explain the decision or say whether it is permanent. The rest of the business is booming. Nvidia reported third-quarter revenue of $115.8 billion and guided the current quarter to $130 billion, plus or minus 3%.

## A Deal Caught Between Two Governments

Hopper chips, including the H200, train and run advanced AI models. Washington restricted such exports to China in 2022. In January, it eased its H200 policy and began reviewing licenses case by case. Ten Chinese companies won approval to buy, including Alibaba, Tencent and ByteDance.

The arrangement came with a 25% tariff on eligible chips imported into the US before they shipped on to China. Huang had lobbied for months to open that door.

Beijing pushed the other way. It restricted H200 imports and withheld approval for large-scale purchases as it tries to cut reliance on US technology. About 600,000 H200 chips sit in storage in Hong Kong, awaiting shipment to the mainland, according to reports. Several large Chinese internet companies still obtained about 10,000 H200 chips each in recent months.

## Summit Day in Shenzhen

The halt surfaces as Donald Trump meets Xi Jinping in Shenzhen today, on the sidelines of the APEC summit. Chip sales have come up at earlier summits: after meeting Xi last year, Trump said he would allow H200 sales to approved Chinese customers. Huang, who lobbied hardest for those licenses, had already faced pressure in Congress over China chip limits. One outlet noted that the stoppage comes days after the co-founder’s departure.

For Chinese AI labs, the gap leaves fewer options. They can wait for Beijing’s approvals, rely on domestic chips, or find other paths. Documents have shown Chinese developers using outputs of US models to train domestic rivals. Strong demand for compute remains, which is why the stockpile in Hong Kong grew despite Beijing’s import curbs.
