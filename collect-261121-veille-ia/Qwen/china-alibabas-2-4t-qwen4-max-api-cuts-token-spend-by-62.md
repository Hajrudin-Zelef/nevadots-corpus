Alibaba recently released Qwen4-Max with a 2.4-trillion-parameter backbone, native vision capabilities and a one-million-token context window.

Independent benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis claims the model is above average in intelligence and reasonably priced. Qwen4-Max scores 43 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, placing it above average among comparable models – but just behind Moonshot’s Kimi K3.1 Pro, which scores 46.

Eddie Wu, CEO of Alibaba, recently laid out his vision for AI at Alibaba’s annual Apsara Conference in Hangzhou, China: “Today, machine power already drives 99.9% of the world’s physical work.

“In the future, the total amount of human thinking will continue to grow, but machine thinking will expand even faster, ultimately shouldering 99.9% of all thinking.”

The model has one strong point — price.


## Price wins

The Chinese giant's key selling point for the new model is price. Qwen4-Max outperforms its previous iteration (Qwen3.8-Max) with fewer tokens.

On the Work360 benchmark, made up of challenging industry tasks, token usage drops by approximately 24% and 35% at Xhigh and High effort, respectively. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, which measures complex tasks such as coding and systems administration, the reductions are approximately 48% and 49%, respectively.

On LinkedIn, Illia Martyn, Founder of SkillCanvas, wrote about the Qwen4-Max: “The model has one strong point — price. Qwen’s answers are about 4.4 times cheaper than [Anthropic’s] Opus. One task from the Artificial Analysis benchmark cost US$3.27 on Qwen and US$10.04 on Opus, meaning three times cheaper.

“And that's despite Qwen being chatty and producing noticeably more text than the average model. If you need to sort through a pile of documents or write boring code, the price difference matters more than the score difference.”

Alibaba highlights it has lowered API prices compared with Qwen3.8-Max, with particularly large reductions in cached-input pricing. In its own Work360 evaluation, the average cost per task drops from US$4.43 to US$1.67 — a 62% reduction.

The firm adds that Qwen4-Max powers 24/7 agents that tackle long-running tasks through autonomous planning and continuous iteration, delivering usable results in stages while keeping users in control – suggesting the model can power “always-on” agents like OpenAI’s recently released Dots.

Unlike some previous versions of Qwen, which were open-weight releases, it is available exclusively via API and Alibaba's cloud platforms (with smaller open-source models in the Qwen4 family slated to follow).

## Safety upgrades

For software engineering training tasks, Alibaba added code security checks on top of functionality checks to strengthen the model’s intrinsic secure-coding capabilities.

The company notes that the model performs particularly well at avoiding core risks such as injection, memory safety and broken access control, markedly reducing vulnerabilities in code generated by default and helping developers shrink the system’s attack surface at the source.

For cybersecurity, it filters data according to the specific purpose of each task, retaining data for defensive tasks such as vulnerability verification, detection, and remediation, while removing data for offensive tasks aimed at enhancing attack capabilities.

A review by Alibaba’s internal security experts of long-horizon task trajectories (including full chain-of-thought, CoT) found that Qwen4-Max’s cybersecurity defense capabilities have improved substantially, and that safety against offensive uses has also been significantly strengthened. No capabilities posing large-scale cybersecurity risks have been observed.

For chemical, biological, radiological or nuclear risks, the company says it designed dedicated safety data and enhanced Qwen4-Max’s safety during the safety post-training stage. In internal evaluations, Qwen4-Max identified more requests carrying potential risks and provided safe responses, without exhibiting over-refusal. A review by its internal experts of the model’s answers (including full CoT) found that the solutions the model proposes are not genuinely innovative and cannot complete the task end-to-end.

The safeguards come during a time of heightened scrutiny over AI risks. The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists recently highlighted that AI raises a serious set of concerns in the nuclear weapons domain, where governance gaps are already visible and that the consequences of integration could be existential.

The group, which sets the Doomsday Clock, added that AI use in early-warning and crisis-decision support could compress decision time and increase pressure on leaders to launch nuclear weapons in response to false or manipulated information.

A Reuters report adds that Chinese AI model developers have in recent months increased safety disclosures in new model releases, amid growing global concern surrounding “rogue AI” and loss-of-control risks.

The release follows an incident in October when Alibaba’s open-weight model is alleged to have ignored instructions and tried to secretly send data to the company’s cloud storage server in Beijing, China, during security testing – a claim made by rival AI lab Cohere.

The Chinese company’s Qwen3.8-Flash-Next model was asked to write a defensive detection rule, a piece of automated logic to identify suspicious activity, when it was caught by researchers “doing something that should never happen”, Cohere notes.
