## Why the Essay Says Now

Amodei gives two reasons. The first is what he describes as recursive self-improvement: âsince roughly this summer, AI has been advancing drastically faster, driven primarily by AIâs growing ability to build the next generation of AI.â He writes that this is âstarting to happen across the industry, including at Anthropic.â

## What the Document Says

The essay sets out a three-step plan and says the steps âdo not need to be taken strictly in order.â

- **Embedded Evaluators.** Each frontier company gives âongoing, employee-like access to a team of embedded third-party evaluators (such as METR).â Their job is to check that safety practices are followed, report incidents, and assess the alignment of training pipelines as well as finished models. Anthropic says it is committing to this step on its own now. The evaluators will get âdesks in our offices, access badges, and company laptops.â They will also have the right to publish findings âwithout editorial control by Anthropic.â Anthropic keeps a narrow right to redact security-sensitive, legally privileged, or confidential material, but the essay says âwe canât redact findings just because they are unfavorable.â
- **Democratic Coordination.** Frontier companies in democratic countries agree on âcommon safety standards as well as limits on the rate of unchecked AI progress.â The essay says the most effective route is âregulation that targets all US frontier AI companies.â Because passing laws takes time, it also asks the US government to issue âa narrow waiverâ of antitrust rules so companies can hold joint safety discussions. One possible scheme it describes is a series of capability âcheckpoints,â where a model that reaches a given capability must come with alignment certifications. The essay also lists measures aimed at China: restricting chip and chip-equipment sales, cracking down on unauthorized distillation, and preventing model-weight theft.
- **Global Coordination.** The essay says âthe US and other democratic governments attempt to coordinate with authoritarian governments, to the extent this is possible.â It lists four levels of possible agreement, in order of difficulty:
  1. A ban on narrow, clearly dangerous uses, such as producing biological weapons.
  2. Testing models for acute risks before release.
  3. A âspeed limitâ on recursive self-improvement.
  4. A full pacing or âpause.â Of this last level, Amodei writes: âI support floating this, but I think it is unlikely to actually happen any time soon.â

The essay says any extra time should go to operational rigor, alignment, interpretability, and testing and evaluation. It states that interpretability work âcould make profound progress in 1â2 years.â
