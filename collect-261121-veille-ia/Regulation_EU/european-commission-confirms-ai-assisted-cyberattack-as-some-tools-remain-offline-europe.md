Some European Union digital tools remain offline after an October cyberattack involving AI, the European Commission confirmed on November 19. Spokesperson Thomas Regnier said authorities detected the incident on October 22 and took applications down as a precaution. Most have been restored, but the investigation remains open and the attackers have not been publicly identified.

October 22: detection and protective shutdowns

The shutdowns were a defensive response, rather than a list of systems officials confirmed the attackers had disabled. Regnier did not identify the attacker or explain the operation in detail, according to the Associated Press.

Politico, which first reported the incident, described a wider operational impact. It said the attack lasted at least 10 days and affected translation and reimbursement tools, along with databases used for salaries and timesheets. Regnier declined to confirm those details.

CERT-EU oversees digital security across the bloc’s institutions, which together employ about 40,000 people in Brussels alone. The Commission said it was keeping in touch with that agency as it responded. Its spokesperson also told Recorded Future News that affected individuals had been notified, without publicly identifying them.

AI use confirmed; the named tool remains unconfirmed

Regnier explicitly confirmed that AI tools were used and said such tools can be misused to detect vulnerabilities. The Record described the software involved as publicly available. But officials did not name it or explain the attackers’ motivation. AP described this as the first time the EU had reported a cyberattack using an AI tool.

In its November 19 article, The Record relayed Politico’s account that CERT-EU had identified attackers using ARTEX, an open-source AI hacking platform.

ARTEX uses commercial AI models to automate the discovery and exploitation of security weaknesses, according to The Record. The outlet said the FBI had warned the previous week that criminals and state-sponsored groups were using it against government agencies and critical sectors worldwide. Some intrusions progressed from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours, the warning said.

we have not detected any evidence of a data breach affecting commission staff as a whole.

Thomas Regnier, European Commission spokesperson, quoted by the Associated Press

An open investigation—and a disclosure dispute

Regnier’s statement about staff data was qualified: investigators had found no evidence of a breach affecting staff as a whole. He also said officials were unaware of any ransom demand, according to The Record.

Asked why the incident had not been disclosed earlier, Regnier said publicizing defensive measures could undermine their effectiveness. He rejected the suggestion that the Commission had concealed the incident, telling journalists there had been no hiding.

The response now extends beyond restoring applications. The Commission said it was preventively checking other systems and staying in contact with the European Data Protection Supervisor.
