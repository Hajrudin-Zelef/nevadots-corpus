*Opting out of AI matters. It is not the same as having a voice in how AI enters public schools.*

Google has faced enormous criticism for making Gemini in Classroom the default for K–12 students. Parent groups pushed back and asked districts to turn it off. On November 3, Google announced that starting in early 2027, Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Search would be off until administrators turned them on. Districts that wanted AI off would lose Search too. Google partly walked this back: districts could keep Search without the Gemini apps, but AI Overviews and AI Mode can’t be removed from Search.

Parents protested in seven cities, and Senator Markey asked Google to let schools turn off AI without losing Search. This week, more than 540 Google employees and contractors petitioned for the same thing. New York City, Los Angeles, and Chicago said they are weighing other search engines. Los Angeles has already disabled AI Overviews for students, and its chief information officer said the district could change its default search engine “literally overnight.”

**More Choice**

Most commentators see this as a fight over who controls the ability to opt in or out of AI. When a monopoly holds these controls, they argue, children’s safety is at risk. Markey’s letter is revealing. He asks whether Google will let schools turn off AI Overviews, Gemini, and Guided Learning while keeping Search, and if that isn’t technically feasible, why not. These are good questions, but the letter asks for a better choice.

**Exit Is Not Voice**

Why isn’t a better choice enough? In 2000, David Labaree, drawing on Albert Hirschman, distinguished between two ways people respond when an institution fails them. They can exit, taking their business elsewhere, or use voice, staying and pushing for change from inside. Exit, Labaree suggested, is the market’s tool. It aims to secure the best “goods” for one’s own children. Voice matters because education is a public good. Even after you leave, you live in the world the system produces.

Opting in or out of AI is a form of exit. It matters, but it leaves the larger question untouched: who decides what AI does in schools? And when the core tools of schooling belong to a company that answers to its investment strategy, the channels for voice weaken.

Exit is also unequal. A large district can switch search engines overnight. A family cannot switch out of the platform its school runs on. Assignments, grades, course materials, and messages to teachers run through Google Classroom, often on school-issued Chromebooks. A parent can object to AI, but leaving the platform means leaving school. Families with their own laptops, home internet, and time can work around a school’s choices. Families who depend on the school’s device and accounts, often lower-income families, cannot. For them, the platform is the school. The families with the least room to exit are the ones who most need voice.

**The All-or-Nothing Bet**

Follow the money. Alphabet, Google’s parent company, plans to spend $195–$205 billion on capital projects this year, most of it on AI infrastructure. That bet pays off only if AI becomes the way people search, not an add-on they can turn off. If schools get AI-free Search, employers, regulators, and colleges will ask for it too. Markey’s question about technical feasibility may miss the point. The barrier is commercial. Schools are not where Google makes most of its money, but they are where it builds its future users.

**Google Chose How Far**

That is why the protests worked only up to a point. Google moved, but it chose how far. Separating the Gemini apps from Search probably cost little. Google didn’t budge on AI in Search, the part tied to its core business. Districts can say yes or no, but Google decides what they are saying yes or no to.

This isn’t unique to Google. In 2024, Khan Academy, a nonprofit, added its AI teacher tools to its order with the Louisiana Department of Education as “standard features” on all teacher and administrator accounts, “including for any renewal.” The state can object to changes in how student data is used, but only until the term ends. Renewals fall under Khan’s “then current” terms. The risk is that districts find out what their systems do only after the vendor has changed them.

Voice works best when it reaches a public body that can act and must answer. A board votes, the vote is on the record, and voters respond. Voice aimed at Google works differently. Google decides what to concede, with no vote or public record, and can change course in the next update. In January 2025, it added Gemini to business Workspace plans and raised prices for every customer, AI user or not. Customers could take it or leave it.

Most AI enters schools quietly, through pilots, renewals, and consent calendars. The largest districts can threaten to leave. But a threat to leave is not a say in what Google builds, and many families cannot leave at all. Google has given schools more choice, for now. What they need is a say in what Google offers in the first place. Until they have it, opting out will keep standing in for voice, and Google and other vendors will keep deciding whether and how far to bend.
