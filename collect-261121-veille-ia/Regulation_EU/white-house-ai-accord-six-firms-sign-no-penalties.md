The White House Accord on Super Intelligence is a voluntary pledge, not a regulation: six technology companies promised internal audits and oversight of their most advanced AI systems, and nothing in it penalizes a company that falls short. President Trump announced it on Tuesday, September 29, 2026, and on the same day signed a separate executive order telling federal agencies to say “Super Intelligence” instead of “artificial intelligence.” Al Jazeera, CNBC and MediaNama covered the two announcements together: a safety pledge from the industry’s biggest names, paired with a rebranding that has nothing to do with safety.

## What the accord commits companies to

The accord, subtitled “Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities,” is a pledge document rather than legislation. It followed a White House lunch that CNBC reported on, and Al Jazeera describes it as a voluntary pact urging companies to self-police. Each signatory agrees to build four layers of controls and audits covering risks such as cybersecurity, biosecurity and chemical threats tied to frontier models.

```
Layer 1: Internal technical controls (training + deployment monitoring)
Layer 2: Dedicated internal oversight team
Layer 3: Independent external auditors
Layer 4: Independent board committee reviewing both internal and external reports
```
The first two layers are internal: monitoring of model capabilities and alignment, and a standing team that oversees those controls rather than treating safety review as a one-time checkpoint. The last two bring in outside auditors and a board-level committee that reviews what both the internal team and the auditors find. Signatories also pledged that their AI tools would not “hack or access technical systems in unintended ways,” which speaks to concern about autonomous AI agents acting outside their intended scope.

The signing list is narrower than the guest list. Critics read that gap as a sign of a self-selected club rather than an industry-wide standard. For a reader judging how much the pledge covers, the missing names matter: it binds, morally at most, only the companies that chose to sign.

## Why “morally binding” is not legally binding

Asked whether the accord carried legal force, Trump said, “I think it’s morally binding,” according to NBC News. In the same coverage he said there was a belief in the room that there should be “tremendous self-regulation,” adding that “we automatically have regulation with the Department of Justice, the FBI, all of that.” He had also called the doomsday scenarios a “hoax” and argued that US companies must keep innovating to stay ahead of China. Al Jazeera’s explainer likewise frames the pact as self-policing, with no penalties for noncompliance.

“A set of principles and commitments around building robust internal controls and detecting if there are any issues with the technology, coupled with multiple layers of auditing and controls, starting with internal risk review, external auditors and evaluators.”

Mark Zuckerberg, CEO, Meta, via NBC News

Zuckerberg’s wording leans on “principles” and “commitments” rather than “rules” or “requirements,” which matches the accord’s legal status: a pledge a signatory can walk away from without consequence. The practical test is simple. A commitment you cannot verify is only as strong as the disclosure behind it, so the thing to look for is whether any signatory publishes audit results or names its external auditors.

## How Zuckerberg and Huang shaped the pact

The accord did not originate inside the West Wing. Semafor’s exclusive reporting, republished by Yahoo News, traces the idea to a conversation between Zuckerberg and House Speaker Mike Johnson at a state dinner the previous week for Chinese leader Xi Jinping. Zuckerberg reportedly raised his concerns about looming AI regulation there, then turned to Nvidia’s Jensen Huang to help draft principles the industry could bring to its next meeting with Trump.

According to Semafor, Zuckerberg circulated a draft ahead of Tuesday’s meeting and Huang helped build support among other invited executives, including those from OpenAI, Anthropic and xAI. The result is a document shaped largely by industry and then announced by the White House. It reads less like a mandate handed to industry and more like an industry proposal the administration agreed to co-sign, which is the right lens for weighing how much pressure it puts on the signatories.

## The “Super Intelligence” executive order and its 60-day clock

Separately, Trump signed an executive order titled “Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence.” Per MediaNama’s reporting, it directs federal agencies to replace “artificial intelligence” and “AI” with “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in official communications where statute does not block the change, and states that the executive branch “will not acknowledge the usage of ‘Artificial Intelligence’ and ‘AI’ in any applicable setting.” For readers who follow government AI use, our piece on the America.gov chatbot built on Gemini and Grok covers a federal deployment this wording will touch.

The order also gives the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology 60 days to propose legislative language formally defining “Super Intelligence” in federal law. Counted from the September 29 signing, that lands around November 28, 2026. It is arguably the most consequential part of the announcement, because a legal definition could eventually underpin binding rules, which the accord itself avoids. The question is whether the draft attaches enforcement to the four layers or only codifies the new terminology.

## How the accord compares with earlier US policy and the EU AI Act

The accord is a third model of US AI policy: neither a binding federal rule nor pure deregulation, but an industry-authored pledge the administration has endorsed without attaching enforcement. The table sets it beside the earlier US moves and the EU’s binding law.

| Framework | Year | Legal Status | Enforcement | Scope | 
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Executive Order 14110 (Biden) | 2023 | Binding executive order | Agency rulemaking authority | Federal agencies, frontier model safety testing | 
| Trump AI Action Plan | 2025 | Policy roadmap | Agency guidance, no mandates | Deregulation, infrastructure, exports | 
| White House Accord on Super Intelligence | 2026 | Voluntary pledge | None (self-policing) | 6 signatory companies’ frontier models | 
| EU AI Act | 2024-2026 phase-in | Binding law | Financial penalties for violations | All AI systems marketed or used in the EU | 

The decision rule that follows from the table: judge a framework by its enforcement column, not its scope column. On that measure the accord sits at the weakest end, and any company citing it as evidence of safety practice is citing a commitment that no outside body can check.

## Gaps in enforcement and disclosure, and the November 28 test

CNBC’s follow-up coverage ran under a headline saying AI safety was left in a more chaotic state after the meeting, reflecting skepticism toward a voluntary pact as a substitute for binding requirements. Three gaps explain why. Nothing requires the six companies to publish audit results, so the public cannot verify follow-through. No government agency is given authority to investigate complaints or demand evidence of compliance. And the accord defines no consequence for a company that signs and later drops its commitments.

The rollout also had a small embarrassment: TechCrunch reported that the signed pledge misspells “United States” in its own text. It is minor next to the substance, but it drew attention because the accord was presented as a carefully negotiated document.

Our read, based on the accord’s structure rather than any confirmed outcome: the November 28 deadline will show whether this becomes a policy turning point or mostly a rebranding with a press event attached. Watch for three things. First, whether the definition arrives with any enforcement language. Second, whether any signatory discloses audit findings or auditors. Third, whether a serious safety incident at a signatory shifts the debate, since the lack of penalties would then become the main story. Anyone relying on the pledge as a safety signal should treat it as a statement of intent until one of those happens.
