Scope. This dossier examines NATO, the European Union, Italy, France, Germany and the United Kingdom from 2024 to 18 November 2026, assessing how competition among advanced AI models could change military cyber defence, cyberterrorism and cognitive warfare through November 2031.

Executive Summary / BLUF

AI’s clearest near-term military value is faster, verified cyber defence. It can accelerate software review and incident analysis. The operational advantage depends on whether teams can validate findings, close exposures and restore services faster under pressure.

OpenAI reported on 17 November 2026 that NATO had used up to 300 billion raw input and output tokens per month under its work with the NATO Communications and Information Agency. The provider’s figure measures usage. The public announcement provides no corresponding measure of security outcomes, cost or sustained operational readiness. Crossed Swords 2026 brought together more than 250 participants from over 40 NATO and non-NATO countries in Tallinn on 2–6 November. The exercise used both on-premises AI and Daybreak and introduced an AI-agent training stream with human oversight. It establishes exercise use. Operational effectiveness remains unquantified in the organiser’s account. Exercise Crossed Swords tests cyber operations and multi-domain coordination — NATO CCDCOE — Nov 2026. ccdcoe.org

The threat is uneven. The strongest documented terrorism-related use concerns propaganda and hate speech. In foreign information manipulation, the EEAS detected AI-related activity in 147 of 540 incidents in 2025, while reporting low organic engagement for much of the synthetic material it observed. The procurement decision is whether gains survive operational constraints. Wider deployment should depend on measured improvements in remediation, recovery and decision quality under degraded conditions, with auditable human authority and a tested fallback.

On 17 November 2026, OpenAI disclosed the scale of NATO’s use of its models for cyber defence through the NATO Communications and Information Agency. The same day, EU defence ministers endorsed a secure European defence cloud as a candidate for joint development. The two decisions expose a choice that defence budgets will have to resolve. The Alliance can acquire powerful models quickly, but its military advantage depends on whether national authorities can turn their findings into verified action when a network is degraded, a supplier is unavailable or an ally needs evidence it is authorised to use. That requirement puts procurement, industrial capacity and diplomatic trust on the same balance sheet. Buying model access is the simplest transaction. Paying for the ability to act on its output is the harder commitment.

The figures show where the money must go

OpenAI reported that NATO had used up to 300 billion raw input and output tokens per month in the work with the NATO Communications and Information Agency. The November disclosure establishes a provider-reported peak. It supplies no corresponding figure for expenditure, verified fixes or time saved. The United Kingdom’s October 2026 announcement comes closer to an operational measure. The government said two pilots had helped identify and fix more than 1,000 potential vulnerabilities across government systems. Its published account describes departments validating and prioritising the findings through existing procedures. It leaves open how the work compared with ordinary practice by severity, review time and time to closure. Poland provides a different form of evidence. In September 2026, CERT Polska identified the models used in research behind a published security advisory and directed operators to available vendor fixes. That traceable chain from research to remediation is a better basis for procurement than the volume of computation alone.

The infrastructure commitments show how quickly the surrounding costs can exceed the apparent price of access. NATO confirmed in July 2026 that its communications and information agency had signed the Protected Business Network contract. Accenture estimated its value at approximately €200 million over seven years and said the platform it would deliver with Leonardo was intended to support secure cloud services for approximately 29,000 users across NATO. The United Kingdom separately allocated £100 million through its Defence Investment Plan to the Rapid AI Delivery Taskforce, whose remit covers AI, autonomy and other frontier technologies. These figures buy different things. The NATO contract concerns the classified digital infrastructure on which future services can run. The UK allocation supports adoption across military commands. The budget question is whether each investment produces a defensive task that an operator can complete and verify, with an identifiable cost for keeping that capability available.

A faster finding can produce a longer queue

At Crossed Swords 2026, the NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence placed Daybreak and on-premises AI in the exercise environment and introduced a dedicated training stream on AI agents. The exercise put the technology alongside the people who would have to assess its output and coordinate action under time pressure. This is where an apparent gain can reverse. A model can shorten the time needed to identify a possible exposure while increasing the number of cases that require specialist review. If a service owner has limited maintenance windows or lacks authority to make a change, the extra findings accumulate. Procurement should therefore measure the interval from a validated finding to a verified resolution, as well as the interval from first report to validation. The second becomes an advantage only when the first remains manageable and essential services stay available.

National control solves different problems in different countries

France’s August 2026 military programming update places ASGARD, its classified AI supercomputer, within a national investment trajectory that includes renewal of the system. Germany’s Open Defense Cloud addresses a different constraint. BWI says the open-source-based platform is designed to be deployable and to handle classified data, with initial applications for a brigade command post scheduled for 2027. France’s investment gives it an environment for sensitive workloads. Germany’s programme makes the availability of useful data and applications during impaired connectivity a testable requirement. Italy faces a different implementation question. Its 2026–2028 defence planning direction calls for coordination with the National Cybersecurity Agency on crisis management for the defence of the state. In an incident affecting an infrastructure operator and military users, the test is how quickly each authority can verify the facts it needs and identify who has the power to act. Britain’s funded delivery taskforce can contribute a fourth form of evidence by establishing which results from government pilots transfer to military environments with different permissions and service constraints.

Those choices can reinforce one another. The Alliance Digital Strategy, published in January 2026, calls for federated infrastructure and common approaches to data labelling, metadata and access control. Its most valuable implication is that authorised results should be usable across different systems. A French assessment can remain useful to a partner even when the underlying classified material stays in France, provided the recipient can establish its provenance, understand its limits and act within its own authority. A German formation can operate through a loss of connectivity if it retains the information and authority needed for its priority tasks. The protected infrastructure around the model matters as much as the model in both cases. A procurement decision that preserves the ability to retrieve evidence and reconstruct an authorised action gives governments room to replace a supplier or change the way a mission is supported.

Faster response still requires separate legal judgments

Europol’s European Union Terrorism Situation and Trend Report 2026 documents the use of generated and edited media by terrorists and violent extremists. Its attack and arrest series does not isolate AI’s contribution to the disruption of critical systems. That evidentiary limit matters when a serious outage coincides with a claim of responsibility or persuasive synthetic material. Under Directive (EU) 2017/541, specified forms of intentional illegal system or data interference can qualify as terrorist offences when the relevant statutory conditions, including a specified terrorist purpose, are met. An operator can begin restoration as soon as the effects are verified. The competent authorities still need evidence of the conduct and purpose before classifying it. The distinction protects response time and the integrity of any later prosecution.

The same discipline applies to foreign information manipulation. In March 2026, the European External Action Service reported AI-related activity in 147 of the 540 incidents it detected in 2025, while observing limited organic engagement for much of the synthetic material it examined. A crisis responder has a narrower concern than a public-opinion analyst. A disputed instruction can matter if it reaches the people responsible for an emergency decision and consumes the time they have to verify it. The relevant evidence is a chronology linking the message, its authentication and the resulting decision. Articles 4 and 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty leave room for consultation and collective defence under distinct conditions. An authenticated instruction can be issued while responsibility remains under investigation, preserving the ability to respond without getting ahead of the evidence required for escalation.

Europe can agree on the handover before it agrees on the cloud

The 2026 Coordinated Annual Review on Defence, approved on 17 November, identified a European secure defence cloud as an opportunity for cooperation. The most useful early deliverable would be an agreed way to hand over a verified incident. The June 2026 EU–NATO progress report already documents crisis contacts and technical cooperation. The EU–UK Security and Defence Partnership provides a separate route for cooperation on cyber and foreign information manipulation. These channels can be tested against a concrete dependency. ENISA’s Cyber Europe 2026 exercise used interconnected rail and maritime networks to examine consequences for essential services. A more demanding follow-on test would trace whether the civilian operator, the national defence authority and the relevant international counterpart can agree on the service’s condition, accept responsibility for their respective decisions and act within a common operational window. That result would tell ministers which aspects of federation merit funding before a common platform is available.

The next two budget cycles will reveal who carries the risk

Over the next 12–24 months, Germany’s published 2027 Open Defense Cloud milestone and the follow-through to the November European defence review offer two points at which to test the choice governments have made. They have backed national delivery and endorsed wider cooperation. The immediate consequence should be measurable in the time it takes an ally to accept a verified finding, a service owner to close an exposure and a duty team to issue an authenticated instruction when ordinary communications are impaired. Renewal and expansion decisions should rest on those results, including performance when a shared dependency fails.

If governments fund access and defer that work, the cost will fall first on the operators whose services Allied plans assume will be available. Civilian infrastructure providers will carry more of the recovery burden, defence teams will spend scarce time reconciling evidence and authorisations, and finance ministries will face the cost of adapting systems after contracts are in place. The return on the November decisions will become visible when a priority service is restored or a protective action is completed within the time the mission allows, with a record an ally can verify.

The military advantage depends on how quickly defenders can close a verified exposure

NATO’s early use of advanced models marks a concrete step in the adoption of AI for cyber defence. OpenAI says it works with the NATO Communications and Information Agency (NCIA) to provide access to NATO’s core institutions, and the exercise organiser independently confirms that Daybreak was used at Crossed Swords alongside on-premises AI. Competition among model providers makes switching a readiness issue

Anthropic’s Project Glasswing, announced in April 2026, and its October expansion of tiered access for qualifying security professionals illustrate how access to advanced cyber capabilities is evolving alongside model performance. OpenAI’s announcement quotes NCIA General Manager Dylan Browne linking usage evidence to cost optimisation and the evaluation of competing solutions. Together, these statements point to a procurement choice between adopting a provider’s integrated workflow quickly and retaining the capacity to switch models without losing logs, permissions or continuity of service. Supporting more than one provider requires additional validation and engineering. It reduces concentration risk only if the alternatives have been tested and can operate through the same disruption. NATO’s revised AI strategy supplies a basis for that approach through its emphasis on traceability, reliability, governability and evaluation. For high-consequence workflows, a repeatable test after a model update or provider outage is more informative than a model ranking at the time of purchase. Cyberterrorism and cognitive warfare operate through different mechanisms

Europol documented terrorist and violent-extremist use of generative AI for propaganda and hate speech in its assessment of 2024. ENISA’s assessment of calendar 2025 identifies ransomware, state-linked activity and hacktivist denial-of-service campaigns as distinct parts of the cyber threat landscape. A serious cyber incident can therefore warrant an immediate restoration effort while the actor and motive remain under investigation. Under Article 3 of Directive (EU) 2017/541, specified intentional acts of unlawful system or data interference can qualify as terrorist offences where the applicable statutory conditions are met, including the serious-harm threshold and a specified terrorist purpose. NATO’s Article 5 assessment concerns a separate question. The Alliance assesses case by case whether a significant cyber or hybrid attack amounts to an armed attack. The use of AI or a public claim of responsibility establishes neither the purpose required by the directive nor the effects relevant to collective defence. Chapter 1. NATO’s Adoption Baseline and the Evidence from Daybreak and Crossed Swords

NATO now has a public record of advanced AI access and use in cyber exercises. The next readiness decision is whether AI-assisted teams can complete more verified defensive work within the same operational window, while preserving service continuity and clear authority to act. The distinction matters because the Alliance has three forms of evidence in view. OpenAI’s November announcement describes access through the NATO Communications and Information Agency (NCIA) and reports peak consumption. The NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence (CCDCOE) independently documents use of Daybreak and on-premises AI in an exercise environment. A national responder, CERT Polska, separately documents model involvement in research that led to an actual vulnerability advisory and vendor fixes. These records illuminate different stages of adoption. A usage figure establishes the scale claimed by the provider, an exercise establishes integration into training, and a published advisory establishes a real defensive outcome in a specific case. None supplies a matched comparison of NATO teams operating with and without AI. The case for widening deployment therefore rests on collecting that comparison at the level of the defensive workflow. NATO already has a timetable for moving from demand to integration

The Rapid Adoption Action Plan, endorsed by Allied leaders on 25 June 2025, gives NATO and its members a practical framework for evaluating commercial AI without allowing a single supplier’s product cycle to dictate the timetable. The plan generally aims for 24 months from identification of a need through acquisition and integration into Allied armed forces. It envisages around three months for market research and, once potential solutions have been identified, generally 12 months for incremental testing, evaluation, verification, validation and integration. The figures are policy objectives with distinct starting points. The plan gives no basis for adding them into a contractual deadline or assuming that a subsequent model release inherits an earlier version’s acceptance. For cyber defence, the implication is that procurement can move at different speeds for different levels of authority. A tool that organises an approved body of evidence can enter a bounded trial relatively quickly. A tool that informs a consequential change on a live system requires evidence about what happens when the input is incomplete, the recommendation is wrong or the service must continue through a failed change. The same procurement can therefore advance quickly while reserving wider operational use for a later decision. Summary of NATO’s Rapid Adoption Action Plan, paragraphs 8–9 — NATO — June 2025. NATO Official text

The Alliance has facilities for collecting part of that evidence before a product reaches a live network. In its July 2026 account, NATO described the Defence Innovation Accelerator for the North Atlantic (DIANA) as having 16 affiliated accelerator sites and more than 200 test centres. NATO identifies those test centres as laboratories, testbeds and field environments available for testing and validation by selected innovators. Their value for Allied cyber adoption would come from testing a narrowly defined defensive task against a common evidence standard, then establishing which parts of the result transfer to a second organisation. A tool that performs well with complete inventories, modern telemetry and experienced reviewers may offer less benefit where asset records are incomplete or teams lack authority to remediate. A useful trial would distinguish limitations of the model from limitations of the environment by recording what information was available, which operator checked the recommendation and where the workflow stopped. The test network provides venues for such work. Acceptance for a particular mission environment still depends on that environment’s data, permissions, service dependencies and accountable authority. Crossed Swords and Locked Shields expose different readiness questions

Crossed Swords is most informative about how AI fits into an operational chain. The organiser’s account places the new AI-agent training stream alongside established work on coordination between military command elements, technical specialists and civilian partners concerned with critical infrastructure. That structure makes authority and timing central to the assessment. An exercise can establish that operators received and examined an AI-assisted recommendation, then ask whether it arrived while they still had time to verify it and whether the designated authority could act on it. A separate exercise offers a different vantage point. CCDCOE reported more than 4,000 participants from 41 nations at Locked Shields 2026, which concluded on 24 April and focused on protecting critical infrastructure and military systems under simulated pressure. Its size illustrates the breadth of the environment in which civilian and military teams can practise continuity and coordination. Participation in either exercise is a measure of scale, and the exercises have different designs. Their complementary value lies in testing distinct questions. Crossed Swords can reveal where an AI-supported recommendation fits into a time-sensitive coordination process. A continuity exercise can reveal whether essential services remain supportable when analysts lose that assistance or have to reconcile conflicting information. The CERT Polska advisory improves the public evidence in a different way. In September 2026, the national responder named the researcher and the OpenAI models used in the work, published its assessment of the affected product and directed operators to available vendor fixes. The institutional significance is the existence of a documented path from AI-assisted research to an accountable public advisory and a remediation decision by the operator. Key judgments. The strongest near-term case is for AI assistance at points where the output can be checked against independent evidence, the responsible operator can reject it and the service owner can complete and verify the action. A configuration that generates more findings without adding capacity to review or resolve them can make prioritisation harder. The right unit of comparison is a completed defensive task under defined conditions. This gives decision-makers a common basis for judging different tools even where national systems, clearance rules or service dependencies differ.

What would change the assessment. An exercise report showing repeatable improvements in verified remediation and service restoration, with unchanged or lower rates of harmful recommendations and unnecessary escalations, would justify greater confidence. If gains consistently disappear when information is incomplete, an approval is delayed or the primary service is unavailable, the evidence favours retaining AI in advisory roles for those workflows.

Chapter 2. Model Competition, Defence Procurement and Resilience to Provider Failure

The most consequential procurement decision is which parts of the defensive workflow NATO and its members can carry across providers and which parts must remain usable when every approved model is unavailable. The Alliance Digital Strategy, published in January 2026, sets the institutional context for that decision. It calls for federated platforms, a hybrid cloud model spanning multiple classification levels, and common approaches to data labelling, metadata and access control. Applied to defensive AI, these principles imply that a supplier’s interface should sit inside an institutional workflow whose underlying evidence, permissions and decision records remain intelligible to the people responsible for the mission. If a service changes its model, an analyst must still be able to identify the evidence available at the time, who signed off and which systems were affected. If the service becomes unavailable, the operations team still needs the approved record and a way to act on it. Passing the same prompt to a second provider proves little if the alternative lacks access to the authorised evidence or loses the ability to reconstruct the prior decision. The Protected Business Network shows the scale of the enabling infrastructure

On 7 July 2026, NATO confirmed that NCIA had signed a contract with Accenture for the Protected Business Network (PBN), describing it as a foundation for classified digital operations across NATO. Accenture estimated the contract at approximately €200 million over seven years and stated that it and Leonardo would design, implement and operate the core platform across a multi-cloud environment provided by NCIA, supporting the progressive adoption of secure cloud services by approximately 29,000 users. The contract value is a supplier estimate and the user figure is prospective. They describe the infrastructure programme and tell decision-makers nothing directly about expenditure on Daybreak, the number of AI users or which models are approved for classified workloads. The relevance of the award lies in how much military AI depends on the infrastructure around it. A model can be replaced more readily when evidence remains retrievable, access controls travel with the workflow and operator tools preserve a common record. Conversely, a programme can use several clouds while retaining a common point of failure in identity, networking or evidence storage. Competition extends to access terms, updates and exit costs

In October 2026 Anthropic announced three access tiers in its expanded Cyber Verification Program and said existing Glasswing members would move to the specialised tier. The commercial significance is that a buyer’s capacity can depend on continued eligibility and the conditions attached to a particular service, in addition to its technical performance. A tender should therefore ask what happens if a model is withdrawn, a capability is restricted, a version changes or the buyer needs to move approved work to another environment. The UK government’s cloud guidance recognises the potential operating benefits of managed services and recommends monitoring where switching costs grow disproportionately. The UK National Cyber Security Centre makes the complementary point that changes to data, models or prompts can alter system behaviour and that operational teams need testing and monitoring around updates. Together, these sources support treating material model changes as acceptance events for the affected workflow. Where a supplier offers versioned access, the operator can compare the replacement against an approved baseline. Where the supplier controls the release schedule, a tested suspension and fallback procedure becomes more valuable because the operator has less control over exactly when behaviour changes. A procurement evaluation should put the strongest model and the best operating arrangement through the same mission test. First, establish the value delivered by the current workflow with the authorised data, operator time and approval chain actually available. Then evaluate competing arrangements against the same task and evidence, including a period in which the primary service is deliberately unavailable. Record both the cost of running the workflow and the cost of keeping it ready. A model with inexpensive inference may require extensive human reconciliation. A second commercially hosted model may offer excellent everyday switchability while relying on the same identity or connectivity layer. A locally operated service may offer more control over when it is updated while increasing the staffing, assurance and infrastructure burden. For the most critical tasks, a manual workflow can preserve authority and access to evidence through broader failure, with less throughput. The commercial comparison should therefore price human review, integration, assurance and rehearsed transition alongside service consumption. No public NATO workload or contract breakdown reviewed for this chapter supports a monetary estimate for those costs. The measures that should govern renewal

A contract renewal should turn on whether the arrangement improves completed defensive work at a sustainable cost and continues to support the mission during disruption. Chapter 3. Terrorist Use of AI, Cyber Disruption and the Threshold for Terrorism

The clearest documented effect of generative AI on terrorism is its use in producing and adapting propaganda. The more consequential proposition, that AI is materially increasing terrorist disruption of critical systems, remains unproven in the public evidence reviewed. Europol’s 2026 assessment documents terrorist and violent-extremist use of image, audio and video generation in the online environment. Its aggregate statistics on attacks and arrests do not isolate the operational contribution of AI. The distinction matters for resource allocation. Investigators need to determine whether a tool was present, whether it materially assisted the conduct under investigation and whether the conduct meets the elements of a terrorism offence. An infrastructure operator has a different immediate responsibility. It must establish what services are impaired, safeguard continuity and preserve evidence while motive remains unresolved. A government can therefore act rapidly on a confirmed disruption while reserving judgment on the actor, the role of AI and the legal classification. This sequencing becomes particularly important when an online claim of responsibility circulates before the operational facts are established. European Union Terrorism Situation and Trend Report 2026, pp. 10–12 and 17–19 — Europol — July 2026. europol.europa.eu

The terrorism figures establish the baseline for assessing AI’s contribution

Europol recorded 45 reported terrorist attacks across 10 EU Member States in 2025, comprising 22 completed, 20 foiled and three failed attacks. Completed attacks caused six fatalities. In 2024, 14 Member States reported 58 attacks, of which 34 were completed, causing five fatalities. Arrests moved in the other direction. Europol reports 486 suspects arrested for terrorism-related offences in 21 Member States in 2025, against 449 in 2024 and 426 in 2023. Calculated from the published counts, reported attacks fell 22.4% from 2024 to 2025, while recorded arrests rose 8.2%, both rounded to one decimal place. These changes describe different populations and processes. An arrest can concern an offence or investigation outside the reported attack series. The count of foiled attacks also depends on which plots authorities detect, investigate and report. The figures therefore cannot establish a common rate of attack prevention or show whether AI made more plots possible. A credible estimate of AI’s contribution would require case-level evidence connecting its use to a defined step in the offence, with a clear account of the other resources available to the actor. European Union Terrorism Situation and Trend Report 2026, p. 18 — Europol — July 2026. europol.europa.eu

The strongest evidence of AI use lies further upstream. Europol describes synthetic and edited material circulating in extremist environments and observes that a substantial part of what it saw consisted of hate speech or material that was offensive but lawful, alongside content that warranted a terrorism assessment. The United Nations Office of Counter-Terrorism documents examples including a synthetic news presenter used to disseminate Da’esh propaganda and AI-generated imagery used to reinforce a far-right conspiracy narrative. Both cases show how generative tools can lower the cost of producing material in formats an audience already uses. Their downstream effect depends on whether real audiences see the material, whether it changes behaviour and whether any resulting conduct crosses a legal threshold. This distinction also applies to platform response. Europol reports that competent national authorities and Europol assessed 81,862 pieces of online content under its terrorism heading in 2025, leading to 61,412 referrals to 99 hosting service providers. These are assessments and referrals within a defined institutional workflow. They provide no count of synthetic items, unique viewers, completed removals or people recruited. Treating any one of those outcomes as equivalent to the volume of referrals would obscure where a counter-terrorism intervention had an actual effect. The legal threshold depends on the act, its gravity and its purpose

Article 3 of Directive (EU) 2017/541 provides the relevant EU criminal-law framework, implemented through national law. Its opening test covers intentional acts defined as offences under national law that, by their nature or context, may seriously damage a country or an international organisation, when committed for one of three specified purposes. Those purposes are serious intimidation of a population, undue compulsion of a government or international organisation, or serious destabilisation or destruction of fundamental political, constitutional, economic or social structures. For illegal system interference, Article 3(1)(i) refers to the circumstances in Article 9(3) or Article 9(4)(b) or (c) of Directive 2013/40/EU. These cover a significant number of affected systems in the specified tool-related circumstances, serious damage, or conduct against a critical-infrastructure information system. For illegal data interference under the same terrorism provision, the cross-reference is narrower and covers the critical-infrastructure circumstance in Article 9(4)(c). The cross-referenced circumstance is only part of the test. A serious outage at a critical operator supplies an urgent basis for response and may satisfy one relevant condition. A terrorism determination still requires evidence of the applicable offence and statutory purpose. The use of an AI model does not change those elements. Online content presents a related but separate legal question. Article 5 of Directive (EU) 2017/541 addresses public provocation to commit a terrorist offence. It requires, among other elements, intent to incite and conduct that creates a danger that one or more of the relevant offences may be committed. Regulation (EU) 2021/784 separately establishes the removal-order framework for terrorist content online, including the one-hour time limit after a hosting service provider receives a competent authority’s removal order. That procedural deadline concerns action on the order. Europol’s referrals statistic measures a different administrative activity. For the analyst, the useful distinction is between provenance, purpose and unlawful conduct. A synthetic-media assessment can help determine how a recording was produced. The text, surrounding conduct and legal context bear on whether publication meets the applicable threshold. Separating these judgments gives authorities room to act promptly on a lawful order while retaining the evidence required for review and ensuring that the presence of AI does not displace the substantive test. Key judgments. AI already affects the production and adaptation of extremist media. Its contribution to serious terrorist cyber disruption remains a case-level question. The circumstances of a disruption, evidence of unlawful conduct and evidence of a specified terrorist purpose should each receive a separate finding. Technical responders can protect services and preserve evidence while investigators resolve the latter two questions.

What would change the assessment. A documented investigation or judgment establishing both a qualifying cyber offence and a material AI contribution would strengthen the case for treating AI-assisted terrorist disruption as a demonstrated operational threat. Several comparable findings would allow policymakers to assess whether the contribution recurs across actors, sectors or types of incident.

Open official record. Europol’s public series provides terrorism counts and qualitative findings on AI use. The decisive collection gap is a case-level record that identifies the contribution of AI, the outcome it helped produce and the competent authority’s assessment of the offence and purpose. Chapter 4. Foreign Information Manipulation, Decision Integrity and Crisis Escalation

AI’s most consequential effect in cognitive warfare may be to consume the time available to verify evidence and issue instructions during a real crisis. An information operation can matter even when it attracts little sustained public engagement, provided it interferes with a narrow audience that must make a consequential decision within a short window. That pathway remains an analytical judgment. The European External Action Service (EEAS) documents the infrastructure of foreign information manipulation and interference, including coordinated activity across channels and the use of synthetic content. It also reports that much of the AI-generated material it observed in 2025 attracted little organic engagement. Those findings place a limit on claims of broad persuasion while leaving a narrower operational question open. Did an incident commander, public authority or infrastructure operator take longer to establish which instruction was genuine, and did that interval affect the response? The answer requires time-stamped operational evidence. An impression count cannot show when an authorised decision-maker learned of a claim, what evidence was available at that moment or whether an independently authenticated instruction would have changed the sequence of events. 4th EEAS Report on Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference Threats — EEAS — March 2026. The EEAS evidence reveals infrastructure and the limits of attribution

The EEAS identified 10,500 unique channels involved in the foreign information manipulation incidents it detected in 2025. These included websites, social media accounts and blogs. Its network visualisations drew on approximately 3,000 of the most recurrent assets. Within that selected subset, the EEAS reported 9.5% as directly related to a state actor through official or state-controlled channels and 90.5% as covert assets linked to or aligned with a threat actor under its classification. The sample was selected for recurrence, so those proportions should remain attached to the mapped subset. The report also states that 65% of recorded incidents remained unattributed, even though it identified indicators of coordination with assets linked to Russian or Chinese attributed infrastructures. This is a substantive distinction for a crisis cell. The identity of a channel, its relationship to another account and the responsibility of a state or organisation for the overall incident are separate findings. Evidence for one can increase confidence in another, but it cannot by itself establish who authorised the operation. Crisis decisions require separate thresholds for authentication, attribution and escalation

The most useful crisis record would capture when the disputed material first reached the responsible decision-maker, when the underlying event was verified, when an authoritative instruction was issued and what delay, if any, the false material imposed. Authentication can proceed while attribution remains open. A service owner can confirm the actual operating condition and an authorised spokesperson can communicate verified instructions even while investigators assess the origin of a disputed recording. The EU AI Act addresses a different part of the problem through Article 50(4), which sets disclosure requirements for deployers who generate or manipulate image, audio or video constituting a deep fake, subject to the provision’s qualifications. A disclosure duty can improve transparency where it applies. In an actual crisis, the decision-maker still needs an independently authenticated instruction and a record of who issued it. Absence of a label establishes neither authenticity nor attribution. The operational metric is whether the responsible person obtained trustworthy evidence in time to make the required decision. Chapter 6. Five-Year Outlook, Indicators, Courses of Action and Net Assessment

Through November 2031, the central outlook is uneven defensive improvement. Some institutions can convert better models into verified remediation, faster restoration and more reliable communications. Others will see the same model capabilities increase the number of cases their existing staff and suppliers must handle. The divergence is likely where access improves faster than authorisation, maintenance and cross-border coordination. The fiscal setting creates room to act, but the connection must be demonstrated at programme level. EDA reports that defence spending across the EU-27 represented 2.2% of GDP in 2025 and projects 2.4% in 2026. Defence investment represented about 32% of defence spending in 2025 and is projected to reach 36% in 2026. These shares describe overall spending and its composition. They identify fiscal space and competing demands, with no breakdown of resources available for defensive AI. The same November review describes a continuing gap between stronger national planning and collective delivery. For 2031, the point is that buying compute and services is only one investment. Three decisions can be made before the long-term outcome is known

The first is to establish a joint acceptance standard for a small set of defensive tasks. An exercise design should specify the same evidence and service target for each participating authority, including the point at which the receiving official can accept responsibility for the case. The second is to fund continuity across vulnerable dependencies. For a mission relying on rail, maritime, communications or other civilian services, the responsible authorities should choose the dependency whose failure would most constrain the response and test it directly. The third is to establish a joint decision chronology for compound incidents. Service operators, cyber authorities and authorised communicators need a common record of when the underlying event was verified, when a disputed instruction was authenticated and when the accountable authority issued a decision. The proposed chronology does not require pooling all underlying evidence. It requires reference points that each actor is authorised to share and that can be matched in an after-action review. Together, these decisions support an auditable investment rule. Widen a workflow when it produces verified improvement in the receiving organisation as well as the originating one. Repair the handover, approval or continuity dependency when the gain disappears at the boundary. The most consequential weakness is a dependency shared by a primary system and its nominal alternative. Final net assessment

By November 2031, advanced AI should make a material contribution to Allied cyber defence where institutions use it to shorten verified remediation, maintain service continuity and exchange findings that partners can act on. The magnitude of that contribution remains conditional on the capacity of operators and authorities to complete the work once a model has produced an assessment. In terrorism, the established contribution is strongest in media production and adaptation. The claim that AI is increasing serious terrorist cyber disruption requires case-level evidence of conduct, purpose and consequence. In cognitive warfare, the most significant operational risk is that disputed information consumes time needed for an authorised response during an already serious event. For Europe, the decision that connects all three domains is to fund the ability to verify evidence, assign authority and continue operating under disruption. A federation that can preserve those functions across nationally controlled systems will gain more from successive improvements in models and will be better placed to absorb a provider or communications failure. The strongest proof of readiness will be an accepted cross-border decision, protective action completed within its required window, and a record that shows exactly how it happened. 2026 Coordinated Annual Review on Defence Report — European Defence Agency — November 2026.
