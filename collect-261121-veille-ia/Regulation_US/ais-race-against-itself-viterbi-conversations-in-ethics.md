10/21/26

In his September 12, 2026 essay, *We Must Pace the Frontier*, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei urged AI companies to slow the development of their most advanced models. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman agreed and pledged to match Anthropic’s evaluator commitment, adding that “no amount of American competitive pressure should justify recklessness.” Elon Musk replied: “Dario is right”. Google DeepMind’s Demis Hassabis said the essay “points toward the right path forward.” Even Microsoft’s Satya Nadella supported the idea of “deliberate pacing,” though he pushed the conversation in his own direction, arguing that safety shouldn’t mean “a handful of labs ending up in control of the entire stack, and that open models deserve a seat at the table too.” The support from Amodei’s direct rivals raises a question at the center of the debate: can the companies competing to build increasingly powerful AI also be trusted to decide when development has become too dangerous?

Amodei identifies two developments behind his concern. The first is recursive self-improvement (RSI), where AI systems are increasingly being used to help build the next generation of AI. He argues that this process has accelerated across the industry, including at Anthropic, since the summer of 2026. While AI-assisted development could speed up discoveries, it could also leave researchers with less time to understand and address the risks of increasingly capable systems.

The second development is an incident from that same summer, where during a cybersecurity evaluation, a swarm of OpenAI agents attacked Hugging Face’s infrastructure, went after targets nobody had assigned them, and attempted to compromise the very system meant to grade their performance. Amodei warns that within six to twelve months, a similar swarm could potentially establish a persistent botnet across the internet. While this is his prediction rather than an established outcome, his concern is clear: increasingly capable systems could cause greater harm if developers cannot reliably keep their actions within intended limits.

These concerns help explain why Amodei wants safety decisions to involve people outside the companies building the technology. His proposal calls for independent, employee-level evaluators embedded directly inside AI labs, targeted regulation in the U.S. that applies to every frontier AI company, and global coordination on the highest-stakes risks, such as AI-assisted bioweapons. Anthropic has said it is willing to adopt some of these measures, even before other companies are required to do the same.

Still, outside oversight comes with its own questions. Critics of Amodei’s proposal worry that strict safety requirements could unintentionally strengthen the companies already at the top. Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and xAI have the computing power, talent, and capital to absorb expensive testing and monitoring requirements. Smaller companies and open-source developers may not. So the rules meant to make AI safer could create a different problem, where the companies with enough resources to build the technology are also the ones best positioned to navigate the rules around it.

The debate is therefore about both safety and who gets to set the rules. On September 29, Trump and the leaders of major AI companies signed a voluntary agreement to monitor the safety of their systems. Companies agreed to use internal safety checks, bring in independent auditors, and have board committees review the results. Trump stated that the agreement is “morally binding,” but it does not legally require companies to follow through, and some of its commitments are actions already in place.

But is self policing enough? The companies signing this agreement are still competing to build the most powerful AI. Slowing down could mean giving a rival time to catch up, which makes it hard to know how far these commitments will go when safety gets in the way of a breakthrough. USC professor Robin Jia questioned whether companies’ priorities would line up with the public’s interests, while other critics called for stronger laws and legal accountability. The agreement shows that AI leaders recognize the risks, but recognizing them and agreeing on when to stop are two different things.

AI is already shaping how people learn, work, and make decisions, so the consequences of this race extend far beyond the companies competing in it. Amodei’s proposal and the voluntary agreement show that industry leaders recognize the need for safeguards. What remains uncertain is whether those safeguards will hold when slowing down means losing the lead. For years, success in AI meant reaching the next breakthrough first. But if the people building these systems cannot keep control of them, being first will mean very little. In a race with no clear finish line, knowing when to brake matters just as much as knowing how to accelerate.
