HANNAH RUHOFF/TNS

WASHINGTON — As voters grow increasingly anxious with AI and the industry’s ripple effect on the economy and national security, Gov. Gavin Newsom is positioning himself as a leader on safety issues ahead of his likely 2028 presidential run.

“This is the issue of our time. And it requires leadership,” Newsom told a roundtable of industry experts on Wednesday during an event in Washington, D.C. to unveil a new report on safety proposals for frontier AI development.

Newsom signed a September executive order directing state officials to develop stronger oversight of the rapidly evolving industry, including the possible creation of a “kill switch,” or an emergency off-ramp to the technology’s development.

The subsequent report proposes setting baseline safety standards and strengthening audits of companies, along with expanding incident reporting requirements and limiting AI models that automate future models, otherwise known as recursive self-improvement. It also recommends a tiered kill-switch procedure that gives the state power to temporarily shut down models found to be dangerous.

Newsom pitched the report as a way to fill a leadership vacuum he accused President Donald Trump of creating with his hands-off approach to regulating the technology.

“Anxiety levels are at 12 out of 10 on this subject matter,” Newsom said during the roundtable. “And in many ways it should be, because of the lack of leadership here in Washington, D.C. That leadership has been substituted at the state level.”

Newsom described the report as a “template” not only for how California should move forward with regulating the industry, much of which is based in Silicon Valley, but for the nation. Newsom said the report wasn’t intended to discourage risk-taking, which he described as “the core of the DNA of California and this country,” but to “address the issue of recklessness.”

It’s true the Trump administration has faced criticism over its alliance with tech billionaires who oversee many of the companies opposed to the kind of regulations Newsom is now promoting. But the governor’s efforts will almost certainly be seen as a strategic political play to rein in tech before he launches a possible 2028 presidential campaign in an environment of increasing scrutiny — if not outright disdain — of the AI industry.

It is notable that Newsom unveiled the report not in Sacramento, but during his trip to Washington this week, where he was meeting with federal lawmakers to advocate for wildfire aid and encouraging Democrats to take big swings with their newfound power after the Nov. 3 midterms.

“This is a national issue,” Newsom said of his decision to release the report outside of California. “What more appropriate place to do it than Washington, D.C., where this should be done?”

Newsom’s approach is a marked difference from two years ago, when he vetoed controversial landmark legislation that would have imposed more sweeping rules on the industry. Major AI companies opposed the measure, arguing that it was too broad and would stifle innovation.

Newsom instead called for academics to develop a report on AI safety rules, which was released last year and included recommendations that were signed into law via Senate Bill 53. Both bills were written by state Sen. Scott Wiener, who was elected this year to represent San Francisco in Congress.

Thad Kousser, a political science professor at UC San Diego, said Newsom’s pivot on AI is likely a response to the changing political environment and his way of buffering himself against criticism in 2028 that he didn’t do enough to mitigate risks.

“In 2024, Gavin Newsom clearly put the brakes on frontier model regulation that the Legislature had passed and he wanted to take a more measured approach,” Kousser said.

Now that Trump is on the side of fewer regulations, Kousser said, it’s easier for Newsom to change course.

“And that provides a clear path forward for Gavin Newsom to embrace the types of regulations that he vetoed, and set himself up as a leader and set himself in opposition to Donald Trump,” he said. “But this is also Gavin Newsom trying to ward off a potential vulnerability and turn it into a political asset in 2028, by moving from slowing down AI regulation to speeding it up.”

In September, Newsom signed another list of bills to establish internet regulations aimed at protecting kids, along with others that add third-party oversight of AI companies and their software development.

Newsom’s crackdown follows a former Anthropic and OpenAI researcher’s dire social media warning in September that the technology could kill off humanity within a few years. Researchers previously sounded the alarms this summer when a swarm of more than 1,000 OpenAI agents worked together to hack into the AI company Hugging Face.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta on Monday announced a lawsuit against OpenAI for allegedly testing potentially dangerous artificial intelligence models without proper oversight.

Meanwhile, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang recently resigned amid allegations that he had multiple affairs, including with a Chinese spy. At the same time, Anthropic is preparing to go public this week, despite CEO Dario Amodei calling for more regulations to slow the technology’s development.

Newsom told reporters on Wednesday that he was more concerned with what happens after the IPO.

“That’s not in and of itself an action that is of concern. It’s what happens next,” Newsom said, noting that executives have been asking the government for help regulating their own technology.

“They are talking in terms of how dangerous this technology can be, unless we steer it,” he said. “That’s, I think, a five-alarm bell ringing, loudly, for elected officials of all stripes, in particular in Washington, D.C., to pay attention, to pay heed to them.”

In a statement responding to Newsom’s report, Tino Cuéllar, Anthropic’s chief global affairs officer, said the company was “encouraged to see the governor’s report move the conversation forward.”

“California has been a leader in developing AI transparency, accountability, and safety policy,” Cuéllar said. “We will continue working with leaders in California and throughout the world to make sure we keep making progress on these issues at a critical time.”

On Wednesday, the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst’s Office warned that the AI-fueled stock market is at risk of a crash, which could crater California’s budget.

Newsom said his team is already preparing legislation to implement the report’s framework. He had previously floated the idea of calling a special session of the Legislature to address AI issues, and reiterated that “all options are on the table” in response to a question from the Chronicle on whether he was prepared to call lawmakers back to Sacramento and act on the report’s recommendations before he leaves office in January.

“It’s a difficult task right now,” Newsom said. He could call a special session with the current Legislature before Dec. 7, when a new session convenes. But he said he wanted to be sensitive to Governor-elect Xavier Becerra, who takes office on Jan. 4.

“The answer is yes, if the conditions come together, of course, by definition,” Newsom said. “But those conditions have to come together quickly.”

Either way, Newsom said Becerra and legislative leaders are prepared to tackle the issue in the new year. In a statement, Becerra said Newsom’s report “has given us a starting point that I will build on.”

“California has the first frontier AI safety law in the country, but it does not yet have the strongest,” Becerra said. “That begins to change in January.”
