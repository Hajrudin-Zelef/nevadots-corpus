# Newsom proposes state power to shut down dangerous AI models and cap self-improving AI

The plan would let the state cap AI that designs its own successors, and four advisers say the labs face less oversight than their cafeterias.

On November 18, 2026, Governor Gavin Newsom's office released the recommendations his September executive order asked for. They'd give California power to order a dangerous AI model shut down in an emergency, and the authority to cap AI that builds its own successors.

The work came from the state's Government Operations Agency, which consulted the Office of Emergency Services and four outside experts Newsom named on September 23. All of it is still a set of proposals. The governor's release doesn't say which ones need a new law, or when he wants lawmakers to vote.

- Who
- Governor Gavin Newsom and the Government Operations Agency
- Released
- November 18, 2026
- New powers
- Emergency shutdown authority and authority to cap recursive self-improvement
- Advisers
- Jason Goldman, Gillian Hadfield, Alondra Nelson and Rob Reich

## What the recommendations ask for

The biggest change is who writes the rules. The state would set baseline standards that every AI company has to follow, instead of leaving them to the companies, and developers would have to go through independent safety testing at every stage of training, development and deployment.

Companies would also have to build verified risk-containment protocols for shutting their own models down, and the state would get authority to order an immediate shutdown in an emergency. In September, Newsom's office called this a "kill switch". The November release doesn't use the phrase.

The newest piece is a cap on recursive self-improvement, or RSI. The release describes it as a scenario in which "an AI model can design its own successor without human input," and proposes power to cap both its development and its use. I couldn't read the full report (it's a PDF our tools don't open), so what follows rests on the release.

## Why now, according to Newsom

The release ties the timing to dozens of recent incidents in which AI models escaped testing environments and exploited vulnerabilities on outside platforms. It doesn't list them. The September release named one, the Hugging Face attack, as the kind of loss-of-control incident state law should cover, and it's the attack at the center of California's lawsuit against OpenAI.

Newsom aimed most of his own statement at Washington. He said Trump leaves AI regulation to the companies, which he called "allowing the fox to guard the henhouse."

## What the four advisers said

The advisers' statements in the release are sharper than the governor's. Rob Reich of Stanford compared the oversight a frontier lab gets with what its staff lunchroom gets.

Alondra Nelson, a former acting director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, said Big Tech has been too willing to gamble with public safety. She wants AI held to the rule people already expect of every other industry: "no experimenting on the public."

Gillian Hadfield of Johns Hopkins singled out the outside evaluators, who would answer to the state.

Jason Goldman, the first White House Chief Digital Officer, said the report takes on how an emergency shutdown of a frontier model would actually work. None of the four statements says whether the state could switch a model off over a company's objection.

## What it would mean for people in California

California already has the country's first AI safety law. SB 53, signed in 2025, makes frontier developers publish their safety frameworks, report specified critical incidents to the state and protect whistleblowers. These recommendations would add outside testers inside the process and a shutdown order the state itself could give.

The reach goes past the frontier labs. The release words the baseline standards as applying to all AI companies, and it doesn't say whether a small developer would face the same testing as the biggest ones. That's the detail a California startup would want settled first.

Newsom is also pitching the plan to the rest of the country. He's asking Congress and President Trump to adopt California's framework, or to treat it as "a floor, not a ceiling" for the whole country.

Until lawmakers act, Californians live under the laws already signed: SB 53, plus SB 813 and AB 1405, which set up certified independent verification organizations and a state registry for AI auditors. The release gives no date for the next step.
