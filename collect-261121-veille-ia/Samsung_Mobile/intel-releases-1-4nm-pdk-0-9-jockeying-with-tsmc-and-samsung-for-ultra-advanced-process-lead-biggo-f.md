Intel Foundry has fired the starting gun in the ultra-advanced foundry race by providing version 0.9 of its process design kit (PDK) for “Intel 14A,” its next-generation 1.4nm-class process, to select customers. Taiwan’s TSMC and South Korea’s Samsung Electronics are also aligning their development schedules with targets of 1.4nm volume production in 2028 and 2029, respectively, setting the stage for an even fiercer battle for technology leadership in the foundry market in the late 2020s.

A PDK is a kind of interface that bundles transistor models, design rules and verification data so that semiconductor designers can design chips tailored to a specific process. The significance of Intel’s 0.9 version is that it is based on actual silicon measurements rather than simulation. For customers, it provides confidence that the process will meet performance and power requirements, allowing them to begin product development.

Ben Sell, corporate vice president and general manager of front-end technology development at Intel Foundry, said, “From a customer standpoint, PDK 0.9 is about giving them the confidence that they can design products based on Intel 14A,” adding that “the process technology is reaching the level of performance and yield maturity the industry expects at this stage of development.” He added, “We changed our collaboration model with equipment and materials partners so that technology development leaders engage directly with suppliers from the early stages of process development.”

Intel 14A uses “RibbonFET 2,” a second-generation gate-all-around (GAA) transistor, the backside power delivery technology “PowerDirect,” and “Turbo Cell,” a high-performance standard cell that shortens critical timing paths. GAA is a structure in which the gate wraps around the channel to improve current control, while backside power delivery reduces wiring congestion and boosts power efficiency. Intel said it is applying manufacturing experience accumulated from running its 18A process for about 12 months to maturing 14A. It plans to increase investment in inline defect detection and modeling and simulation to catch problems early in development and improve yields and process variation.

The schedule is laid out in stages. Intel will provide PDK 1.0 to customers in the first quarter of next year, enter trial production in the second half of the same year, and push for a volume ramp in 2028. PDK 1.0 is a milestone that verifies that preparations for mass production are proceeding as planned, but it does not by itself mean the start of mass production.

Intel Foundry’s challenge is to convert development results into actual customer orders. Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan said on a recent earnings conference call that “we are making progress with multiple potential customers on 14A, and they are already actively engaging and collaborating.” However, Intel has not yet disclosed any customer that has committed to adopting 14A, any tape-out cases, or specific production contracts. Because recovering the cost of developing cutting-edge processes requires producing chips in the hundreds of millions, securing external customers is seen as a key variable that will determine the success or failure of Intel Foundry’s business.

Intel is also accelerating the expansion of its design ecosystem. It has formed a design technology co-optimization (DTCO) collaboration with Cadence aimed at next-generation AI-based design tools and intellectual property (IP), while Keysight, Siemens and Synopsys have completed certification of design tools for 14A. Ali Farhang, vice president and general manager of the design technology platform at Intel Foundry, said, “The next major milestone is Intel 14A PDK 1.0, scheduled for the first quarter of 2027,” adding, “We will build trust by keeping our commitments with a customer-first attitude.”

The competitive landscape is also narrowing quickly. TSMC, the No. 1 foundry, has already begun securing production capacity for its 1.4nm-class A14 process and plans to move to volume production in 2028 after trial production in 2027. A14 uses second-generation nanosheet transistors and the design optimization technology “NanoFlex Pro.” TSMC Chairman C.C. Wei said, “We are seeing more active customer engagement in A14 than in our 2nm process to date,” adding, “We expect A14 to be a larger process node than N2.” TSMC, which has already entered 2nm volume production, is simultaneously expanding existing advanced process capacity and developing next-generation processes.

Samsung Electronics has adjusted its 1.4nm volume production target to 2029 from 2027 as originally proposed. Instead, it will focus its capabilities on stabilizing yields for its 2nm process and on securing customers in artificial intelligence (AI) and high-performance computing (HPC) beyond mobile. Its 2nm follow-up process will be developed around DTCO to improve performance and power efficiency while leveraging existing infrastructure. For its most advanced 1.4nm process, it is in discussions with multiple customers and has entered the collaboration stage with some, including confirming test chip development.

This matter directly affects the medium- to long-term landscape of the foundry market. With Intel releasing PDK 0.9 for 14A based on actual silicon verification, fabless customers now have greater room to weigh Intel alongside TSMC and Samsung Electronics as a candidate for next-generation processes. However, with Intel targeting 2028 volume production and TSMC targeting A14 volume production in the same year, the actual competition for customer allocations is likely to hinge on whether PDK 1.0 and trial production schedules are met in the first half of 2027. For Samsung Electronics, the key question is whether improvements in 2nm yields lead to 1.4nm orders. Which company completes its design assets (IP) and design tool ecosystem first will also be a variable influencing customer choices, meaning all three players will compete not only on process performance but also on schedule predictability and the speed of their response to partners.
