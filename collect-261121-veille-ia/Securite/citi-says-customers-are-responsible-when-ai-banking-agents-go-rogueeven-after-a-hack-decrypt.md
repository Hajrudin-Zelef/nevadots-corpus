#### In brief

- Citi’s November agreement treats activity by third-party AI agents as customer-authorized, even if credentials are leaked or an agent ignores instructions.
- PNC updated its rules Wednesday, while Citizens warned customers about AI-agent activity; JPMorgan and Bank of America had already tightened policies.
- Federal rules still restrict when banks can hold consumers liable for genuinely unauthorized electronic transfers, regardless of contractual wording.

Citi has updated its online banking terms to make customers responsible for the actions of third-party AI agents accessing their accounts, including when an agent leaks their credentials and someone else uses them to get in.

The change, reported by TechCrunch Wednesday, makes explicit how the bank intends to handle an increasingly awkward question: Who pays when software handling your money makes a mistake?

Under Citi’s new terms, actions taken using credentials supplied to an outside agent are treated as authorized by the customer, even if the tool ignores instructions, exceeds its permissions, or shares those credentials with another party.

Citi says it has no obligation to investigate whether the agent followed the customer's original prompts, subject to applicable law.

A Citi spokesperson told *TechCrunch* that the November revision clarifies the risks of sharing banking credentials or granting outside tools account access. The provision does not apply to AI agents offered by Citi itself.

## Banks tighten the rules on AI access

Citi is not acting alone. JPMorgan and Bank of America announced similar policies in October, while Citizens Bank warned customers this week by email that they could be responsible for what their AI assistants do.

PNC also changed its digital agreement on Wednesday. Its rules distinguish bank-approved connections, which can provide limited access, from handing an agent unrestricted access to an account.

The move follows a banking security scare from October 22. Major banks had detected AI agents logging into customer accounts, sometimes taking steps that appeared to go beyond their users' instructions, and executives said they struggled to determine which agents had been properly authorized.

An AI agent differs from a conventional chatbot because it can act on a user's behalf, potentially navigating websites, accessing financial records, or initiating payments. That makes a mistaken instruction or compromised login more consequential than a bad answer about a bank balance.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency has since issued AI examiner guidance that addresses outside agents, payment controls, and human approval. The framework is nonbinding and does not itself create new requirements for banks.

## A banking agreement cannot erase federal protections

Citi's language does not automatically eliminate consumer fraud protections. Under U.S. federal consumer protection rules, banks generally must investigate unauthorized electronic transfers and limit customers' financial liability for fraud. Those protections cannot simply be waived through a banking agreement, although their application to transactions performed by customer-authorized AI agents remains less clear.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says banks cannot use agreements to waive those legal protections, although the rules distinguish theft by an outsider from transfers actually authorized by a customer or someone given access.

Whether a particular AI-related loss qualifies as unauthorized depends on how the transaction occurred.

PNC's updated agreement takes a stricter operational approach: Customers may use certain connection methods approved by the bank, but cannot give third-party AI agents their passwords or one-time security codes, and agents generally cannot initiate payments unless PNC expressly permits it.
