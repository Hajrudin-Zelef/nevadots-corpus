## What happened to Sora?

Sora 2 launched on 30 September 2025 alongside an invite-only Sora app in the US and Canada, with an Android app following in November 2025. It quickly became known for realistic motion, synchronised audio and its social feed of remixable clips, including “cameos” of users who had verified their likeness.

In January 2026, OpenAI removed Sora generation from free ChatGPT accounts. On 24 March 2026 it announced that Sora would be discontinued in both the app and the API. The consumer experience closed on 26 April 2026, and the Videos API and all Sora 2 models were removed on 24 September 2026. OpenAI did not name a direct replacement model. Publicly, it said it was focusing compute on its highest-value uses, and the Sora research team shifted towards world simulation research.

## How to export your old Sora videos

If you made videos with Sora, OpenAI provided an export option through a Sora sunset page, with exports delivered by email. OpenAI said it would permanently delete Sora data after the discontinuation and any final export window. If you have not exported yet, check OpenAI’s help centre immediately for the current status, and download anything you want to keep. Unused credits could be applied to other OpenAI products, and subscription refunds followed ChatGPT’s normal process.

## What Sora did well, and what to look for in a replacement

Understanding why people liked Sora helps you choose the right replacement. Sora 2 stood out for a few things:

- **Physical realism.** Objects moved with convincing weight, and scenes held together better than many earlier models.
- **Synchronised audio.** Dialogue, sound effects and ambience were generated with the picture.
- **Cameos.** Verified users could appear in videos, with controls over who could use their likeness.
- **Remix culture.** The social feed made it easy to build on other people’s clips.
- **ChatGPT integration.** Many people already had a ChatGPT subscription, which made trying Sora easy.

When evaluating alternatives, compare them on the same points: realism and physics, audio, likeness controls, consistency across shots, ease of use, and whether the tool fits into subscriptions you already pay for.

## 1. Google Flow with Veo and Gemini Omni

**Best for:** most former Sora users who want realistic video with sound.

Google’s Flow workspace, powered by the Veo models and the newer Gemini Omni models, is the most complete replacement for Sora today. Veo 3 and later generate synchronised sound, ambience and dialogue, which was one of Sora 2’s signature features. Gemini Omni, introduced at Google I/O in May 2026, adds conversational editing and the ability to combine text, images, audio and video as inputs. The August 2026 Omni 1.1 Flash update brought scene extension up to about 40 seconds, first and last keyframe control, cheap 360p drafts and 1080p or 4K final renders.

Flow also offers an avatar feature for your own likeness after verification, echoing Sora’s cameos, and all output carries SynthID watermarks. Developers can access Google’s video models through Google AI Studio and Google’s enterprise platform.

**Watch out for:** credit limits on lower plans and gradual feature rollouts by country. Full guide: how to use Google Flow and Veo.

## 2. Runway

**Best for:** creators and agencies who want fine control and editing in one place.

Runway has been a leader in AI video since before Sora, and its Gen-4 family of models focuses on consistency, references and controllability. Its References features help keep the same character, product or location across multiple shots, which is essential for ads and short films. Runway also offers a broad set of editing tools, such as removing objects, extending frames and changing styles, so you can do more without leaving the platform.

**Watch out for:** credits can go quickly on high-quality settings, and the interface has a learning curve.

## 3. Kling

**Best for:** longer clips and good value.

Kling, from Kuaishou, became popular for longer generations and strong motion at competitive prices. Newer versions improved realism and added audio. It is a good option for creators who need more seconds per generation or produce a high volume of clips.

**Watch out for:** availability, payment options and data policies vary by region; review them before using it for client work.

## 4. Seedance

**Best for:** cinematic clips where sound and mood matter.

Seedance is ByteDance’s video model family. Its newer versions are known for cinematic framing and audio-first generation, where sound and visuals are produced together. It is available through ByteDance’s own creative products and through API partners. Pricing per second has generally been higher than budget options, so it suits projects where quality matters more than volume.

**Watch out for:** access routes differ by country, and enterprise users should review data handling terms carefully.

## 5. Luma Dream Machine

**Best for:** fast ideation, mood boards and stylised shots.

Luma’s Dream Machine is quick and approachable, which makes it useful for exploring ideas before committing to a polished render elsewhere. Its image-to-video and camera controls are good for turning a still concept into motion. Many creators use it as a sketchpad alongside a more powerful final tool.

## 6. Pika

**Best for:** playful effects and quick social content.

Pika focuses on fun, shareable effects and fast edits, such as transforming objects or adding stylised motion to photos. It is less suited to realistic ads, but useful for social media experiments and trends.

## 7. HeyGen and avatar tools

**Best for:** presenter videos, training content and multilingual explainers.

If you used Sora’s cameos to put yourself or a presenter on screen, avatar platforms are a more controlled option. HeyGen lets you create a digital avatar from a short recording of yourself (with consent checks), generate talking-head videos from a script, and translate them into other languages with lip sync. ## Best Sora alternatives by user type

### For small businesses and local brands

Start with Google Flow if you already pay for a Google AI plan, because it covers realistic product and lifestyle shots with sound in one place. Add Canva or your phone editor for text, logos and offers. Use real footage for anything that must show exactly how your product looks and works. For local brands in India, festival content, food shots and store ambience are easy wins.

### For agencies and production teams

Runway’s references and editing tools make it easier to deliver consistent client work, and Flow is a strong second tool for realism and audio. Keep a shared prompt library and reference sheets per client, and be clear in contracts about which shots are AI generated.

### For educators and trainers

Avatar tools such as HeyGen are often more useful than general video models, because they produce clear presenter-led explainers quickly and can translate them into regional languages. Use general video models for illustrative B-roll.

### For developers

Google’s video models are available through its developer platforms, and Runway and Kling offer APIs. Check rate limits, pricing per second, content filters and data retention before building on any of them, and design your integration so you can switch providers if needed.

### For hobbyists

Use free credits on Luma, Pika, Kling and YouTube’s built-in tools to experiment before paying. Many hobbyists find that one paid plan plus free tiers elsewhere covers their needs.

## How to test alternatives fairly

One impressive demo clip tells you very little. Before committing to a plan, run a simple test:

1. **Pick five prompts** that reflect your real work, for example a product close-up, a person walking through a space, a food shot, a stylised animation and a shot with dialogue.
2. **Run each prompt three times** in each tool, because results vary between generations.
3. **Score each result** on realism, prompt accuracy, motion quality, consistency, audio and artefacts such as warped hands or text.
4. **Note the cost** in credits or money for each usable clip, not just each generation.
5. **Check the terms** for commercial use, data handling and watermarking.
6. **Decide by use case.** It is common to keep two tools: one for realistic work and one for quick or stylised content.

## A sample prompt to compare tools

Use one detailed prompt across several tools to see how each interprets it. For example:

“Handheld medium shot of a young woman in a yellow raincoat walking through a busy Mumbai street market in light rain, colourful umbrellas and fruit stalls around her, she stops to smile at a vendor handing her a mango. Natural daylight, slightly overcast, realistic documentary style. Sounds of rain, chatter and distant traffic.”

Look at how each tool handles the crowd, the rain, the hand-off of the mango, facial expression and the soundscape. Differences show up quickly in scenes with many moving parts like this one.

## Moving your Sora workflow to a new tool

If Sora was part of your regular content process, switching is easier with a little preparation:

- **Save your best prompts.** Good prompts transfer reasonably well between tools, though you will need to adjust wording. Keep a shared prompt library with notes on what each prompt was for.
- **Collect reference material.** Export your favourite Sora clips and stills to use as visual references in tools like Runway and Flow.
- **Rebuild characters carefully.** If you used recurring characters, create reference sheets with multiple angles so new tools can keep them consistent.
- **Update templates.** Adjust your editing templates for new aspect ratios, clip lengths and audio handling.
- **Retrain your team.** Each tool has its own controls. A one-hour session comparing the same prompt in two tools is a fast way to learn.

## Cost: what AI video really costs

Comparing headline subscription prices can be misleading, because tools measure usage differently: credits, seconds of video, resolution tiers or a mix. A tool that looks cheaper may need more attempts to get a usable result. The most useful number is your **cost per usable second**: total spend divided by the seconds of video you actually publish.

Some practical ways to keep costs down:

- Draft at low resolution or with “fast” model variants, and render finals only for selected shots.
- Plan shots with a storyboard so you generate with purpose rather than at random.
- Use real footage for shots that need precise product detail, and AI for atmosphere and transitions.
- Review your usage monthly and drop tools you rarely use; see API credits and AI subscriptions.

## Responsible AI video

Sora’s cameo system drew attention to a real issue: realistic AI video can be used to put people in situations they never agreed to. Whatever tool you choose:

- Only generate recognisable people with their clear consent.
- Label realistic AI content according to platform rules.
- Do not create fake testimonials, fake news footage or misleading product demonstrations.
- Respect copyright and trademarks, including characters and logos.
- Keep prompts and source files as a record of how content was made.

Sora’s closure is a reminder that AI products can disappear quickly, even from the largest companies. For individuals and businesses, the lesson is to avoid locking important workflows into a single tool. Keep your prompts, references and source files portable, export your work regularly, and be ready to switch.
